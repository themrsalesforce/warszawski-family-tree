Shimon and Bertha
The generation before Joe




Joe’s father was Shimon Warszawski and his mother Bertha, according to the family’s recent
account. Bertha’s parents are named as Mordechai and Elka Zelkind. The same account says Joe
was their only child. This corrects a tempting but unsupported inference that every older “Yossi” in
family conversation must be Joe’s brother.


Family recollection places Shimon in the Khust/Transylvania region, but the discussion sometimes
shifts between him and Larry Hersh. It is not yet safe to attach each place named in the exchange to
a specific grandfather. Bertha’s wartime route is still an open question: one descendant recalled
that she spoke about where she fled from, not where she went, how she returned to Poland or how
she met Shimon.


The absence of a complete story is not a failing of the family. It is part of the historical situation. A
final volume should find survivor records, a photograph or a direct interview before turning this
generation into a reconstructed journey.

Zion Memorial Park’s register
Interment dates, not death certificates




The cemetery register lists BERTHA Warszawski interred November 29, 2016, Z6 row 1 grave 58.
Her photographed headstone reads May 15, 1935 - November 28, 2016. The burial fits the next day,
but the family’s 26 Cheshvan yahrtzeit maps to November 27 daytime in 5777 (beginning after
sunset November 26). This discrepancy remains open; the Hebrew inscription and death record
may clarify it.


The register lists MORDUKH Zelkind interred September 25, 1996, and ELKA Zelkind March 16,
2005. Mordukh’s photographed stone carries his portrait and appears to give 1912-1996; exact birth
and death days are not visible. A photographed stone with Elka’s portrait appears to read ELKA
ZELKIND, 1914-2005, although the site indexes her as “Zelkino.” That year conflicts with an indexed
November 12, 1913 birth. A clearer image or original birth record is needed. GAIL Zelkind’s
photographed stone reads July 25, 1942 - February 28, 2013, while the cemetery lists her burial on
February 1, 2013. One record errs. A 1996 original death notice explicitly calls Gail Mordukh’s
daughter; whether Gail is the Gala named in the 1997 notice remains open.


Under Hersh, the register places ILEEN, interred May 24, 2009, beside LAWRENCE, interred
January 3, 2012. The placement and period notices strongly tie the pair to Lori’s parents and
support Gabby’s 2009 death year rather than the rough family estimate of 2008. A second Lawrence
row marked RESERVE is not another burial. An original May 23, 2009 death notice names Ileen as
Lawrence’s wife but gives a May 24 service date, not her day of death.

Bertha, called Grammy
A remembered voice and a surviving name




Bertha was “Grammy” to the next generation. The family remembers that Shimon called her Bella.
When Shimon and Mushky Warszawski later named their daughter Nechama, Bella became her
nickname; a later baby’s Baila name is also discussed in relation to the family’s remembrance.
These are family accounts of naming, not evidence of an official name change for Bertha.


One recollection tells of a friend Frieda meeting Bertha’s grandchildren and crying when she
realized whose family they were. Her reported words, “Bertha was my friend,” give a glimpse of a
relationship beyond the family roster. To print the scene as a direct quotation in a final book, the
family should confirm the recollection and its speaker.


Family reminders place Grammy’s yahrtzeit on 26 Cheshvan and birthday greetings on May 15. A
photographed headstone gives May 15, 1935 and November 28, 2016, with burial the next day. The
family’s 26 Cheshvan yahrtzeit conflicts with the stone’s civil death date and still needs resolution.

before publication.

Larry’s documented surname
From a family variant to Hersh




The family had supplied “Hirsch” for Larry and Lori’s maiden name. A Cleveland Jewish News
obituary for Larry’s sister Judith Dratler, née Herskowitz, identifies her predeceased brother as
Lawrence Hersh. This is a stronger documentary basis for Larry’s surname than the earlier
inference from Lori’s name. The family-used Hirsch form remains a variant to investigate; The
1986 engagement notice also prints Laurel Ruth as Miss Hersh, establishing that period spelling for
Lori; a 1961 naturalization card documents a name-change annotation, though the earlier legal
sequence still merits checking.


Judith died in 2025 at age ninety-six. The obituary says other parents and siblings perished in the
Holocaust. This supports the reported family account that Larry and Judy survived while other
relatives did not, but it does not give a complete sibling count or identify every victim.


A US Holocaust Memorial Museum catalog entry identifies a three-videocassette oral history
interview with Larry Hersh, recorded August 13, 1984, by Peggy Nathan (RG-50.091.0080). The
existence of his own account is a major source lead. A separate Fortunoff/Yale catalog synopsis of
an August 13, 1984 Larry H. interview has now been read; this edition has not viewed the video or
read a transcript, and does not attribute exact spoken words to him.




SOURCE FRAME | Cleveland Jewish News, Judith Dratler obituary, June 9, 2025; USHMM catalog RG-50.091.0080. Lori’s
surname documented in the 1986 notice; Larry’s 1961 card is separately cited below.

Ilene, called Gabby
One person, several names




Lori’s mother was Ilene, called Gabby by the family. A direct clarification gives her Hebrew name
as Shaina Yuda. Earlier working material had mistakenly risked treating Gabby as a separate
grandmother; she is the same person. Her husband Larry’s Hebrew name was given as Aryeh Leib.


The family remembrance places Gabby’s yahrtzeit on 28 Iyar and had estimated her death around
2008. A death index has an “ILEEN HERSH” entry for May 22, 2009, a date matching 28 Iyar 5769.
The cemetery register independently places Ileen’s burial on May 24, 2009 directly beside
Lawrence. That pairing strongly supports the identity and year 2009, and an original notice
confirms her identity and the year. It announces a May 24 service, but does not give her day of
death; the derivative index date needs a death record.


Gabby’s parents are called Victor and Pearl in family conversation. One account said they were
from France; an earlier correction says Victor was born there but Pearl was born in Latvia. Pearl’s
surname Gold is another reported lead, not yet joined to a documented civil record. The origin
conflict remains open.

A photograph from 1961
A possible European setting for Larry




Family discussion describes an old photograph as a summer camp in Rimini, Italy, in 1961. The
comment says the group came by train from Copenhagen and identifies Larry in the image with
Lazar and Garelik on either side. The photograph itself has not been inspected for this volume, so
neither visual identification nor original caption is verified.


If the family holds it, a scan of front and back could turn the recollection into a substantial spread:
who owned the photograph, who wrote on it, which individuals can be identified, and whether the
camp had a documented name. That is a better way to situate Larry in postwar Europe than
repeating an uncertain country of birth.


The catalog synopsis of a matching Fortunoff oral history offers a wartime and postwar
chronology, not a check on this particular 1961 photograph. Until the image is inspected, it remains
a promising lead rather than an illustration to print with a confident caption.

A birth in postwar Poland
October 19, 1959




Joe Warszawski was born October 19, 1959. The family account places his birth in Poland after the
war, but the city has not been established. A descendant recalled uncertainty about how Shimon
and Bertha returned to Poland after wartime displacement. This edition leaves that route as a
question.


A civil birth record would supply a city for Joe himself. His father’s Social Security index now gives
Warsaw as his own birthplace, but that says nothing by itself about where Joe was born. A Polish
record could also settle spelling variants and identify a district for other archival work. Until then,
“postwar Poland” is a context given by family memory, not a specific map coordinate.


Joe’s father Shimon and mother Bertha later built a family life that the next generation associates
with Cleveland. How and when they arrived there remains to be established from documents or
firsthand recollection.

Cleveland Heights
A school record and the limits of a record




A Cleveland Heights High School memorial lists Joseph Sam Warszawski with the class of 1978. It
helps place Joe in the Cleveland years and preserves a form of his full name. It cannot tell us what a
school day felt like to him, who his friends were, or which teacher he remembered.


Family accounts place Joe and Lori in Cleveland. The family’s later memories supply a different
sort of evidence from the school entry: Joe waking early for the market and his interest in
skydiving are reported in family conversation. Those hints deserve direct interviews before they
become full narrative scenes.


The birth, school and death records span decades; much of the person is necessarily between them.
The goal of this volume is to make that open space visible, then fill it with family-held material
rather than plausible invention.




SOURCE FRAME | Cleveland Heights class of 1978 memorial and family-knowledge recollections; personal anecdotes need
family confirmation.

The early market
What a household remembers




One daughter remembered that Joe woke very early to go to the market. The surviving family
conversation offers the line as a memory, not a fully dated account of his work. It is tempting to
write the sights and sounds of the market, but none have yet been described by a witness.


The concrete detail nevertheless matters. It names an early hour and a habit that his children could
recognize. A good next interview would ask which market, why he went, who went with him, and
whether any photograph or receipt survives. The answers could turn a single sentence into a
grounded chapter.


Another remark says he had a thing for skydiving. It is not yet clear whether he jumped, watched,
joked about it or hoped to try it. The family’s phrasing is preserved as a question for those who
knew him rather than transformed into a claim that he was a skydiver.




SOURCE FRAME | Family-knowledge memories recorded in 2022 and 2024; no independent corroboration or invented scene.

Purim in Cleveland
A remembered dance




Shoshy recalled people from Telshe yeshiva coming to dance with “Abba” on Purim. The
recollection does not fix the year, the room, or every person present, but it places Joe in a social and
religious scene that his children remember with warmth.


For a final printed scene, the family could identify the yeshiva visitors and select a photograph
from the same period. Without that, this book leaves the memory at the scale in which it was given:
a short account from his daughter, not a reconstructed celebration.


It is one of the places where this family history feels different from a pedigree. A son or daughter
can remember movement, sound and company where a record records only a name.




SOURCE FRAME | Shoshy’s family-knowledge recollection, February 2021; date of the original gathering unknown.

Lori, or Leah Rochel
The present and the remembered past




Lori is called Leah in parts of the family record, and a family naming conversation gives the fuller
Hebrew Leah Rochel. The current working tree had recorded her maiden surname as Hirsch,
inferred from family usage. Larry’s sister’s obituary, however, names him Lawrence Hersh. A
period engagement notice identifies Laurel Ruth as Miss Hersh. That period notice establishes
Hersh as her printed maiden spelling; Hirsch remains a family-used variant to reconcile.


Her parents Larry and Ilene/Gabby connect the household to a second survivor line. Lori’s
reminders of their yahrtzeits show how that line remained present in the next generation. They do
not by themselves prove civil death dates.


The family can give this chapter more depth through Lori’s own account. Where did she grow up
with Larry and Gabby? What did she learn about the family’s past from them, and what was not
spoken about? Her voice should lead those answers rather than an editorial guess.




SOURCE FRAME | Family naming recollection; Larry’s Hersh spelling documented in Judith Dratler obituary. Lori’s maiden
spelling Hersh also appears in the 1986 engagement notice.

WARSZAWSKI                                                                                                   JOE AND LORI




February 2017
Death, travel and remembrance




Joe died February 18, 2017. Family reminders give his yahrtzeit as 22 Shevat. A daughter
remembered the drive to Cleveland for his levaya and that her husband Shmuel turned twenty-five
around the same time, old enough to rent a car. The practical observation is part of how the
journey stayed in family memory.


The levaya and the anniversary of loss continued to bring relatives together. This volume does not
reproduce private shiva correspondence or imply that a family message was intended as a
published eulogy. A formal obituary or recorded eulogy would be valuable before a final printed
account.


His Hebrew name has not been verified. Descendants’ names may suggest a pattern, but a
namesake inference is not a substitute for an inscription or family confirmation.




SOURCE FRAME | Family-confirmed civil death date; family-knowledge travel and yahrtzeit memories. Full obituary not yet
verified.

The nine children
A roster held without birth order




Joe and Lori’s children are Ilana, Aliya, Shoshy, Yechiel, Shimon, Chaya Brocha, Hadassa, Simcha
and Maimi in the current family account. The list is not birth order. Simcha is a daughter; Yechiel is

Family conversation supplies occupations, moves, birthdays and celebrations for many of them. It
is not a mandate to publish all those details. A formal book should invite each adult to write two or
three lines in their own words and choose one image, while the tree retains the larger web of
relationships.


This book keeps the focus on situational moments that explain a connection or memory, not a
directory of current locations.

The family’s dates of remembrance
Yahrtzeit as a shared act




Family reminders give Joe’s yahrtzeit as 22 Shevat, Bertha’s as 26 Cheshvan, Larry’s as 8 Tevet and
Gabby’s as 28 Iyar. A memorial prints Larry’s civil death as January 3, 2012, which converts to 8
Tevet 5772 before sundown, a strong independent match to his family yahrtzeit. The actual time of
death was not checked; a date after sundown would be the next Hebrew day. Gabby’s 2009
cemetery interment now supports that death year; the exact day remains unsettled.


The reminders mattered because they asked living relatives to pause together. A yahrtzeit
gathering for Grammy involved candles and learning Mishnayos in the family account. Joe’s
yahrtzeit brought relatives to a small gathering. These shared acts connect people now even when
the precise wartime routes of grandparents remain hidden.


This book distinguishes the rhythm of remembrance from the archival question. Both are worthy
of record, but they answer different questions.

Judith Dratler’s obituary
A document that changes a name




Judith Dratler died June 5, 2025, aged ninety-six. The Cleveland Jewish News identifies her as née

Living descendants of Judith are named in the obituary, but they are collateral to this book’s
central account; their names and contact details need not be copied into the family volume.

Larry in his own words
A cataloged interview waiting to be read




The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum catalog lists an interview with Larry Hersh,
conducted by Peggy Nathan on August 13, 1984 (RG-50.091.0080, three U-Matic videocassettes). A
Fortunoff/Yale catalog synopsis of Larry H. (HVT-341) has the same interviewer and date and
supplies a detailed chronology. Its public summary has been read; the underlying two-hour,
thirty-one-minute video and any transcript have not.


This is a source of a different order from a later family summary. The synopsis locates several
places, people and transitions that the family remembers only in fragments. It is a curator’s
summary, not a verbatim interview. Until the video or transcript is read, it would be wrong to
write a scene in Larry’s voice or present catalog language as his exact words.


Once the transcript is accessible, compare his own dates and name spellings to civil records and
family recollections. Then select short quotations with an exact tape or transcript citation and an
honest note about what is not covered.

Joseph at Sinai Synagogue
A bar mitzvah notice in 1972




The October 13, 1972 Cleveland Jewish News lists Joseph as the son of Mr. and Mrs. Szyman
Warszawski and announces his bar mitzvah for the following morning at Sinai Synagogue. The
notice thus gives Saturday, October 14 as the planned ceremony. It independently anchors the
printed Szyman spelling and father-son relationship thirteen years before Joe’s engagement notice.


The notice gives a period street address, which is omitted here: a historical residence need not
become a locator in a family book. It does not establish the exact hour of the ceremony, a Hebrew
birthday or an adult religious practice. The value is a dated childhood milestone and a parent link
in the original neighborhood press.


Joe’s birth is given elsewhere as October 19, 1959. A bar mitzvah five days before the civil
thirteenth birthday raises a Hebrew-date question rather than proving the birthday wrong; Jewish
coming-of-age follows the Hebrew calendar. An invitation or family memory could explain the
schedule.

Larry in Cleveland, 1972
A public activist role in the period press




On the same issue’s second page, an article about a Soviet emigration tax identifies Larry Hersh as
the Cleveland leader and national board member of the Jewish Survival Legion. It calls him a
former local Jewish Defense League coordinator. The article quotes him saying that a small local
group had been formed and presents the Legion’s demand about the tax.


This is a specific public and political role in 1972, not evidence of a profession or a complete
account of his views. It adds context to the 2008 Altalena interview’s account of activism after the
war, but its organizational description belongs to the newspaper’s reporting, not to an inferred
family biography.


The article can prompt a family interview: what did Lori and her sisters know of that public work,
and how did it fit with the father they remember at home? The archival clip supplies the date and
role; family testimony would have to supply domestic context.

Khust to liberation
The Fortunoff/Yale synopsis of Larry H.’s 1984 testimony




The Fortunoff catalog summarizes a two-hour, thirty-one-minute August 13, 1984 interview with
“Larry H.” by Peggy Nathan. The date, interviewer, Khust origin and story align with the separately
cataloged Larry Hersh interview at the Holocaust Memorial Museum. The synopsis describes Larry
as born in Khust, Czechoslovakia, the oldest of five children. A family account remembers seven
siblings; these counts conflict and should be tested against the actual testimony and family records,
not quietly averaged.


Its chronology begins with Czech and Jewish schooling, Zionist activity, and the Hungarian
occupation in 1938. After restrictions and the loss of the family businesses, the synopsis places him
in Budapest in 1942 with a ticket to Palestine that he gave to someone else at his mother’s urging.
His father obtained papers for service in a Hungarian slave-labor battalion. The summary then
follows Larry to Baia Mare, an escape, deportation to Auschwitz, and a voluntary transfer toward
Warsaw after learning that his father and uncle had been sent there.


The catalog records a reunion with father and uncle, a death march to Kutno, transport to Dachau
and Landsberg, and liberation from a train by United States troops. His father and uncle had been
taken away after falling ill. The public synopsis cannot stand in for his voice, and it does not supply
the private testimony’s exact wording. That boundary matters most in writing about separation
and loss.
