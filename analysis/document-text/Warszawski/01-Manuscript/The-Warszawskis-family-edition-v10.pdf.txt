BEFORE THE STORY

A note on the text
This book gathers everything the family knows about the Warszawski line - what the records show, what the
stones say, and what the family remembers - told as one continuous story.

Names appear as the family uses them: Szyman is Shimon; Gabby is Ileen; Zeidy Larry is Aryeh Leib, and
on his naturalization card, Arie Lawrence Hersh. Where a record and a memory differ, both are given in the
telling. What no record or memory supplies is left blank rather than guessed.

The photographs and documents come from the family's own archive: headstones at Zion Memorial Park in
Bedford Heights, the cemetery's register, Cleveland's Jewish press, and the public naturalization files of
Cuyahoga County. The original records stay in the family archive; what is printed here is for the family
table.

This is a living book. Corrections, memories and photographs are always welcome - the next edition will be
richer for them.

The old generations
Survivor families, the names Hersh, Warszawski, Zelkind and Garson, and the record
of lives interrupted by war and remade in Cleveland. The documents are thin where the
world was cruel; what survives is printed here whole.
CHAPTER ONE

The tailor of Cedar-Center
Szyman Warszawski, 1918 - 1995

Szyman Warszawski - Shimon to his family - was born in Poland on April 1, 1918; a Social Security index gives the
city as Warsaw. He lived in Russia through the Second World War, and came home to a Poland that was no longer the
world he had left. In 1962 he immigrated to Cleveland with his wife Berta, and there he built the life his grandchildren
would remember: for twenty-five years he owned the Mayflower Tailoring Company at Cedar-Center, a member of
Kol Israel, a working man of the old school. He retired around 1988, seven years before his death.

He and Berta were married forty years. Their son Joseph - Joe - was their only child, born after the war; five
grandchildren followed. A brother, Benyamin, made his life in Israel.

The records disagree about one thing, and the disagreement is worth preserving. His headstone at Zion Memorial Park
gives his Hebrew name as Shimon ben Zisl - his father named Zisl. A Social Security index entry names his parents as
Joseph Warszavski and Rochel Goldberg. Both are printed here, unsettled, exactly as they stand; perhaps Zisl-Joseph
was one man with two names, as families often carried. What is certain is the life: Warsaw, the war years in Russia,
the return, and then thirty-three years in Cleveland.

Szyman died on July 18, 1995, and was buried the next day at Zion Memorial Park, plot 6-6-111 - directly beside
Mordukh Zelkind, his father-in-law, at 6-6-110. The two families had come through the century together, and in the
cemetery of a city an ocean from Warsaw, they lie side by side.

CHAPTER TWO

Berta, called Grammy
Bila bat Mordechai, 1935 - 2016

Berta Zelkind was born on May 15, 1935, a daughter of Mordechai and Elka Zelkind, and grew up in a world that was
coming apart. She spoke in later years of where she fled from - not of where she went, how she returned, or how she
met Szyman.

What survives is the warmth. To the next generation she was Grammy. Szyman called her Bella - the name traveling
down the family, as names do. A friend named Frieda, meeting Berta's grandchildren long after, cried when she
realized whose family they were. "Bertha was my friend," she said. It is a small scene, and it says more about her than
a date can.

Her headstone reads: Bila bat Mordechai - her father Mordechai's name, carried in stone - May 15, 1935 to November
28, 2016, niftera 27 Cheshvan 5777, "Beloved Mother, Grandmother, Great Grandmother, Sister." She was interred
the next day, November 29, in section Z6, row 1, grave 58. The family keeps her yahrtzeit on 26 Cheshvan; the stone's
Hebrew date reads the 27th. Both are kept here, side by side, as the family keeps them.

CHAPTER THREE

The Zelkinds of Babruysk
Mordechai and Elka, and four daughters

Mordechai Zelkind - Mordukh - was born in 1912; Elka Kogen, his wife, was born November 12, 1913, in Babruysk,
on the Berezina in what is now Belarus. A Social Security index names her parents as Isac Kaga and Tsilya Gershon.
How Mordukh and Elka survived the war, and when they came to America, the record does not say.

What it tells us in full is the family they raised. Mordukh's death notice in the Plain Dealer of September 25, 1996
reads: "MORDUKH ZELKIND, beloved husband of Elka (nee Kogen), devoted father of Bertha Warshawsky, Gail
Zelkind, Maria Komisarchik (Isaak) and Celya Elin (Matvey), loving grandfather of Joseph Warshawsky, Dimitry and
Kira Komisarchik, Inna and Boris Elin, great grandfather of seven... Interment Zion Memorial Park."

Every name in that notice is a thread. Bertha Warshawsky is Berta - Grammy - Joe's mother; the Warshawsky spelling
is how the paper heard the name. Gail, Maria and Celya are Berta's sisters. And "grandson Joseph Warshawsky" is Joe
himself, named in his grandfather's notice twenty-one years before his own stone would stand in the same cemetery.

Mordukh was interred on September 25, 1996, in section Z6, row 6, grave 110 - beside Szyman, his son-in-law, at
6-6-111. Elka followed him on March 14, 2005, and was interred March 16, in Z6, row 4. Her stone reads 1914 -
2005; the Social Security index gives her birth as November 12, 1913. The year stands as the stone has it, with the
index noted alongside - a single year's disagreement in a life of ninety-one.

Gail Zelkind, Berta's sister, was born July 25, 1942 and died February 28, 2013. Her stone carries her portrait, a young
woman smiling out of black granite - the only face of her the record holds.

Mordukh's own notice, in the Plain Dealer of September 25, 1996, keeps the household whole: "MORDUKH
ZELKIND, beloved husband of Elka (nee Kogen), devoted father of Bertha Warshawsky, Gail Zelkind, Maria
Komisarchik (Isaak) and Celya Elin (Matvey), loving grandfather of Joseph Warshawsky..." - Joe Warszawski, named
in his grandfather's notice, twenty years before his own stone. And the cemetery keeps the two families as close as the
notice does: the burial registry places Mordukh at plot 6-6-110, directly beside Szyman Warszawski at 6-6-111 -
father-in-law and son-in-law, side by side at Zion.

The Kogen question
Elka's maiden name, and a rabbi of Cleveland

Elka Zelkind was born a Kogen - her husband's 1996 death notice prints it plainly: "Elka (nee Kogen)." Her Social
Security index entry names her parents as Isac Kaga and Tsilya Gershon, of Babruysk. Beyond that, the Kogen line
has not yet been documented - but one Cleveland name keeps surfacing, and it is printed here as a candidate, nothing
more.

Rabbi Meyer Kogen appears in Cleveland's communal record exactly where a relative of Elka's family might: as a
guest, with his wife, at the Jewish Community Federation's board meeting of March 15, 1954, and the next year as an
instructor in the Cleveland Institute of Jewish Studies' department for the training of Hebrew teachers - Humash with
Rashi, Mishnah, Aggadah, Hebrew literature, Jewish history. His stone stands at Mount Olive Cemetery in Solon:
"Rabbi Meyer Kogen, 1888-1973," the Hebrew reading Meir Tzvi ben Avraham. He died on December 8, 1973.

Name, title, city and era all fit. What no document yet supplies is the link itself: nothing in the file ties this rabbi to the
Kogens of Babruysk, or to Elka. The candidate is kept on the table because the question is worth asking plainly -
whether the rabbi who taught Cleveland's Hebrew teachers was kin to the grandmother who came from Babruysk.

The rabbi's own record has since filled in. His death notice in the Plain Dealer of December 9, 1973 names him the
widower of Esther, nee Reiter; the father of Rabbi David C. Kogen of New York, of Mrs. Naomi Spiegelman, wife of
Rabbi Marvin Spiegelman, and of the late Leo; the grandfather of nine; the brother of Jacob Eshel of Miami and of
Yetta Pietrokowski and Tzipora Kroitblat of Tel Aviv - with interment at Mount Olive, exactly where his stone stands.
Esther's own notice, from the Cleveland Press of April 1960, survives only in a corrupted machine transcription, and
its names are held unread until the original scan arrives. None of this closes the question: a full household for the
rabbi, and still no document that ties him to Elka. The candidate stays on the table, as a candidate.

CHAPTER FIVE

Zeidy Larry
Arie Lawrence Hersh, 1925 - 2012

Larry Hersh was born Aryeh Leib on June 1, 1925, in Czechoslovakia - in the family's memory, the Khust region of
Subcarpathia, where the Hershkovitz families had lived for generations under names the border kept changing. He
survived Auschwitz. Of seven siblings, he and his sister Judy came through; the others did not. Judy - Judith Dratler,
nee Herskowitz - lived to ninety-six and died in Cleveland on June 5, 2025. Her obituary gives her whole: wife of the
late Phillip Dratler, mother of Leigh Wasserman and of Jay, grandmother and great-grandmother, laid to rest at Bet
Olam Cemetery in Beachwood after a private funeral - and it remembers her alongside her parents and siblings who
perished in the Holocaust, an obituary's plain confirmation of what the family always said about Khust, and of the
Herskowitz spelling her line carried. His own 1984 testimony remembers the family a little differently - "I had four
sisters. And we were five kids. I was the only boy" - five children against the family's seven; both are kept, his
testimony first. The next chapter gives his war in his own words.

After the war his road ran through Israel: when he took his American citizenship he was recorded as a subject of
Israel, and the index trail suggests he reached the United States around 1954. What Israel held for him - where he
lived, whom he knew - the record has not yet yielded.

On August 11, 1961, in the U.S. District Court at Cleveland, he became an American, and the court's card settled the
family name in his own hand: "Name changed as part of nat. from: ARIE - LARRY - HERSH KOVITZ" - signed Arie
Lawrence Hersh. Hershkovitz, the name of the old country, became Hersh on the card that made him a citizen.

He did not spend his American years quietly. In October 1972, Cleveland's Jewish press named him a leader of the
city's Jewish Survival Legion and a national board member - formerly the local coordinator of the Jewish Defense
League: a survivor of Auschwitz, twenty-seven years on, standing publicly for Jewish defense in his new city. The
family remembers the same man at the kitchen table - Zeidy Larry, Aryeh Leib. Both portraits are true.

His own account of the war years exists. On August 13, 1984 he sat for a Holocaust oral history - three videocassettes,
now in the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's collection.

Larry died on January 3, 2012 and was interred the same day at Zion Memorial Park, plot Z3-1-35 - directly beside
Ileen, at Z3-1-34, as they had lain side by side for three years already, and now for good.

CHAPTER SIX

Zeidy Larry's war
In his own words - the 1984 oral history

On August 13, 1984, Larry Hersh sat for the Fortunoff collection at the National Council of Jewish Women in
Cleveland and told his war whole, across three videocassettes. The transcript is machine-made, so his words are
quoted here in short, careful pieces - but the story is his.

He was born in Khust, Czechoslovakia - he spells it C-H-U-S-T - a town of thirty-five or forty thousand, eight or ten
thousand of them Jews, in June 1925. His was, he says, an ideal childhood: an affluent home - his father dealt in
lumber and wine - cheder, then Czech elementary school, then the Hungarian gymnasium, until the Jewish students
were put out; summers in the Carpathians; Betar, where he rose to lead the region's youth. He read Gone with the
Wind, he liked to say, in four languages. In 1938 he won a fully paid place at a Prague school for talented youngsters -
and his paternal grandmother went to the rabbi: he'll eat treyf, there'll be no Shabbos, no tefillin. His father canceled
the ticket.

In late 1942 the Zionist organization chose him for one of Khust's four Palestine certificates. His mother objected - it
would look like the big macher was taking for himself - and his younger sister refused it too: she wanted the family
together. He gave it away. "The paradox of it," he says, "is the ones who left, they all survived."

Then the ghetto. He organized the food wagons on the move in; the Judenrat man called him by his Jewish name,
Leibe; the Gestapo chief interrogated him personally. Warned that his arrest was coming, his father bought him a way
out - a genuine Hungarian army call-up signed by Colonel Kelety, later recognized as a Righteous Gentile. In the labor
service at Nagybanya he organized a hard core of eight men and smuggled a wagon of bread, hidden under hay, to the
starving ghetto; the general caught him and looked the other way - "next time don't be a dumb Jew and let yourself be
caught" - and later praised the job well done. His friend Leonard Fox, who lived to see Cleveland, took forty-five
lashes on a later run. Larry escaped over the fields, came back to the ghetto at night, and was deported with its
liquidation in the morning.

Five days in a freight car, eighty to a hundred to a car, water and nothing else, and then Auschwitz, two days before
Shavuot - the calendar says May 26, 1944; in his telling he once said 1943, and the book keeps both. Mengele
conducted the selection; Larry said he was seventeen, and was sent right. His prisoner number was 8485. Learning that
his father and an uncle had been sent to Warsaw, he swapped blocks to follow them - to the camp built on the ruins of
the ghetto, where prisoners cleaned bricks from bombed buildings and loaded trains for the Russian front. He found
his father after six weeks. He translated for a guard the words written in blood on the Anielewicz bunker wall: we
were betrayed, remember us. Assigned to clean the killer-dog cages, he fed his father, his uncle and his friends for
weeks on the kitchen food the work earned. He saw the SS burn bound prisoners stacked, he said, like wood - "the
horror of being stripped of the ability to even scream at one's own pain."

CHAPTER SIX - CONTINUED

Zeidy Larry's war - II
From the Warsaw camp to Cleveland

In August 1944 the camp dissolved ahead of the Russian advance. Some ten thousand were marched out - two days
standing without food or water, then the salt-herring ration, and prisoners who stooped to drink had their necks
crushed. Fewer than eight thousand reached Kutno; about two thousand survived the cattle train to Dachau. His father
and uncle made it. From Dachau to Landsberg, Lager 7 - underground earth huts, typhus quarantine - and then
Kaufering, Lager 4, night construction for the underground airfields. There his father, forty-eight and broken, was
taken away with the older generation, and was never seen again.

In the early spring of 1945 Larry lay twenty-eight days with typhus, unfed, untreated - an SS guard held a rifle to his
head, and a second guard said leave him, he's finished anyhow; save the bullet. In May the evacuation train was
strafed - the US Air Force disabled the locomotive - and he and two others, the last three of his eight-man hard core,
escaped through the forest to American-occupied Schwabhausen. He weighed thirty-eight kilos.

The after-war had its own roads. In the hospital, a Jewish US Army doctor traced his family by telephone from
Cleveland - the message ran that Leibi Hersch, son of the sister Leah, was alive: so his mother's name is known. In
Prague he learned that his next-to-youngest sister had survived, liberated at Bergen-Belsen. In Budapest he was
recruited by Joel Brand - Larry had seen Eichmann there in 1943 without knowing who he was - and he ran the Bricha
borders from 1945 to 1947, sailed for Palestine, was hunted by the British, was smuggled out to Cyprus hidden among
the oranges, and returned to Israel in 1948 on the Altalena. Eight years in the Israeli army carried him through the
1956 war.

In 1956 he came to Cleveland, to that sister - she had been there since 1947, taken in by their uncle Janowitz "like one
of their own daughters." He met his wife in Cleveland, and the rest of his story is this book's: Ileen, three daughters,
the Marmarosher temple on Lancashire - one record says B'nai Jeshurun, and both are kept - and the grandchildren
who called him Zeidy Larry.

CHAPTER SEVEN

Ileen, called Gabby
Shaina Yuda, 1933 - 2009

Ileen Garson was born in Cleveland on July 30, 1933, the youngest of Victor and Pearl Garson's three daughters, and
grew up on the east side in the years when the streetcars still ran. Her Hebrew name was Shaina Yuda; to her
grandchildren she was Gabby. She married Larry Hersh - Zeidy Larry - and together they raised their family in
Cleveland.

Her obituary in the Plain Dealer of May 23, 2009 keeps the whole constellation in one paragraph: "beloved wife of
Lawrence; devoted mother of Suzanne Greenberg, Seth, Laurel Warszawski, Joseph and Marci Hersh; cherished
grandmother of Ilana, Aliya, Shoshana, Yechiel, Moriah, Shymon, Chaya, Simcha and Hadassah Warszawski, Koby
and Dylan Greenberg; dear sister of the following deceased: Rochelle Horwich and Rosamond Simon."

Laurel Warszawski is Lori - Joe's wife. The nine Warszawski grandchildren named in that notice are Joe and Lori's
children, Gabby's grandchildren, each one counted. Her sisters, Rochelle and Rosamond, had gone before her.

Gabby died on May 22, 2009 - 28 Iyar 5769, the date the family keeps as her yahrtzeit - and was interred on May 24 at
Zion Memorial Park, plot Z3-1-34. When Larry followed in 2012, the cemetery's register placed him in the next grave
over. The two plots, Z3-1-34 and Z3-1-35, are the whole story of a marriage, told in the cemetery's own shorthand.

CHAPTER EIGHT

The Garsons of Paris
Victor Garson, 1895 - 1978

Victor Garson was born in Paris on May 30, 1895, the son of Solomon and Rachael Garson. In 1902 - so his wife
would swear decades later - he entered the United States at New York, a boy of seven; the family had sailed from Le
Havre, and almost certainly Solomon and Rachael brought him over themselves.

He grew up in Cleveland, became a bookkeeper, and on September 12, 1916 married Pearl Adelstein in Cuyahoga
County. Three years later, on September 18, 1919, he stood in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio
and swore his Declaration of Intention - the first formal step toward citizenship. The clerk wrote him down: born
Paris, May 30, 1895; occupation bookkeeper; last foreign residence "Pairs, France," the clerk's own misspelling of
Paris; emigrated from Le Havre, the vessel unnamed. He renounced his allegiance to the French Republic. He was
twenty-four, three years married, a father of four-month-old Rochelle.

The certificate itself took fifteen more years: Victor Garson was naturalized on November 20, 1934. He lived the rest
of his life in Cleveland, and died in East Cleveland on August 13, 1978, five years after Pearl. They lie at Zion
Memorial Park. His notice, in the Plain Dealer of August 14, 1978, styles him simply husband of the late Pearl; the
funeral was held that Monday at three o'clock at the Berkowitz-Kumin Memorial Chapel, and the family sat shiva
again at the Sanford Simons'. The notice's one misprint is preserved as printed: it calls him dear grandmother of eight
and great grandmother of one.

One page of his story was nearly misread. An early transcription of the declaration gave his last foreign residence as
Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine - and for a while the search ran to Metz. A magnified reading of the original settled it: the
word is "Pairs," Paris, where he was born. The correction is printed here as it belongs in a family record - plainly, and
in the same ink as the mistake.

CHAPTER NINE

An American twice over
Pearl Adelstein Garson, 1897 - 1973

Pearl Adelstein was born in Cleveland on December 1, 1897 - American from her first breath, in the city where she
would live her whole life. Family memory had placed her birth in France, or in Latvia; her own hand settles it. On
January 25, 1940, swearing her Petition for Naturalization, she wrote: "born in Cleveland, Ohio on Dec. 1, 1897. My
race is Hebrew."

That an American-born woman should have to petition for citizenship at all is the story. When Pearl married Victor
Garson on September 12, 1916, he was a French national - and under the Expatriation Act of 1907, an American
woman's citizenship followed her husband's. By saying yes to Victor, Pearl lost the country of her birth. The Cable
Act of 1922 opened the way back, and in 1940 she took it, swearing "I have not acquired any other nationality by
affirmative act." Her petition was granted; certificate 73676. Cleveland-born, made foreign by marriage, American
again by her own signature.

The petition is a family document in the fullest sense. It lists her three daughters with their exact dates, all born in
Cleveland: Rochelle, May 9, 1919; Rosamond, January 6, 1926; Ileen, July 30, 1933. Ileen is Gabby. On one sworn
page, Pearl gives us her birth, her marriage, her husband's crossing and naturalization, and the birthdays of all three
girls.

Pearl died in Cleveland Heights on March 3, 1973. Her obituary names her daughters - Rosamond Simon, Ileen Hersh
and Rochelle Horwich - and through Ileen the line runs on: to Lori, to Joe's house in Cleveland, and to the nine
grandchildren named in Gabby's own notice thirty-six years later. Her funeral was held at the Berkowitz-Kumin
Memorial Chapel on Sunday, March 4, 1973, at two o'clock, with interment at Zion Memorial Park, and the notice
asked, Please omit flowers. The family sat shiva at the home of Mr. and Mrs. Sanford Simon - Rosamond and her
husband, identified by the notice itself.

CHAPTER TEN

The Garson question
A marriage in Metz, 1865, and the missing link

Where did the Garsons come from before Paris? The family's oldest documentable footprint is Victor's - Paris, 1895.
Behind that, the trail thins to a surname and a region.

In Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine, the civil register records a marriage on September 1, 1865: Aron Garson to Caroline
Levy. In the Metz-Chambiere cemetery the same couple lies under a second spelling - "Gerson Aron, 1841-1924" and
"Levy Caroline Gerson, c.1845-1909." A rare surname, one region, two spellings - and in Cleveland, Victor's marriage
record names his father as Solomon.

If a Solomon Garson - or Gerson - was born to Aron and Caroline in Metz in the years after 1865, the line from Metz
to Paris to Cleveland would close into a single thread. That record has not yet been found. A "Guerchen, Abraham
Gershon" who died at Hellimer in 1855 may belong to an earlier generation still.

And there is one more archive to exhaust: a hundred and thirty-three manifests in the Ellis Island records carry the
name Garson between 1892 and 1924. Somewhere in that stack should be the Le Havre sailing of 1902 - Solomon,
Rachael, and a seven-year-old boy named Victor.

                                                 THE WARSZAWSKIS
FROM THE ARCHIVES DE PARIS

Julia's children
Three Garson children of Paris, 1895 - 1898

The registers of Paris hold three more Garsons, and they hold them close. On November 4, 1895 - five months after
Victor's birth across the same city - a young domestic named Julia Maria Garson, eighteen years old, bore a son,
Marcel Eugene Jules, at a midwife's home in the 18th arrondissement. No father is named on the act; the boy carried
his mother's name. Two weeks later, on November 18, Julia crossed the city to the 17th arrondissement and stood
before a second officer to recognize the boy as hers - act N° 3451, an acte de reconnaissance, a young mother making
sure the law knew her son was her son. That act gives her name in full: Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson.

In January 1897 the registers record a second son: Leon Jules, born on the seventeenth in the 5th arrondissement, to a
mother the clerk wrote down as Melina Perpetue Garson, twenty, a domestic - the same rare name, the same street, the
same woman. And in March 1898, back in the 18th, a daughter: Charlotte, born to Julia Garson, now twenty-two, at
another midwife's rooms. Again, no father is named. Three children in four years - Marcel, Leon, Charlotte - each
carried into the register by their mother alone.

The ages agree with each other. The surname is rare, and all three children came into the world at midwives' homes.
The acts are printed here as one woman's story - Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson, born about 1877 - and the family may
yet prove that she belongs to Solomon's generation of Garsons. If she was his sister, then Marcel, Leon and Charlotte
were Victor's first cousins, growing up in Paris while he crossed the ocean.

Leon's act carries the fullest afterlife. In its margin, two later hands recorded a whole French lifetime: married to
Marie Marthe Therese Rollet at Epineuil, in the Cher, on August 21, 1920; died at Lagny-sur-Marne on August 31,
1980 - eighty-three years, from a midwife's rooms in Paris to a marriage in the countryside and a long life past the
wars. The Rollet line may have descendants living today. Marcel's margin is briefer: a death recorded on February 21,
1963. What became of Charlotte, the registers have not yet said.

The four Garson households
One surname, four doors, 1893 - 1902

The full sweep of Paris - every birth table in all twenty arrondissements, 1893 to 1902, read down to the Gar- seam -
found the name in four households, and only four.

Julia's is the first: the unwed domestic whose three children fill the pages before this one. The second is Louise
Garson, a rag-picker three years Julia's senior, also unwed, who registered her son Gustave in the 5th arrondissement
in May 1901 - two women of the same rare name, alone in the same city, a few years and a few streets apart; whether
they were sisters is a question the registers have not yet answered. The third is a wine merchant's family: Charles
Alphonse Garson and Juliette Honorine Boclin, whose son Marcel Alphonse was born in the 4th in September 1902.
And the fourth a shoemaker's: Marius Gabriel Garson and Marie Guden, whose daughter Aline was born in the 6th in
October 1894 - and whose margin, written twenty-six years later, records her marriage to Edouard Adolphe Godenne
in 1920.

Four doors, one surname. Which of them opens onto Solomon and Rachael - onto Victor - the acts so far do not say;
none of them names him. But the sweep that was meant to find one boy's birth certificate found a small Paris of
Garsons instead, and every door is now on the map.

CHAPTER TWELVE

A postwar childhood
Joe Warszawski, born October 19, 1959

Joe Warszawski - Joseph Sam, Yosef Yitzchak ben Shimon - was born on October 19, 1959, in Poland, into the
after-silence of the war. The family remembers the country but not the city; the record has not yet supplied it. His
father had been born in Warsaw and lived through the war in Russia; his mother had fled from somewhere she would
only ever describe as the place she fled from. Three years after Joe was born, the family carried him to Cleveland, and
Poland became the old country.

He grew up on the east side. A notice in the Cleveland Jewish News of October 13, 1972 announced his bar mitzvah
for the next morning at Sinai Synagogue - a boy of the Heights, stepping up to the Torah the week he turned thirteen.
He went on to Cleveland Heights High School, class of 1978, where the school's memorial page today keeps his name
and his dates.

Of those years the family keeps the texture rather than the chronology: a household with one child at its center,
Szyman at the tailoring shop at Cedar-Center, Berta - Bella - at the heart of the home.

Joe and Lori
Cleveland, 1986

The Cleveland Jewish News of 1986 carried the announcement: the engagement of Laurel Ruth Hersh to Joseph

CHAPTER FOURTEEN

What a household remembers
The early market, Purim, and a thing for skydiving

A daughter remembers that Joe woke very early to go to the market. It is one line, and it holds a whole life: the father
up before the house, out the door in the dark, doing the day's work before the day began.

Another memory has music in it. On Purim, people from Telshe yeshiva would come to dance with Abba. The year is
not fixed and the room is not named, but the scene is unmistakable - the students spilling in, the dancing, the house
loud with it. It is the kind of detail a pedigree never holds, and a family never loses.

And one remark keeps its own mystery: he had a thing for skydiving. Whether he jumped, watched, or only talked
about jumping, the line is printed as it was given - a father's small daring, preserved by the people who loved him.

Joe Warszawski died on February 18, 2017 - 22 Shevat 5777. He was fifty-seven. The family drove in to Cleveland
and spent the week together in the house.

His stone at Zion Memorial Park names him in two languages, and names his father in Hebrew: Joseph Warszawski -
Yosef Yitzchak ben Shimon - October 19, 1959 to February 18, 2017, niftar 22 Shevat 5777, "Beloved Husband,
Father, Grandfather." The family keeps his yahrtzeit each 22 Shevat, and the grandchildren named for him carry the
rest.

AFTERWORD

This edition
September 2026, third cut

This book is the Warszawski story as the record stands in September 2026: from Warsaw and Babruysk, from Khust
and Auschwitz, from Metz and Paris to Cleveland - and on into the nine children and their children.

Everything in these pages is tied to a source - a document in the family archive, a photographed stone, a public
register, or a memory given by the family - and the sources are gathered at the back of the book. Where records
disagree, both readings stand in the text. What the record does not contain is simply not here.

This third cut adds the Cleveland Public Library's necrology citations, chased to the original notices: Pearl's funeral
and Victor's, the shiva at the Sanford Simons', Judy's obituary whole at ninety-six, and Rabbi Meyer Kogen's
household - still a candidate, now a documented one. The second cut adds Zeidy Larry's own telling of his war, from
the 1984 Fortunoff oral history - Khust to Auschwitz, Warsaw, Dachau and Schwabhausen, and home by way of the
Bricha and the Altalena; the Kogen question, kept plainly as a question; Mordukh's notice and the side-by-side plots at
Zion; and the notes-and-sources section that ties every fact to its record, person by person.

About this book
The Warszawskis was assembled for the family from the records above and the family's own archive, in
September 2026.

The research tree behind it - a hundred and thirty people, a hundred and three sources, every fact tied to its
evidence - lives in the family archive, and the public tree site carries the same pedigree online. The original
documents and photographs are preserved in the archive; the pages of this book are their telling.

Corrections, memories, photographs and documents are always welcome. This is the second family edition; the
