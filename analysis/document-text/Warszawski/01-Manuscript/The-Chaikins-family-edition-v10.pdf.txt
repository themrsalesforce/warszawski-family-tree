BEFORE THE STORY

A note on the text
This book gathers everything the family knows about the Chaikin line - what the records show, what the
stones say, and what the family remembers - told as one continuous story.

Names appear as the family uses them: Meir Chaim is recorded in one cemetery's books as Mayer; Koppel
Bacher signs himself Abraham Koppel Bacher; Sarah Chana carries her father's name, Kahanov, and her
family's. Where a record and a memory differ, both are given in the telling. What no record or memory
supplies is left blank rather than guessed.

The photographs and documents come from the family's own archive: the printed pages of Tiferes Azriel
and the Rebbe's letter in Igros Kodesh, the ordination of 1955, the Canadian Jewish Review obituary, the
DP camp registrations of the Arolsen Archives, and the portraits the family keeps. The original records stay
in the family archive; what is printed here is for the family table.

This is a living book. Corrections, memories and photographs are always welcome - the next edition will be
richer for them.

PART ONE




The rav of Snovsk
Meir Chaim Chaikin lived three lives - the yeshiva bochur of Snovsk and Nevel, the
underground rav of Soviet Georgia, and the dean of Montreal - and he lived all three
the same way: learning, ruling, and refusing to let go.
CHAPTER ONE

The star pupil of the Chofetz Chaim
Meir Chaim Chaikin, 1907 - 1930

Meir Chaim Chaikin was born on 13 Shevat 5667 - January 28, 1907 - in Naptonitsh, a hamlet near Snovsk in
Chernigov province, the son of Ezriel ben Aryeh Leib Chaikin, a wheat merchant, and Sarah, a daughter of Reb Zeev
Wolf Raskin of Homel. A printed directory of 1965 would later give his birthday as October 15; the family's sefer
gives 13 Shevat, and the sefer is followed here.

The Chaikins of Snovsk were kin to Reb Yoel Chaikin of Padobranka and to Reb Zalman Chaikin, whose daughter
married into the Schneersons - the family of the Rebbe. The Revolution took the wheat business and sent Ezriel to
forced labor; he died on 3 Kislev 5684, when Meir Chaim was sixteen.

The boy had already been noticed. He learned in Snovsk, in the yeshiva that the Chofetz Chaim's displaced students
carried with them, and the Chofetz Chaim himself tested the boy and blessed him. From there to Tomchei Tmimim in
Nevel, and ordination at twenty-one - among his signatories, Reb Refael Cohen of Germanovitch. In 1927 he stood in
yechidus with the Rebbe Rayatz in Leningrad. In 1929, still single, he was already the rav of Surazh, in the Vitebsk
province.

The Yevsektsia noticed him too. There was a plot, and an escape across a frozen river - the kind of story the family
tells in one sentence because one sentence is all that survived. And there was a wedding: on 12 Tammuz 5690 - July 8,
1930 - in Kutaisi, Georgia, he married Sarah Chana Kahanov, whose father had been sent to the Caucasus by the
Rashab a generation before.

CHAPTER TWO

The name Chaikin
The deep lineage behind the Snovsk stock

The Chaikin name runs deeper and wider than Snovsk. A printed family tree of the name, kept on the movement's
encyclopedia - with its own caveat that it sketches a general picture rather than fixing facts - climbs from Reb Chaim
Chaikel through Reb Moshe of Kobrin, son-in-law of Chaim Chaikel and brother of Reb Aharon of Karlin, down
through Rav Yoel and Rav Tzvi Hirsh to a generation of seven sons, and it ends one step from the Rebbe's own house:
Zelda Rachel of this tree was the Rebbe's great-grandmother, and Reb Shneur Zalman Chaikin of Podobranka, of
whom the next chapter tells, was his great-grandfather, named for the Alter Rebbe himself.

The movement's memory knew this closeness without the paper: Meir Chaim's family in Snovsk were kin to Reb Yoel
Chaikin of Padobranka and to Reb Zalman Chaikin, whose daughter married into the Schneersons. Whether the
printed tree's root joins the Snovsk stock is this book's standing question - the encyclopedia's whole category of the
name holds no entry for an Ezriel - but the name Ezriel runs on both sides of the page, and the chapters that follow
give the elder tree its due.

                                                   THE CHAIKINS
CHAPTER THREE

Shneur Zalman of Podobranka
The Rebbe's great-grandfather - the story of the stove

Reb Shneur Zalman Chaikin of Podobranka was a chassid of the Tzemach Tzedek, the Maharash and the Rashab, and
a prize student of Reb Pesach of Mlastovka. The Rashab would ask after him of his son-in-law, Reb Baruch Shneur
Zalman - "how is my mechutan, Reb Zalman" - and Baruch Shneur told many stories in his father-in-law's name. His
brother, Rav Yoel Chaikin, was the rav of Podobranka and the childhood melamed of Reb Levi Yitzchak, the Rebbe's
father; his sister Esther married Reb Shimon Zilber. He died on 29 Teves.

One story carries him whole, preserved in Reshimat Devarim from the telling of Reb Shmuel Gronem Esterman by
way of Reb Yehuda Chitrik. A rich man of the town - worth some twenty-five thousand rubles - would come and sit
with the poor by the stove. When one of them wondered where a meal might be found, the rich man said, go to
Zalman Chaikin's house - his own house - and when they got there, he opened the cupboards himself and fed them.
The poor men, not knowing him, scolded the arrogant beggar who gave away another man's bread.

CHAPTER FOUR

The magnate of Kapitonovka
Rav Tzvi Hirsh Chaikin, c. 1784 - 1884

Rav Tzvi Hirsh Chaikin, son of Rav Yoel, was the rav of Kapitonovka and a chassidic magnate - a man who sent large
sums to the Maharash. The Maharash's letters to him open, in the manner of kings writing to friends: To my friend, the
wondrous rav. He lived to make aliyah at ninety-seven, in 5641, and died in the Holy Land.

His fame in the movement rests on one piece of devotion: he uncovered the fate of Reb Moshe Schneuri - the
Tzemach Tzedek's son, lost to the family in the old country's troubles - through his correspondence with the Maharash
and with Reb Levi Yitzchak. Where the record was silent, his letters pressed until it answered. From him descend the
tree's three branches: Moshe, of the Rabinowitz line; Shmaryahu, of the Schneerson line; and Yoel Meir, of whom the
next chapter tells.

                                                   THE CHAIKINS
CHAPTER FIVE

Yoel Meir's road to Jerusalem
The court, the estate, the aliyah - and the son of Romema

Reb Yoel Meir Chaikin, born in Mogilev, was a yoshev - a settled elder - at the court of the Tzemach Tzedek. He
married Chaya Esther, a daughter of Reb Tzadok HaLevi; her name also comes down as Halevi Zadikov, and both
readings are kept. In 5620 he held an estate between Horodishche and Krementchug, farming and a winery. Around
5646 the landlord went bankrupt and the pogrom whisper reached the estate, and the family made aliyah. He farmed
near Jaffa, and at the end he was carried to the Chabad section of the Mount of Olives, dying on 5 Av 5658; Chaya
Esther followed on 21 Teves 5678. One printed entry would have him born as early as 1804-05; the family's reckoning
has placed him nearer 1840, and both stand here. Nine children survived him, among them a London branch -
Nachama, the wife of Moshe Blumenfeld, and a son, Hillel.

His son Reb Menachem Mendel Chaikin - born 1868 - had come up to the Land as a boy of thirteen, in 5641, with his
grandfather, the old magnate of Kapitonovka. In Hebron he learned under his kinsman Reb Shimon Menashe Chaikin
- born in Shklov in 1798, mechutan of Reb Eliyahu Yosef of Dribin, the man whose kever, the anash.org telling says,
would not open until a cavern appeared beside it. Menachem Mendel married Chana Zelda Zalznik in 5646, dealt in
wine, spent the years 1893 to 1898 in London, and came home to co-found and serve as rav of Romema. A letter from
the Rebbe Rayatz to him survives, warm with honorifics. He died on 21 Teves 5708 - 1948 - and his levaya went up
the Mount of Olives under Jordanian sniper fire: the last burial there until 1967. His matzeva was erected twenty-two
years later.

                                                   THE CHAIKINS
CHAPTER SIX

The attendant of the Tzemach Tzedek
R' Yisrael Chaikin of Nevel, 5601-5608

A hundred years before Meir Chaim took his semicha in Nevel, a Chaikin was already standing in the movement's
innermost room. The kuntres the Previous Rebbe published on the Tzemach Tzedek's battle with the Haskalah tells of
the Petersburg committee of 5601, and in its fifth chapter a young man steps out of the shadows: "the young R' Yisrael
Chaikin - a native of the city of Nevel - distinguished himself in the most clandestine work, with the deputy minister
of the interior, Andratov." The quiet channel to the deputy minister ran through him.

When the Tzemach Tzedek was held in Petersburg in the summer of 1843, the order over his rooms admitted no one -
"except his attendant Yisrael Chaikin." He was the sole visitor at the door. The great account of that summer's
commission - the twenty-two sittings in which the Rebbe defended the Torah before the ministry's scholars - rests, the
kuntres says, on the telling of the chassid R' Yisrael Chaikin, who attended his master through all the days of his stay
in Petersburg. When the Interior Minister later received the Rebbe with fitting honor, two intercessors walked in with
him: Feitelson and Chaikin. And by the fall of 1847 he sat on the Vaad Askanei HaKlal, the general committee of
communal affairs, the first to learn that a second rabbinical conference was being planned.

Whether this Chaikin of Nevel belongs to our own line, the record does not say; Snovsk and Nevel are two hundred
kilometers apart, and no document bridges them. But the same town holds the family again a century later - it is where
Meir Chaim studied at Tomchei Tmimim and took his semicha - and that makes the attendant's story feel less like a
stranger's than the paper can prove. It is printed here for what it is: the earliest documented Chaikin in the Chabad
record, standing at the hinge of the movement's hardest century.

CHAPTER SEVEN

The Georgia network
The Rayatz's staffing system, 5688-5690

The letters of the Previous Rebbe preserve, almost as office memoranda, how the Jews of Georgia were staffed
between the wars - and at the center of the system stood Rabbi Shmuel Levitin, whom the letters style the chief rabbi
of Georgia. In Marcheshvan 5688 the Rebbe sent a young tamim, Siman Yakabshvili, home through Kutaisi as
shochet-u-vodek - slaughterer and inspector - to strengthen the chadarim, reporting to the rabbis Slavin and Altshuler.
It is the exact template of the shlichus that brought Meir Chaim to Georgia in 1930. A letter of Rosh Chodesh Kislev
directs a candidate for the Tiflis rabbinate to "our friend Rabbi R' Shmuel Levitin, the chief rabbi of Georgia" - Levitin
was the address through which the network was run. By 5689 the same machinery was placing men in Baku, where
Meir Chaim would later be arrested; and a placement letter of 5690 remembers Zuber - the same Yaakov Yisroel
Zuber who would one day follow the family to Stockholm - for the years he spent in the cities of Georgia, where he
worked and accomplished much.

Read together, the letters describe a second wave of the underground: the tmimim the Rebbe planted across Georgia in
the late 5680s, who held the chadarim and the shechita through the terror years. That reading is the research archive's
hypothesis, and it is labeled as one - no single document says it in so many words. But the template letters are
documents, and Meir Chaim's own appointment to Tiflis came from the same hand and the same desk.

                                                     THE CHAIKINS
CHAPTER EIGHT

Nine years
Tiflis, arrest, and the camps

From 5697 he served as rav of Tiflis - his son Azriel remembers Sachkhere first, then Tbilisi - leading a community
that the NKVD wanted not to exist. The brissos were clandestine. The yeshivos were cellars. The arrests came for
everyone eventually, and one day they came for him.

How exactly, the record tells three ways, and all three are printed here as they stand. His own telling, decades later on
camera, says 1941, for rabbinic work. A 2019 retelling says he was arrested in Baku on the road toward Samarkand,
accused of smuggling Georgian tea. A third account, from 2020, says late 1941 or early 1942, on a charge of
speculation. The arrest file itself has not yet been found; when it surfaces, one of these tellings will become the story.

What the tellings agree on is the sentence: nine years. And they agree on what went in with him - tefillin, a shofar,
matzos smuggled past the guards - and on the bread, dry, that was all he would eat. They agree on the clemency of
Kalinin that cut nine years to three. And they agree on the last morning: his release fell on Shabbos, and he would not
sign the papers. He stayed two more days in the camp rather than sign, and walked out on Monday, a free man who
had not desecrated the day. After the war's end he served as rav of Gori, and waited for the way out.

                                                     THE CHAIKINS
CHAPTER NINE

The secret portrait
The Soviet years, told whole - from the 2019 interviews

In 2019, Rabbi Azriel Chaikin sat for a long series of interviews with Chabad.org's Dovid Margolin and told the
Soviet years whole. What follows is his telling, printed beside the images that survived it.

The Rayatz received the young Meir Chaim in Leningrad - an audience the son kept from his father's retelling all his
life. Then Surazh, where Meir Chaim served as rabbi and shochet, until the GPU came for him in the night and he
escaped by minutes. Decades later, in Israel, a man recognized the old rav: he had been the landlord's son, and he
confirmed that the GPU really had come that night - and found the bed empty.

Georgia, from 1930. In July 1930, in Kutaisi, Meir Chaim married Sarah Chana Kahanov; her father, Reb Eliyahu
Shmuel, had been sent to Georgia by the Rebbe Rashab himself, in 1918. Azriel was born the following year in
Zestafoni, where his grandfather was the shochet - so the 2019 telling; other tellings give Bandza, and both are kept. In
Sachkhere, where Meir Chaim succeeded Rabbi Yaakov Yisroel Zuber - the same Zuber whose post he would later
take again in Stockholm, which explains the Stockholm appointment - the mothers came home on 'maternity vacation'
to give birth and have their sons' brissim, because the towns they worked in could not be trusted with either. In Tbilisi,
around 1937, Azriel was hidden from the Soviet school in his grandfather's attic over the mikva. That was my
kingdom, he said.

The NKVD came at midnight, as it did to everyone. Their pitch to him survives in the son's verbatim: People already
think you're working for us - why don't you just work for us? His answer survives too: I have done nothing wrong.
Either you are bluffing, or it is all false.

They arrested him in Baku - smuggling Georgian tea, the charge read. The sentence was nine years; he served three
and was freed in the postwar amnesty - so the 2019 telling, while Tiferes Azriel records the sentence commuted by
Kalinin. Both are kept, as the record keeps them. In 1946 the family went out on forged Polish papers, through Lvov.

And the portrait. A rare photograph of the Rebbe Rayatz had crossed the Latvian border into the Soviet Union in the
mid-1930s, hidden, and lived in the family album through the searches and the camps. In 1946 it crossed out again
with them, to be displayed afterward in Europe and in Montreal - a picture that survived two borders and the whole
Soviet night. It is printed on the next page, and the passport its subject once signed follows it.

                                                         THE CHAIKINS
CHAPTER TEN

The exodus
Pesach 5706 - out of the Soviet Union

It came on Pesach 5706, in the spring of 1946, in the brief window when Poland took back its prewar citizens and the
Soviets looked the other way. With repatriation papers - Polish papers, obtained as such papers were obtained - Meir
Chaim, Sarah Chana, young Azriel and two daughters moved west through Lvov. His brother-in-law, Reb Chaim
Osher Kahanov, ran that route twice, going back in to bring others out.

Prague first, where a beis din of refugees answered the questions of the uprooted. Then Antwerp, and a shteibel on the
Oostenstraat. And then, in the summer of 1947, the tragedy this book tells in Sarah Chana's own chapter.

From Antwerp he was sent north: the Rebbe Rayatz instructed him to take the rabbinate of Stockholm. There, from
about 1947, he led Adas Yisroel - the congregation's own history lists Rav M. Chaikin among its rabbis of the 1940s -
and founded Tiferet Bachurim, whose government recognition stretched refugees' residence permits into something
livable. He performed brissos across Scandinavia and sat on the agunah cases of a continent full of missing husbands.
When he left for America, the Canadian Jewish Review would look back and call him, simply, Chief Rabbi of
Stockholm.

CHAPTER ELEVEN

The shechita battle he inherited
Stockholm, 5712 - the letter to Reb Zuber

The battlefield Meir Chaim inherited in Stockholm is on paper. His predecessor there was Reb Yaakov Yisroel Zuber -
the same Zuber whose post he had already taken once before, in Sachkhere - and in Adar of 5712, four years before
Meir Chaim arrived, the Rebbe wrote Zuber the letter that defined the fight: Igros Kodesh, volume 5, letter 1452.

The Stockholm government had moved to require the electric stunning of cattle before shechita. A leniency circulated,
backed by rabbis in the Holy Land and by a veterinary professor: stunning by nerve-shock, they argued, did not kill.
The Rebbe took the leniency apart. Death by nerve-shock leaves no post-mortem signs, he wrote - the professor's calm
laboratory proof proved nothing, because the death it inflicted was invisible to its own instruments. He doubted the
great posek whose endorsement traveled with the hetter had been given the accurate facts. And he closed with the
instruction that governed the rabbinate Meir Chaim was about to take up: stand by your issur.

Zuber did not stand alone in it. Beside him in the Swedish shechita struggle stood Reb Yisrael Zecharya Mendel
Chasdan - paying the government's daily fine rather than stun - the same Chasdan who had helped spirit the Rebbe
Rayatz out to Stockholm and received his thank-you letter, who with Zuber arranged some three thousand visas to
Japan, and whose family, a generation later, the Johannesburg record calls relatives of the Bachers. His uncle Reb
Zalman Chasdan, who raised him, had been in the Rayatz's orbit since the 1920s - the Rebbe's own Purim memoir of
5686 records a pidyon nefesh for him.

Stockholm had already held the Rayatz himself. On 25 Adar I 5700, pausing in the city on his escape route from Riga
to New York, he wrote from Stockholm a letter of welcome to a new student of Tomchei Tmimim - printed in Igros
HaRayatz, volume 14, letter 261. Eight years later the same city's rabbinate passed to Meir Chaim. The line from the
Rayatz's stopover to the family's Stockholm years is unbroken on the paper.

CHAPTER TWELVE

Fifteen years in Montreal
1951 - 1965

The Rebbe's consent came, and in 5711 the family crossed again. The sources disagree on the exact year of arrival -
the obituary says 1950, the Russian Jewish Encyclopedia says 1951, and one reckoning starts his tenure in the summer
of 1952; all three are kept. What is not in dispute is the fifteen years that followed.

He became the rav of Beis Yisrael V'Shmuel, a member of the Vaad Ha'ir's beis din, and Rosh Yeshivah of Tomchei
Tmimim - dean of the Rabbinical College of Canada for fifteen years, a title the Canadian Jewish Review would use in
his obituary without needing to explain it. The 1965 Who's Who in Canadian Jewry gives him a share of its page 120 -
the Caucasus pulpits, Stockholm, and Montreal from 1951, with his portrait - and that page is printed at the end of this
part. In December 1955 the yeshiva ordained a class at the Young Israel Auditorium, and the Chronicle's page prints
him first among the staff: Rabbi M. Chaikin, Rosh Yeshivah. A color film of that evening survives, eleven minutes
and change, and in it he is exactly where the page puts him.

Parkinson's disease came in his later years. He gave shiurim until his last day. He died at home in Montreal on
Thursday, December 9, 1965 - 15 Kislev 5726 - at fifty-eight; one website says 1966, and the family's documents and
the contemporary press say 1965. The funeral left from the Rabbinical College, Rabbi L. Kramer officiating, and went
on to 770, where the Rebbe took part. He lies in the Chabad society section of Old Montefiore Cemetery, recorded
there under the spelling Mayer Chaikin. His widow was Celia; his sons Ezriel, of Copenhagen, and Berl; his daughters

The Rebbe's letters to Meir Chaim
Igros Kodesh, 5708 - 5720

Five letters from the Rebbe to Meir Chaim survive in the printed Igros Kodesh, and together they trace the refugee
years.

The first three belong to 5708, the Antwerp year. Letter 310 of volume 2, dated 22 MarCheshvan, concerns the Kehot
printing and distribution network - the same work the wedding teshura's facsimile preserves in his own hand. Letter
335 is dated in the printed volume to Purim Katan; it matches another teshura page, though the facsimile's own date
line reads 10 Shevat, and both dates are kept until the original settles the difference. Letter 400, of 20 Elul, carries the
editor's note that an identical copy went to another address - the network again.

The fourth - volume 3, letter 464, 12 Adar 5709 - is the Sweden letter: the mission to Stockholm stated outright. And it
carries one sentence that rewrites a page of this family's tree. The Rebbe writes that the message is also for Meir
Chaim's father-in-law, identified by the editor as Reb Eliyahu Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov. So he was alive in early
1949, and with or near his daughter's family - presumably in Stockholm - having come out of Russia with the
Chaikin-Kahanov group. Where his story ran afterward, the record has not yet said.

The fifth - volume 19, letter 7271, 25 Nissan 5720 - is a bar mitzvah blessing for Berl. It confirms the year, 5720,
matching a birth in 5707 - and it settles the 1965 obituary's loose name: Berl was Meir Chaim's son, the future rav of
Cleveland, whose chapter comes later in this book.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN

Sarah Chana of the Kahanovs
1910 - 1947

Sarah Chana was born on November 10, 1910 - the date comes from the Antwerp burial register, which gives her
birthplace as Skolow, a name no one has yet resolved: Shklov, perhaps, or Skole. She was the daughter of Reb Eliyahu
Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov of Pahar, a talmid of the original Tomchei Tmimim in Lubavitch, and of Chava Reise, a
daughter of Reb Asher Margolis, the rav of Rakshik - and through him, a descendant of the Tosfos Yom Tov.

She was nineteen at the chuppah in Kutaisi on July 8, 1930. She made the underground years her own: Tiflis, the
arrest, the camps' long shadow, Gori, and then the exodus - Lvov, Prague, Antwerp, with three children, in the spring
of 1946.

In Antwerp, in the summer of 1947, she gave birth to her youngest son, Sholom DovBer - Berl to the family. Fifteen
days later, on July 30, 1947 - 13 Av - she died. She was thirty-six. The Antwerp burial register holds her as Sara
Chaikin-Kohanoff, born 10.11.1910, and the name, the maiden name, the dates and the window all fit; the register and
the burial index differ over the plot letter and a day, in the small way of clerks, and the original certificate is still being
sought. The inference about what took her - childbirth, two weeks on - is the family's, and it is labeled as such here.

In the 1965 obituary of her husband, two of the nine grandchildren are named Sarah Chana. She was gone eighteen
years, and the name was still being handed forward.

The Tosfos Yom Tov's line
Eliyahu Shmuel and Chava Reise Kahanov

Reb Eliyahu Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov of Pahar - son of Reb Chaim HaKohen Kahanov and Chaya Tzirel - was a

His wife, Chava Reise, was a daughter of Reb Asher Margolis, the rav of Rakshik in Lithuania, a descendant of the

name - the name she was born with - the record has not yet supplied.

Their son was Reb Chaim Osher. Their daughter was Sarah Chana. Between those two, the family crossed the century.

CHAPTER SEVENTEEN

Cyla
Zobina, Schmukler, Chaikin, Ushpol - 1918 - 2006

Her life would one day be told in a book of its own, and it was. But the bones of it belong here too.

She was born in September 1918 - the 27th by her displaced-persons card, the 20th by her JDC card - in Pinsk by both
cards, though the published biography of her life says Koidanov. A textile worker, Yiddish her first language. She
married Meir Eliezer Schmukler, and the war made her a widow with two boys, Zalmen and Froim; her sons' accounts
of their father's death differ - conscription and a German bullet in one telling, a secret-police arrest at the war's start in
another - and no record has yet settled it.

On October 22, 1946, the Allies registered her at Pocking as Zobina Cyla, widowed, born in Pinsk, destination
Palestine. The Joint's Munich office opened a file the next summer - August 5, 1947, destination USA, closed that
November - and the boys' United Nations identity cards from the Pine City assembly center, signed by their mother,
are dated February 16, 1948. Instead there was Paris, and the Joint again: a JDC emigration file, case SWE 66, filed
April 29, 1949 from an address on the rue Bichat, and a departure list that records the Zobina trio, Paris to Sweden, in
the spring of 1949.

In Sweden, or in Paris, or in a DP camp - the three accounts are all in the record, and all are kept - she became the wife
of Rabbi Meir Chaim Chaikin, and the mother of his household. The 1965 directory calls her Tcherna, daughter of
David Zobina; the 1965 obituary calls her Mrs. Celia Chaikin. She raised his children, and her own two sons with
them, and after his death she married Yitzchok Dovber Ushpal. Her brother Hershel - Grisha - survived Siberia, lost a
wife and daughter to the Nazis, and reached Canada after a visa campaign that drew in Prime Minister Trudeau. She
died on October 23, 2006, at eighty-eight, and lies beside Meir Chaim in the Chabad society section of Old
Montefiore, in the same ground as the Rebbe's Ohel.

Michoel Lipsker
1907 - 1985 - Sarah's father

Michoel Lipsker was born in 1907 in a Jewish agricultural colony near Yekaterinoslav - he writes its name in his own
notes, in Yiddish letters no one has yet pinned to a map - and the family later moved to Poltava. His parents were Reb
Chaim Tzvi and Batsheva; her maiden name comes down three ways - the chart says Jaffe, the memoir Mamyaffo, and
Berman appears besides - and all three are kept. His father's death is dated Adar II 5693 in Michoel's own account;
another telling puts it earlier, under interrogation. The two are kept apart, as the record keeps them.

In Leningrad, during the siege, he and his brother-in-law Rabbi Baruch Shifrin did the work no one else could do: they
gave thousands of Jews a Jewish burial. After the war he was the mashpia of the Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva in Kutaisi
- the same Georgia where the Kahanovs had been sent a generation before. In 1946 he escaped west and settled in
Paris, and from Paris the Rebbe made him his first shliach to Morocco; the family joined him there in 1950.

His daughter Sarah married Rabbi Azriel Chaikin. His nephew is Rabbi Mendel Lipskar, who led Chabad of South
Africa from 1971. His memoir - youth, siege, Morocco - survives, preserved in a wedding teshura. His stone names
him Michoel son of Chaim Tzvi Lipsker, gone 5 Iyar 5745. His wife Teibel - Taiba, daughter of Reb Eliyahu Kalman
Par and Henya, sister of Sonia Shifrin - followed him in Iyar 5750; her stone names her father, her husband, and
nothing else, which is also a kind of summary.

The Bachers of Johannesburg
South Africa took a Lithuanian cattle farmer and made him a builder of Jewish
Johannesburg - and took his son at fourteen, sent him through 770 and Montreal, and
got back the man who built the rest.
CHAPTER NINETEEN

Solly Bacher
Chaim Shaul Zev HaLevi Bacher

Chaim Shaul Zev Bacher - Solly, to Johannesburg - came to South Africa from the Ruch family of Rokiskis,
Lithuania, a Lubavitch household with its own place in the movement's memory: in 1930, the Frierdiker Rebbe stayed
with the Ruch family in Rokiskis, the household Solly married into.

In South Africa he farmed cattle, and in 1946 he wanted to sell. The Frierdiker Rebbe advised him to hold. Two years
later an offer arrived - fifty thousand South African pounds - and this time the answer was sell. The family had
remembered for decades that there were letters from the Rayatz; here is what the letters were about.

He married Chana, whose yahrtzeit the family long kept as around 19-20 Adar, the year uncertain; the Johannesburg
burial register now makes it exact - 20 Adar 5754, March 3, 1994 - and she lies at Westpark, E-1072, the plot her son
Koppel and Selma later shared with her. Solly himself, the register records, died on 12 Elul 5725 - September 9, 1965
- and lies at E-1044. After his death, his son Koppel offered the free use of the family home in Observatory to a young
institution of higher learning, and in thanks it was renamed Kollel Yad Shaul in Solly's memory - dedicated in
September 1969, housed in the family home until the beginning of 1980, and carrying his name still. South Africa was
already inside the Rayatz's relief network in Solly's Rokiskis years. A letter of 17 Adar I 5689 - February 1929, one
year before the Rebbe Rayatz stayed with the Ruch family - presses the matzah-for-Soviet-Jewry campaign and counts
on the Chief Rabbi to influence the colonies in general and South Africa in particular; Chief Rabbi Dr. Hertz's
Johannesburg was, even then, an address the Rebbe's office wrote to.

And in the Rebbe's published correspondence there is a letter to him - Igros Kodesh, volume 7, letter 1735, dated 4
Mar-Cheshvan 5713, Brooklyn - encouraging a warm connection with the Previous Rebbe, assuring him the blessings
remain in full force, and guiding him in business and in spirit. It is printed on the next page.

The landsmanschaft's memorial volume gives him a fuller portrait than any American record. Even the scope of his
large businesses "did not change a hair of his folksy, Jewish style and manner": the businesses were closed on Shabbos
and holidays, and he observed Shabbos and the holidays as in his old home. He was known as a philanthropist, but he
distinguished himself with his anonymous help - "there are many people who do not know that he helped them." He
gave generously to the yeshivos of Jerusalem, and he was one of the pillars of the Johannesburg Chabad. His farm
near Germiston was the local place of hakshara for Habonim - the training farm where the young halutzim learned the
work they would carry to the land.

Six more letters tell the story whole. In the last days of September 2026 a set of unpublished correspondence came to
the family - six letters between Solly Bacher and the Frierdiker Rebbe's office, Marcheshvan 5709 to Teves 5710,
October 1948 to January 1950. A telegram of Cheshvan 5710 carries the decision in a handful of words: selling the
farm, and may Heaven grant success. The letters around it give the reckoning as the office kept it: the farm, bought in
1942 for thirty-five thousand pounds, sold for three thousand - and out of the proceeds, two thousand pounds as a gift
to the Rebbe's work, five hundred as a free-loan fund, five hundred to charity. The family's memory of the offer had
always run higher - fifty thousand - and both figures stand here as each record gives them. The last word in the file is
dated 29 Teves 5710, ten days before the passing: agreed to sell the shop. A word about the file itself, in the manner of
this book's notes: the letters survive as a fresh same-day scan shared by an outside correspondent, and their content
matches the printed record at every checkable point - down to a wording a printed volume's footnote could only have
taken from these originals - but the pages carry no catalog numbers, and the Rebbe's replies are the unsigned office
letters of the period. That is how they are presented here. 60

The new shul, Kislev 5724
The dedication - December 1, 1963

In the last days of 1963, Johannesburg's Chassidisher Shul dedicated its new building, and the Rebbe marked it with a
letter - Igros Kodesh, volume 23, letter 8387, written Erev Shabbos Vayeitzei 5724 - to the participants in the
foundation-stone laying and dedication. The date falls on Sunday, 15 Kislev 5724: December 1, 1963.

The building was more than real estate. The shul's rabbinic chair had stood between tenures since Alter Hillewitz, and

Bacher married Selma Goldman.

The builder
Koppel Bacher, 1941 - 2024

In 1962 the Rebbe pressed him on his shidduch with characteristic directness: So why aren't you doing anything about
it? I want you to go right now and arrange a meeting. Koppel went to a phone booth and arranged it. He married
Selma Goldman of Brooklyn, and in 1964 the couple returned to South Africa as emissaries - which reconciles the two
dates the record gives: 1961 for his own return after yeshiva, 1964 for theirs.

Every Hoshanah Rabbah he had an aliyah in the Rebbe's minyan. One year a delayed plane made him miss it, and the
Rebbe said: I missed you this morning - I didn't see you being called to the Torah. The photograph on the next page
catches one such morning in 5752.

South Africa was not gentle. He survived an AK-47 held to his head; his office was attacked five times, and he was
tied up and robbed each time. His own account of it is one line: I just closed my eyes and pictured the Rebbe saying
m'zol zich nit shreken - one must not be afraid. A Chanukah gelt story survives in two tellings - a silver dollar given to
little Shaul at age five, in one, because you won't be here for Chanukah - and both tellings are kept.

He built. He brought and financed the Lipskars' start in South Africa in 1972, helped bring the rabbis who followed -
Yossy Goldman among them - and was a founding member of the Hanholo of the Lubavitch Foundation of South
Africa. When communal bodies wrote to the Rebbe after the 1979 Torah Academy land dispute asking him to curb his
errant emissaries, the Rebbe wrote back telling them to reverse their own decline. And on February 11, 1990 - the day
Mandela walked free - Koppel received and conveyed the Rebbe's message for South African Jewry: Tell them they
have nothing to fear, and that South Africa will be good for Jews until the coming of moshiach.

Selma
Sima Ronya bas Shlomo, 1943 - 2021

Selma Bacher was born Goldman, of Brooklyn - Sima Ronya, daughter of Reb Shlomo and Helen Goldman; one
notice writes Sima Rania bas Shlomo, and the family's reading is kept. Her maternal grandmother was active in the
Lubavitch Ladies Auxiliary - "in which she assisted in raising funds to participate in rescuing the Frierdiker Rebbe and
his family from war-torn Europe," her obituary records - and the family's part in that rescue runs through both her
lines. Her brother Dovid Goldman lives in Flatbush; her sister Rochel Weiner in Queens. That grandmother was Ita
Yehudis, whose yahrtzeit the family keeps on 1 Tammuz; one family telling places her a generation closer, and both
readings are kept.

In Johannesburg she became an educator: founding principal of the Torah Academy Girls High School, and later a
Limudei Kodesh teacher in the elementary school - the half of the building that doesn't make the papers, which is to
say the half that matters. She died on May 3, 2021 - 21 Iyar 5781 - at seventy-eight. The movement's notices
remembered the principal of the Chabad Girls High School; a tribute by a onetime guest, Tzemach Yehudah Richter,
published in Your Aliyah Advisor on the day she passed, remembered the house - an open home, its owners
cornerstones of Johannesburg's Lubavitch community: a sheva brachas at the Bachers', where, he wrote, they treated
us like royalty. Several great-granddaughters now carry her name: Sima Ronya, born again in the next rooms of the
family. The comment thread under her obituary reads like a class register. "A very special lady with deep and
profound insight, eidel and gentle ways and a twinkle in her eye." "A talmida chachoma who learned each day and
taught thousands." "She was both my teacher and principal and she truly cared about each student, explaining with
patience and love." COLlive called her one of the founders of the Jewish community in Johannesburg - a city she and
Koppel reached, in its phrase, when it was "a barren wasteland of Yiddishkeit" - and recorded that she completed an
in-depth study of the entire Tanach twice over. The SA Jewish Report counted thirty-three years in the classroom, and
the year after her death the school named its Nach program for her: "a six-year journey through the whole of Tanach."

One Brooklyn rabbi often mentioned beside her in the record - Rabbi Yossy Goldman, who came to South Africa in
1976 and built Torah Academy's school - is no relation: his father was Reb Shimon Goldman of Crown Heights, hers
Reb Shlomo, and the shared surname is the whole of it.

Nearly half a century on, her school is building again. The school's 2023 annual report records construction begun on
Campus Leah, a purpose-built new campus for the Girls High School she founded in 1979, and the school's first
democratic parent-elected board, seated that October. The half of the building that doesn't make the papers is getting
walls of its own.

For a generation before the destruction, the rabbinate of Rokiskis ran in the family's own circle. From 1906 to 1915 the

and shtetlekh... After he died, his rabbinical seat was taken by his son-in-law, Reb Shmuel, who is now in New York

family record as the great-grandfather of Berl Chaikin of Cleveland - which would make the last rabbis of Rokiskis
kin to Sarah Chana's house. The side of that descent is not stated in the source, and it is left open here as it is in the

Rokiskis told the Alter of Slabodka that "Btzalel der rav" held the town - a single memoir's datum, recorded as such.

In the winter of 5689 Levitin was arrested in Nevel, where the Rebbe had set him over the Beis Midrash l'Rabbanim,
and the Rokiskis chassidim wrote to the Rebbe Rayatz in Riga. The answer, dated 24 Shevat 5689, is the town's
Chabad soul in one paragraph: "his situation is not yet known... It is right and necessary that they gather a proper sum,
to be held until it becomes possible, with G-d's help, to bring him to you - for the sum they wrote of is very small, and
they must endeavor to collect properly." The town was raising ransom money for its former rav, ten years gone. He
survived; by February 1940 he was in Brooklyn - a telegram of 26 Shevat 5700 reaches "Rabbis Levitin and Jacobson"
at their Brooklyn address - and he ended his days the senior mashpia of 770.

After Levitin came Rabbi Betzalel - the landsleit's relief parcel of March 14, 1920, a hundred pounds, was sent "to the
address of the Rakishok Rabbi, Reb Betzalel, of blessed memory" - and after Betzalel, the last: Dr. Zelig Orelovitch,
"Shlomoh Skopishker's son," a PhD of the Berlin Beis Midrash Elyon, married to Roza Snieg. He was invited to a
rabbinate in Sweden and offered one in Johannesburg - the very city the Bachers had made their own - and he stayed.
When the end came at the open pits, he spoke to the condemned for two hours, and perished with Roza and their son
Moshele. The Johannesburg pulpit that might have been his is one of the book's quiet what-ifs.

CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE

Reb Zelig Rakishker
The Lomza rosh yeshiva, 1879 - 1941 - a Ruch of the old home

No document ties him to Sorrel Ruch's line, and the tree keeps him unlinked; but he was a Ruch of Rokiskis in her
own generation, his son wrote his life in Johannesburg in the landsmanschaft's own circle, and the story belongs beside
hers. The memoir runs in the Rokiskis book under the title My Father, Of Blessed Memory, signed Meir Dovid of
Johannesburg; read at full resolution, its byline names the subject - the Lomzha rosh yeshiva Rabbi Yehoshua Zelig
Ruch - and its author is his son, Meir Dovid Ruch.

Rokiskis, the son writes, was famous in the rabbinic-yeshiva world as the birth-town of R' Zelig Rakishker - and
famous in another way at home: the town was, in his phrase, the fortress of Lubavitch chassidus in Lite, and it
shrugged at sitting-and-learning. The boy was born in 1880 by his son's reckoning - the necrology gives 1879, and
both are kept - "the son of the simple and decent Jew R' Michel and Pere, plain, ordinary but honest folks." At
bar-mitzva age he went up to Slabodka, and the Alter asked where he came from and who was rav there. Btzalel is the
rav, the boy answered. The Alter marveled: Btzalel is the rav THERE, and yet such a child comes from there. He
became the Alter's emissary when the musar dispute erupted at Telz and Mir, spending months in each yeshiva until
the quarrel settled - a successful synthesis of lomdus, Chabad and musar, his son writes, formed the complete
yeshiva-man. Reb Lazer Lomzer chose him as son-in-law, and he was appointed rosh yeshiva of Lomza. In the First
World War he evacuated with his family and his students to Priluki, near Poltava, and students from Minsk, Kiev and
Kharkov followed him back to Lomza when he rebuilt. On the road home, his son records, "a warm home we found at
his brother R' Pesach Ruch's, and family." Family tradition holds that this Reb Pesach of Rokiskis was the same
Pesach Ruch who would host the Frierdiker Rebbe in the town in 1931; the name, the place and the era all agree, and
the identification is offered as such.

When his father-in-law founded the yeshiva's branch in the Land, Zelig traveled with the first fifty bochurim - his best
students, his son writes - "to be there the first rosh yeshiva." He loved the land, and he returned to his Lomzha students
all the same. The standard histories record the founding group led by Rabbi Eliyahu Dushnitzer, with Rabbi Moshe
Aryeh Ozer and later Rabbi Reuven Katz at the yeshiva's head; the memoir's account is the family's, and both stand.

In the 1930s the Depression crushed the yeshiva's finances, and he had to leave it for a short time and come to South
Africa to raise funds. The Ponevezher Rav told his son: "The Lomzha Rosh Hayeshiva lacks the ability to make
money. His virtues stand in his way." Yet it worked - "only with his sincerity and decency," the son writes - and
decades later a Jew in Johannesburg was still telling him: your father is remembered here. "Thank God my trip was
successful," Reb Zelig told it. "I influenced one Jewish man to put on teffilin and another to keep his store closed on
the Sabbath." A man in Johannesburg told the son: "To this day I keep my store closed on Shabbos Shuva... in
memory of your father."

In September 1939, in Lomza, German soldiers cut his beard. He answered them "danke sehr" - thank you very much -
the only ironic thank-you, his son writes, that he ever uttered. He refused escape while any of his students could not
come - "go, children, I will yet come to you" - and by the time he could leave, the roads were sealed. Days before the
destruction, students hiding in bunkers smuggled him and his family to Vilna; tired and broken, he paid a last visit to
Rakishok, which welcomed him warmly. On his death the two memorial accounts part ways, and both are printed: one
line in the book says he perished at the hands of the Nazi hangmen in Lomza; his son's account and the necrology
memorial together say he died a martyr in Vilna, with his students - "the beloved and dear in their lives were even in
their death not parted."

The landsmanschaft
The Rakishker circle of Johannesburg

The Rakishoker landsleit of Johannesburg kept the old home alive in the new one, and a Ruch sat at the center of it.
Berl Ruch was born in 1919 in the village of Kurkletz near Rakishok; his father Pesakh, "an ardent Hasid and deeply
religious man," moved the family to town during the First World War, and Berl went from kheder and Talmud Torah
to a yeshiva bench before emigrating to South Africa at the beginning of 1929. There he helped found the Rakishok
Aid Society and served as its treasurer; when the Aid Society and the Landsmanschaft merged he was chosen
vice-chairman, under chairman Yerakhmial Arons-Arsh - "and is to this day," the memorial book says. In the 1930s, at
a committee table in Johannesburg, it was B. Ruch who rose to speak about not buying German goods. In 1937 he sat
on the committee that assisted the visiting fundraiser from home. "He helped the Chabad movement financially," the
profile adds, "and he helped the Joniskelis Rabbi, Reb Yosef Sidrin, when he came to collect money for the Rakishok
Yeshiva. At the same time, he was a Zionist and he was devoted to the Zionist movement heart and soul." On June 16,
1949, Berl and Mrs. Ruch were in the room when the landsleit resolved to publish the memorial book itself, and B.
Ruch was chosen for the five-man commission that made it - the book these chapters have been quoting.

His wife Liba was born in 1921 in Panevezys and came as a girl to Muizenberg; English-schooled and
Yiddish-speaking, she became, the book says, "a devoted member of the Rakishok Landsmanschaft: helps to organize
events, to send packages for landsleit and to gather funds," and was chosen to its committee. "Her love of Yiddish
theater is great. And to this day she takes part in producing and appears with recitations at various small art evenings."

The book's survivor list, compiled around 1952, carries one line the family reads with care: "Pesakh, Leyba and
Basheve Ruch, in Siberia." If that Pesakh is the family's own - the identification is tentative - then the old family tree's
Siberian-exile line is corroborated by the landsleit; but a survivors' list is a list of the living, and the same family tree
dates his death to about 1941. Both are kept.

The book preserves the society's inner life with the same care. Its correspondence committee - Shuel Bacher among
the names, with Zerach Kosef and Berl Stein, the second name read uncertainly - kept up an intensive letter exchange
with the old home, and the Aid Society, the book says, never refused anyone help. Its women are named beside its
men: Toybe Orlin, Etl Arans-Arsh, Libe Ruch, Alte Muskat, and the vice-chairwoman Sarah Klam. The merger of the
Aid Society and the Landsmanschaft passed on July 7, 1948, after Yerachmiel Arans-Arsh rose to defend it. And the
portrait pages give the circle faces: Toybe and Moshe Orlin, who entered the landsmanschaft together;
Yitzchak-Zerach Beinart, among the society's first twelve founders in 1912; Nachum Blacher; Yerachmiel and Etl
Arans-Arsh. A generation earlier, in Rokiskis itself, a photograph taken on July 2, 1925 shows the town's Keren
Kayemet committee of thirteen beneath a hand-painted banner and a Star of David - Yerachmiel Ruch named first
among the standing, and Chana Arsh, Pesach's partner in the iron store, among the seated.

The house on the market square
Pesach Ruch of Rokiskis - merchant, host, exile

In the center of Rokiskis, on the market square, stood a wooden house the Ruch family built in 1921 - built jointly, the
family's record says, by Pesach Ruch and Rachmiel Ruch. The town's 1921 owners' map marks the lot; the kehilalinks
record makes Rachmiel Pesach's brother, while the family's written tree calls him a cousin, and both readings are kept.
The house still stands, a shop at its street level.

Pesach was a metal-products merchant. His store - the ANGRO-IRON store on Independence Square, run with his
partner Chana Arsh, as the memorial book's own caption names her; an earlier reading heard the partner as Khone
Arsh, and the variant is kept - stood at number 26 on the square until the partners split it into separate iron stores in the
late 1930s. He held four properties in the town. A guided tour of the old town, recorded by a visitor, remembers him
elected to the city council - the council lists have not been pulled - and tells a First World War story about him: that he
hid a wealthy persecuted landowner during the 1914 expulsions, and that the landowner's postwar gratitude made him
one of the town's wealthiest men. The book prints it as the tour tells it. The memorial book's own pages are firmer:
Pesach Ruch co-financed the building of the town's Talmud Torah, and when the writer L. Jaffe visited, it was Pesach
Ruch who drove out to meet him with a carriage and a horse - one horse, the page says, where an earlier telling had
two, and both are kept - while Maccabi and Hashomer Hatzair marched, and the Gentiles of the town called him der
yidisher kayzer, the Jewish emperor.

His children: Leibe, with his wife Gita; Batya - Baske - who became Zborovsky; Rachmiel, who settled in Australia;
Pinchas, who settled in Israel. His wife's name the record has not settled: one page gives Basheve, and the memorial
list gives Pere-Eyde, died in distant Siberia, 15 Sivan 1952. Both stand.

The end came by deportation. The tour tells it as July 1941: the wealthy capitalists - Pesach and his wife, their son
Leibe, their daughter Batya - taken to Pavlovsk, in the Altai territory. The dates strain against the documented record -
the mass deportation from Lithuania was June 14, 1941, and by mid-July the Germans held the town - and a memorial
passage places a Ruch iron business in Tomsk, in Siberia, built together with Pesach Ruch; whether that was a later
move or the partner's own exile, the passage has not been re-read. What the family's photograph establishes is the end:
a 1952 photograph from Siberia, and its caption - Pesach died in Siberia, after 1952, and never returned.

The week the Rebbe stayed
Rokiskis, 1 - 9 Adar 5691 - February 18 - 26, 1931

In the first week of Adar 5691 - February 18 to 26, 1931, the dates pinned against the rabbinic calendar - the Frierdiker
Rebbe, Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, came to Rokiskis and stayed the week in Pesach Ruch's house on the
market square. The visit had been postponed from the winter on account of illness. Earlier printings and the family's
own notices long gave 1930, and a summer visit; the Rebbe's published record fixes Adar 5691, and the variants are
kept.

The town competed to host him. By the tour's telling, Pesach won the honor by undertaking all the expenses and
preparing the second floor and special rooms, against a rival who proposed sharing the costs. The documentary record
adds the detail: Pesach built a special room for the guest and connected the house to the electric grid - a rarity in those
days. A family legend, printed as a legend, says the dancing after the Shabbos meal shook the snow clean off the roof -
the only clear roof in Rokiskis that mid-winter.

The week survives in the Rebbe's own published record. The hall was packed day and night, a witness wrote - like the
Days of Awe. The maamar Vehabriech Hatichon was said before kabbalas Shabbos in the lodging itself; Ata Echad
after Mincha in the great beis midrash. On the Monday several hundred women assembled and asked to see him; he
came out and blessed them, and spoke to the women in Yiddish. On the Tuesday evening, at a gathering on taharas
hamishpacha, the Agudas Nashim women's union was founded, its pinkas opened with the founding inscription he
wrote and signed in the house hall. To the teachers of the Yavneh schools he said, on the Thursday: the fate of
hundreds of Jewish children - meaning thousands - is placed on your conscience. That Thursday, 9 Adar, before his
departure, he said the farewell maamar in the house - Na'aseh na aliyas kir ketanah, let us make a small upper room,
the verse of a room prepared for a holy guest - then met the yeshiva students and the Yavneh children, and at half past
two the town saw him off, hundreds escorting him with song, to cries of tzeischem l'shalom.

The photograph of the visit was taken inside the Ruch house. A Ruch descendant, publishing it, wrote that his father
took it during the visit, in the family home; the print in the memorial book carries a local studio's signature - J.
Klopsky, the reading is uncertain - and a second, Hebrew hand, and the two attributions stand side by side. Rachmiel
Ruch, the family photographer, is a candidate for the cameraman; the record does not say.

And the family's own account, from Reuben Ruch, Rachmiel's son, who grew up in that house: the Rebbe stayed a
week; minyanim were held in the house; visitors came and went; and on the day of his departure he blessed the
gathered Ruch relatives. Reuben said that everyone who received the Rebbe's blessing survived the Holocaust. It is
printed as the family tells it - an eyewitness tradition, and the measure of what the week meant.

Torah Academy
The school at the center of the story

The school that Selma and Koppel built their working lives around deserves its own chapter. Rabbi Mendel Lipskar's

school named its Nach program for her, "a six-year journey through the whole of Tanach." The family keeps teaching
in the school: Chanie Bacher's first-graders sang their siddur concerts in the SA Jewish Report's pages. The school
today counts some sixteen hundred students across its campuses.

A childhood in Tbilisi
Azriel Chaikin, born 1931

Azriel Chaikin was born in 1931 in Soviet Georgia - Bandza, in one telling of his own, Zestafoni in the 2019
interviews; both are kept - the year after the Kutaisi chuppah. The sources quarrel over his exact birthday and even his
birth year, with one encyclopedia offering 1938; the family biography's 1931 is preferred here.

He was a child of the underground: he learned in the Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva in Kutaisi while his father sat in the
camps, and his bar mitzvah was in Kutaisi too. Around that bar mitzvah there is the memory the family tells quietly: a
visit to his father, in prison. In the spring of 1946, at fifteen, he made the exodus with his parents and sisters - Lvov,
Prague, Antwerp - and in 1947 he crossed to New York and entered the central Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva at 770.

In 1950, about nineteen, he wrote to the Rebbe about a visa to Canada, where his father was heading. His own rabbinic
career began early: rabbi of a synagogue in Pittsburgh in 1954, dayan in Montreal in 1955 - twenty-four years old and
already sitting on a beis din.

There is no city where a mikva can't be built
Morocco, 5716 - 5718

In the winter of 5716 he married Sarah, the daughter of Rabbi Michoel Lipsker - the shadchan was Rabbi Binyomin
Gorodetsky, and a blessing letter from Rebbetzin Chana is preserved. The records call her both Chaya and Sarah,
perhaps Sarah Chaya; the family knows her as Sarah, and so does this book. They settled in Agadir, Morocco, where
her father had been the Rebbe's first shliach to the country, and they built: a yeshiva in Agadir, a Beis Rivka school for
the girls.

And a mikva. Agadir had none, and the materials were what they were, so the mikva of Agadir was built with blocks
of ice factory concrete - the story survives with the Rebbe's answer attached to it, when the impossibility was raised:
there is no city where a mikva can't be built.

On 15 Shevat 5718 - 1958 - Morocco expelled him, on a false suspicion over aliyah. A governor shouted he's a spy; he
was tailed to the plane. The Rebbe personally telegraphed the United States ambassador and the consul in Casablanca:
I have known Rabbi Chaikin many years; his work is only religious education. The expulsion stood - Moroccan
independence had its own logic - and the couple turned north, to Denmark.

Chief Rabbi

The Rebbe's letters to Azriel
Igros Kodesh, 5716 - 5721

Nine letters from the Rebbe to Azriel Chaikin survive in the printed Igros Kodesh, from the Agadir years to the
Copenhagen rabbinate, and they read like a master class in what the Rebbe asked of his young shluchim.

The first is dated 4 Sivan 5716, weeks after the wedding - volume 13, letter 4413. The Rebbe presses the expansion of
the yeshiva and the camp, remarks that Azriel's new wife should put her talents to work in the camp, and adds a
postscript that belongs to the century: check - unofficially - whether the changed situation for Jews in the Arab lands
would block a return permit if he visited New York.

Letter 4639 of the same volume, 21 Av 5716, chides him over cuts at Beis Rivka - expansion, not standing still -
names Rabbi Binyomin Gorodetzky as his shadchan, and permits him to write that he acts on the Rebbe's suggestion
in buying a building. Volume 14, letter 4874, 26 Cheshvan 5717, founds the all-day Beis Rivka school, and quotes the
Rayatz's street-lamp sicha - the small light that shames the darkness. An open letter of the same volume, 5075, names
Azriel the living spirit of the Agadir mikva. And in volume 15, letter 5328, the Rebbe names him outright his point
man in Morocco. Volume 13, letter 4273, preserves even the silence between letters: the Rebbe tracking that no letter
had arrived from Azriel.

Then the Copenhagen era. Volume 17, letter 6460, 25 Av 5718, is the first letter addressed to Copenhagen - the
timeline pin of the move north, six months after the Morocco expulsion - an avoda letter: when you toil, you find.
Volume 20, letter 7607, 25 Adar 5721, handles the sale of chometz with a communal power-of-attorney form - the
paperwork of a chief rabbi. And letter 7750 of the same volume, 13 Elul 5721, answers a tefillin question and then
settles a career: when Azriel talked of leaving Copenhagen, the Rebbe told him a new rabbinate needs time to bed
down - stay a few more months. He stayed ten years.

The Cleveland rav and the Crown Heights household
Sholom DovBer, Chaya Tzirl, Hinda - the Berl question, answered

Sholom DovBer Chaikin - Berl - was born in 1947 in Antwerp, fifteen days before his mother's death; one
encyclopedia moves his birth to Montreal and dates it 27 Tammuz, but the family was in Antwerp, where his mother
died on July 30, and the Antwerp reading stands. The inference that childbirth took her is the family's, and it is labeled
as such. He grew up, in the family's phrase, never leaving his father's hand until his last day - and he collected the
shiurim that became Tiferes Azriel.

He learned at 770 from 5725 to 5730 and was baal koreh in the Rebbe's mourning-year minyanim. He married
Sheindel-Sheina, daughter of Rabbi Chaim Shneur Zalman Schorr, spent about ten years on shlichus in London, and
became rav of the Chabad community in Cleveland and a member of Vaad Rabonei Lubavitch. His children are
shluchim across the map - Dovid Leib and Menachem Mendel in Toronto, Meir in Montreal, Eliyahu Shmuel in
Barbados, Elchanan in Baltimore, with daughters' households in Moscow, Cleveland, Staten Island, Oak Park,
Philadelphia and Bala - first cousins of Yossi, and a whole cousin-line of their own.

His sister Chaya Tzirl - the Clara, Mrs. Baruch Brickman, of the 1965 obituary - married Baruch ben Tzion Brickman
of Klimovitch on 19 Adar 5718, with the Rebbe as mesader kiddushin. Baruch, who died 29 Iyar 5786, was a
co-founder of the Empire Shtiebel; the 19 Kislev campaign kept his line verbatim - der choson Brickman, drei mol
azoy fil: the bridegroom Brickman gave three times as much. Their son is Rabbi Meir Chaim Brickman of Sea Gate,
named for his grandfather; their daughter Sarah Chana Posner is named for her grandmother. Their sister Hinda is
Mrs. Nathan Gourarie of Montreal, mother of Joseph Yitzchak, Sholem Ber, Ovadya and Sarah Chana.

Yossi and Rivky
The parents

Yossi Chaikin - Joseph Yitzchak, as the 1965 obituary lists him among the nine grandchildren - grew up in Brussels,

AFTERWORD

This edition
September 2026, fifth cut

This book is the Chaikin story as the record stands in September 2026: from the yeshiva the Chofetz Chaim's exiles
carried to Snovsk to the camps of Tiflis, from a frozen-river escape to Antwerp, Stockholm and Montreal, and on into
the family's fourth generation - and now back up the deep tree, to the magnate of Kapitonovka and the Rebbe's
great-grandfather at his stove in Podobranka.

The third cut brought the attendant of the Tzemach Tzedek and the Georgia network that ran through Rabbi Shmuel
Levitin; the last rabbis of Rokiskis and the landsmanschaft that carried the old home to Johannesburg; Sorrel with her
tramcar in the rain; Zaida Shlomo's pulpit and the street that bears his name; the elder Koppel restored to his brother's
side; and the school's own chapter. This fourth cut adds what the last day of September brought: the six farm letters,
read against the family's memory of them; the comment-thread memories of Koppel and of Selma; Campus Leah
rising for Selma's school; the son of Reb Zelig given his full name at last - and one correction, made in the open: the
Lomzha memoir's byline, read again at full resolution, is Meir Dovid Ruch of Johannesburg writing his father's life,
and an earlier reading has been retired.

Everything in these pages is tied to a source - a document in the family archive, a photographed stone, a printed page,
or a memory given by the family. This cut adds what the family asked for: a notes section at the back of the book that
cites, person by person and fact by fact, the record each statement stands on, followed by an outline of the major
sources - the printed Igros Kodesh, the movement's encyclopedia, the Johannesburg burial register and its stones, the
newspapers and the archives. Where records disagree, both readings stand in the text. What the record does not
contain is simply not here.

The family archive keeps growing, and the book will grow with it - but nothing in this edition waits on it. This fifth
cut adds what the last night of September brought: the house on the market square, and the week the Frierdiker Rebbe
stayed in it, from the town's own records and the Rebbe's; Pesach Ruch's store, his partner, his exile and his end in
Siberia; the landsmanschaft's inner life - its committees, its women, its portrait pages - and the Keren Kayemet
committee of 1925, Yerachmiel Ruch standing and Chana Arsh seated; the full filmed transcript of Sunday dollars,
5750; and Zaida Shlomo's arc closed - the stone beside Helen's, and the congregation's end in 2019. Corrections,
memories and photographs are always welcome.

About this book
The Chaikins was assembled for the family from the records above and the family's own archive, in September
2026.

The research tree behind it - three hundred and eleven people, three hundred and fifteen sources, every fact tied
to its evidence - lives in the family archive, and the public tree site carries the same pedigree online. The original
documents and photographs are preserved in the archive; the pages of this book are their telling.

Corrections, memories, photographs and documents are always welcome. This is the September 2026 family
edition.
