About this edition
A book for the family


This edition brings the family narrative and its documentary record together in a
single substantial volume. It preserves the source images and the older editions,
while correcting superseded readings and showing uncertainty where the evidence
remains divided.

Prepared as a private family review edition dated 1 October 2026. The current
published tree recovered for this edition is version 1.23.0, with 132 people and 104
sources. The print register includes a separately documented correction and addition
layer, including family-confirmed descendants and newly legible obituary
households. The unaltered recovered data is retained with the build package.

The core family, the unlinked French research households and the unresolved Kogen
and Janowitz questions are identified separately. A diagram records the evidence for
a household; it does not by itself certify every date or relationship. The
accompanying narrative and source register explain the limits.

The photographs and facsimiles are retained for private review with source credits
and watermarks. Reproduction rights for a public or broadly distributed edition have
not been cleared. Original archive files can contain historical addresses and other
personal information; the source package should remain within the family.

Print specification: US Letter, 8.5 × 11 inches. Binding margin 1 inch on both sides.
Embedded DejaVu fonts. Continuous page numbers, clickable contents, family-group
references and person/source identifiers. The reading copy is trim-sized; a
production export may add the printer’s required outer bleed allowance.



Previous editions remain part of the family archive. This is a new dated edition, not a replacement of the
originals.

Reading this family history
Private family edition • 1 October 2026


The Warszawski story joins families from Warsaw, Babruysk and Subcarpathia with a
Cleveland-born Garson daughter and a Paris-born Garson son. The chapters follow
the people who became Joe and Lori Warszawski’s household, then the nine children
and their families. Original records and photographs sit beside the lives they
document.

Names move between languages and records. Szyman is Shimon; Berta is Grammy;
Ileen is Gabby; Arie Lawrence Hersh is Zeidy Larry. A familiar name is used in the
narrative, while variants remain in the source register and family atlas. A date in a
funeral notice is not automatically a death date, and a cemetery interment date is
identified as such.

The evidence has different strengths. Civil records, court files, contemporary notices
and photographs document what their makers recorded. An oral history records a
person’s recollection. A family memory is valuable testimony, but it does not become
a civil record by repetition. Conflicts remain visible where they affect identity,
kinship or chronology.

The family atlas divides the tree into readable household diagrams, each with stable
person identifiers and a cross-referenced register. Paris and Tigery families, the
Cleveland Kogens and other unresolved leads are kept distinct from the documented
family. A shared surname does not supply a missing parent-child link.

This dated edition preserves the earlier family narrative, corrects superseded
readings and adds the substantive records gathered through 30 September 2026.
Earlier editions and original sources remain unchanged. It is intended for private
family review. Newspaper, cemetery and publisher photographs retain their source
credits; availability in an archive does not establish permission for wider publication.

The places and the generations
A short route through the book


Warsaw and Babruysk lead to Szyman and Berta Warszawski. Berta’s parents were
Mordukh and Elka Zelkind; the later notices name Berta’s sisters and their children.
The war years left gaps that later family notices cannot fill.

Khust, Nagybanya, Auschwitz, Warsaw and the Dachau camp system appear in Larry
Hersh’s testimony. His postwar account runs through Bricha, Palestine and the
Altalena before Cleveland in 1956. The dates, names and sequences in his later
published accounts do not always agree; they are compared rather than combined.

Paris and Cleveland lead to Victor Garson and Pearl Adelstein. Their 1916 marriage,
Victor’s 1919 declaration and Pearl’s 1940 petition give the firm documentary spine
of the Garson story. The French research chapters examine other Garson households
without making them Victor’s ancestors.

Cleveland is where these lines meet. Joe and Lori’s engagement, their children’s
notices, the family’s memories and the graves at Zion Memorial Park record a
household that carried the past into another generation.

WARSZAWSKI AND ZELKIND



The tailor of Cedar Center
Szyman Warszawski 1918-1995


Szyman Warszawski - Shimon to his family - was born in Poland on April 1, 1918; a
Social Security index gives the city as Warsaw. He lived in Russia through the
Second World War, and came home to a Poland that was no longer the world he had
left. In 1962 he immigrated to Cleveland with his wife Berta, and there he built the
life his grandchildren would remember: for twenty-five years he owned the
Mayflower Tailoring Company at Cedar-Center, a member of Kol Israel, a working
man of the old school. He retired around 1988, seven years before his death.

His 1995 obituary described a forty-year marriage to Berta, their son Joseph, five
grandchildren then living, and a brother, Benyamin, in Israel. Later generations
would enlarge the family beyond that contemporary count.

The records disagree about one thing, and the disagreement is worth preserving. His
headstone at Zion Memorial Park gives his Hebrew name as Shimon ben Zisl - his
father named Zisl. A Social Security index entry names his parents as Joseph
Warszavski and Rochel Goldberg. Both are printed here, unsettled, exactly as they
stand; whether Zisl and Joseph identify the same man has not been resolved. What is
certain is the life: Warsaw, the war years in Russia, the return, and then thirty-three
years in Cleveland.

Szyman died on July 18, 1995, and was buried the next day at Zion Memorial Park,
plot 6-6-111 - directly beside Mordukh Zelkind, his father-in-law, at 6-6-110. Their
neighboring graves document the closeness of the two households in Cleveland.
Sources: Family archive, September 2026 edition, original page 8; see the source register.

WARSZAWSKI AND ZELKIND



Berta called Grammy
Bila bat Mordechai 1935-2016


Berta Zelkind was born on May 15, 1935, a daughter of Mordechai and Elka Zelkind,
and grew up in a world that was coming apart. She spoke in later years of where she
fled from - not of where she went, how she returned, or how she met Szyman.

What survives is the warmth. To the next generation she was Grammy. Szyman
called her Bella - the name traveling down the family, as names do. A friend named
Frieda, meeting Berta's grandchildren long after, cried when she realized whose
family they were. "Bertha was my friend," she said. It is a small scene, and it says
more about her than a date can.

Her headstone reads: Bila bat Mordechai - her father Mordechai's name, carried in
stone - May 15, 1935 to November 28, 2016, niftera 27 Cheshvan 5777, "Beloved
Mother, Grandmother, Great Grandmother, Sister." She was interred the next day,
November 29, in section Z6, row 1, grave 58. The family keeps her yahrtzeit on 26
Cheshvan; the stone's Hebrew date reads the 27th. Both are kept here, side by side,
as the family keeps them.

Sources: Family archive, September 2026 edition, original page 11; see the source register.

WARSZAWSKI AND ZELKIND



The Zelkinds of Babruysk
Mordukh and Elka and their four daughters


Mordechai Zelkind - Mordukh - was born in 1912; Elka Kogen, his wife, was born
November 12, 1913, in Babruysk, on the Berezina in what is now Belarus. A Social
Security index names her parents as Isac Kaga and Tsilya Gershon. How Mordukh
and Elka survived the war, and when they came to America, the record does not say.

What it tells us in full is the family they raised. Mordukh's death notice in the Plain
Dealer of September 25, 1996 reads: "MORDUKH ZELKIND, beloved husband of
Elka (nee Kogen), devoted father of Bertha Warshawsky, Gail Zelkind, Maria
Komisarchik (Isaak) and Celya Elin (Matvey), loving grandfather of Joseph
Warshawsky, Dimitry and Kira Komisarchik, Inna and Boris Elin, great grandfather
of seven... Interment Zion Memorial Park."

Every name in that notice is a thread. Bertha Warshawsky is Berta - Grammy - Joe's
mother; the Warshawsky spelling is how the paper heard the name. Gail, Maria and
Celya are Berta's sisters. And "grandson Joseph Warshawsky" is Joe himself, named
in his grandfather's notice twenty-one years before his own stone would stand in the
same cemetery.

Mordukh was interred on September 25, 1996, in section Z6, row 6, grave 110 -
beside Szyman, his son-in-law, at 6-6-111. Elka followed him on March 14, 2005, and
was interred March 16, in Z6, row 4. Her stone reads 1914 - 2005; the Social
Security index gives her birth as November 12, 1913. The year stands as the stone
has it, with the index noted alongside - a single year's disagreement in a life of
ninety-one.

Gail Zelkind, Berta's sister, was born July 25, 1942 and died February 28, 2013. Her
stone carries her portrait, a young woman smiling out of black granite - the only face
of her the record holds.

Sources: Family archive, September 2026 edition, original page 13; see the source register.

HERSH AND GARSON



Zeidy Larry
Arie Lawrence Hersh 1925-2012


Larry Hersh’s 1984 oral history places his birth in Khust, Czechoslovakia, in June
1925. A later compiled tribute gives 1 June. In the family he was Aryeh Leib, Zeidy
Larry, a survivor whose childhood world lay in Subcarpathia. His naturalization card
preserves the earlier name as Arie-Larry-Hersh Kovitz and his chosen American
signature as Arie Lawrence Hersh.

He and his sister Judith survived the Holocaust. Family memory speaks of seven
children; Larry’s testimony says that he had four sisters and was the only boy, five
children in all. These are competing accounts of the family’s size. Judith Dratler’s
2025 obituary identifies her maiden surname as Herskowitz and remembers her
parents and siblings who perished.

Judith, called Judy, died on 5 June 2025, aged ninety-six. Her obituary names her late
husband Phillip Dratler, her daughter Leigh Wasserman and son Jay, and subsequent
generations. She was buried at Bet Olam Cemetery in Beachwood after a private
funeral. The obituary links the surviving Cleveland family to the losses Larry
described, without supplying every missing name.

Larry’s own account dates his arrival in Cleveland to 1956, after Israel and the 1956
war. An earlier index-based estimate of about 1954 is retained as a superseded
inference, not the narrative date. His 1961 naturalization card records him as a
subject of Israel; the oral history supplies the more extensive postwar journey.

In October 1972 the Cleveland Jewish News identified him as a Jewish Survival
Legion leader and national board member and as the former local coordinator of the
Jewish Defense League. The family remembered him at home as Zeidy Larry. His
later public writings show a continued concern with Jewish security and Holocaust
remembrance.

The family’s compiled record gives his death as 3 January 2012. Zion Memorial Park
records interment on that date at Z3-1-35, beside Ileen at Z3-1-34. The exact death
day is not independently established by the interment entry alone.

Sources: Larry Hersh oral history, 13 August 1984, USHMM RG-50.091.0080; 1961 naturalization card; CJN, October
1972; Judith Dratler obituary, 9 June 2025; Zion Memorial Park register.

HERSH AND GARSON



Larry tells his childhood and war
The oral history recorded on 13 August 1984


The following account summarizes Larry’s 1984 oral history, interviewed by Peggy
Nathan for the National Council of Jewish Women, Cleveland Section, and preserved
by the USHMM. The supplied transcript warns of transcription errors. Scenes,
numbers and dialogue below are attributed recollections rather than independent
verification of each event.

He was born in Khust, Czechoslovakia - he spells it C-H-U-S-T - a town of thirty-five
or forty thousand, eight or ten thousand of them Jews, in June 1925. His was, he
says, an ideal childhood: an affluent home - his father dealt in lumber and wine -
cheder, then Czech elementary school, then the Hungarian gymnasium, until the
Jewish students were put out; summers in the Carpathians; Betar, where he rose to
lead the region's youth. He read Gone with the Wind, he liked to say, in four
languages. In 1938 he won a fully paid place at a Prague school for talented
youngsters - and his paternal grandmother went to the rabbi: he'll eat treyf, there'll
be no Shabbos, no tefillin. His father canceled the ticket.

In late 1942 the Zionist organization chose him for one of Khust's four Palestine
certificates. His mother objected - it would look like the big macher was taking for
himself - and his younger sister refused it too: she wanted the family together. He
gave it away. "The paradox of it," he says, "is the ones who left, they all survived."

Then the ghetto. He organized the food wagons on the move in; the Judenrat man
called him by his Jewish name, Leibe; the Gestapo chief interrogated him personally.
Warned that his arrest was coming, his father bought him a way out - an army call-up
which the transcript associates with a commander rendered “Kelety”. In the labor
service at Nagybanya he organized a hard core of eight men and smuggled a wagon
of bread, hidden under hay, to the starving ghetto; the general caught him and
looked the other way - "next time don't be a dumb Jew and let yourself be caught" -
and later praised the job well done. His friend Leonard Fox, who lived to see
Cleveland, took forty-five lashes on a later run. Larry escaped over the fields, came
back to the ghetto at night, and was deported with its liquidation in the morning.
Sources: USHMM, Larry Hersh oral history, RG-50.091.0080, parts 1-2; interview 13 August 1984. See the comparison
chapter for later versions.

HERSH AND GARSON



Auschwitz and Warsaw
Larry’s testimony continued


Five days in a freight car, eighty to a hundred to a car, water and nothing else, and
then Auschwitz, two days before Shavuot - the testimony’s 1943 references conflict
with the established 1944 ghetto and deportation chronology. Mengele conducted
the selection; Larry said he was seventeen, and was sent right. The transcript
renders his prisoner number as 8485 in one passage and 845 in another. Learning
that his father and an uncle had been sent to Warsaw, he swapped blocks to follow
them - to the camp built on the ruins of the ghetto, where prisoners cleaned bricks
from bombed buildings and loaded trains for the Russian front. He found his father
after six weeks. He translated for a guard the words written in blood on the
Anielewicz bunker wall: we were betrayed, remember us. Assigned to clean the
killer-dog cages, he fed his father, his uncle and his friends for weeks on the kitchen
food the work earned. He saw the SS burn bound prisoners stacked, he said, like
wood - "the horror of being stripped of the ability to even scream at one's own pain."

The sequence is preserved as Larry recalled it. The accompanying original interview
and its transcripts allow the family to return to his words. The transcript’s year and
prisoner-number conflicts remain unresolved; those details should not be copied into
a civil chronology without qualification.

Sources: USHMM, Larry Hersh oral history, RG-50.091.0080, parts 1-2, interview 13 August 1984.

HERSH AND GARSON



From the camps to Cleveland
Larry’s testimony continued


In August 1944 the camp dissolved ahead of the Russian advance. Some ten
thousand were marched out - two days standing without food or water, then the
salt-herring ration, and prisoners who stooped to drink had their necks crushed.
Fewer than eight thousand reached Kutno; about two thousand survived the cattle
train to Dachau. His father and uncle made it. From Dachau to Landsberg, Lager 7 -
underground earth huts, typhus quarantine - and then Kaufering, Lager 4, night
construction for the underground airfields. There his father, forty-eight and broken,
was taken away with the older generation, and was never seen again.

In the early spring of 1945 Larry lay twenty-eight days with typhus, unfed, untreated
- an SS guard held a rifle to his head, and a second guard said leave him, he's
finished anyhow; save the bullet. In May the evacuation train was strafed - the US
Air Force disabled the locomotive - and he and two others, the last three of his
eight-man hard core, escaped through the forest to American-occupied
Schwabhausen. He weighed thirty-eight kilos.

The after-war had its own roads. In the hospital, a Jewish US Army doctor traced his
family by telephone from Cleveland - the message ran that Leibi Hersch, son of the
sister Leah, was alive: so his mother's name is known. In Prague he learned that his
next-to-youngest sister had survived, liberated at Bergen-Belsen. In Budapest he was
recruited by Joel Brand - Larry had seen Eichmann there in 1943 without knowing
who he was - and he ran the Bricha borders from 1945 to 1947, sailed for Palestine,
was hunted by the British, was smuggled out to Cyprus hidden among the oranges,
and returned to Israel in 1948 on the Altalena. Eight years in the Israeli army carried
him through the 1956 war.

In 1956 he came to Cleveland, to that sister - she had been there since 1947, taken in
by their uncle Janowitz "like one of their own daughters." He met his wife in
Cleveland, and the rest of his story is this book's: Ileen, three daughters, the
Marmarosher temple on Lancashire - one record says B'nai Jeshurun, and both are
kept - and the grandchildren who called him Zeidy Larry.

Sources: USHMM, Larry Hersh oral history, RG-50.091.0080, parts 2-3. Numerical estimates and sequences are Larry’s
recollections.

Bread for the ghetto
Two versions of an act of resistance


A second published account attributes a closely matching story to “Larry Hirsch.”
Adam Reviczky’s book places this speaker in Huszt, born in 1925, speaking several
languages, and reaching Nagybanya with a summons procured by his father. These
unusual shared details make the identity match strong. They do not turn the two
tellings into independent witnesses.

In the 1984 oral account, bread was hidden under hay and the commander gave
quiet approval after the fact. The later book describes an audience first: Larry had
collected bread from labor servicemen’s rations, and Lieutenant Colonel Reviczky
instructed the men to take the bread by horse-drawn transport before it dried out.
The book describes an arrest by gendarmes and difficulty securing release.

The later version names Reviczky explicitly, while the oral transcript renders the
commander’s name as Kelety. The later account puts the summons and flight to
Nagybanya in April 1944. It describes hiding with Christian acquaintances and travel
into the Great Plain before deportation. The oral account instead describes a return
to the ghetto after a failed bread run and deportation the next morning.

These differences matter because an apparently seamless story would erase what
the sources actually say. The two accounts agree on a young multilingual man, a
father’s effort to save him, bread deliveries, danger and subsequent deportation.
Their precise sequence and the commander’s name remain questions for further
record and audio work.

The text examined was the publisher’s OCR of the 2008 Múlt és Jövő edition, printed
pages 448-450. The original page images were not available for visual verification.
The chapter therefore paraphrases cautiously and supplies no purported exact
Hungarian transcription. A bibliographic record for a 1985 edition does not establish
that the passage was identical in that edition.

Sources: Adam Reviczky, Vesztes háborúk - megnyert csaták (2008), pp. 448-450, publisher OCR at szaktars.hu;
USHMM RG-50.091.0080, parts 1-2; research comparison, 30 September 2026.

HERSH AND GARSON



The Altalena in Larry’s memory
The 1984 testimony and two newspaper accounts


Larry’s 1984 oral history says that he returned to Israel on the Altalena. Later
Cleveland Jewish News stories add a memorable episode: he swam ashore at Kfar
Vitkin with a waterproof pouch for Menachem Begin. Ellen Harris published a
version in 2003; Cynthia Dettelbach published another in 2008. Both are accounts
attributed to Larry, not independent corroboration by two passengers.

The 2008 account describes recruitment to move arms from Czechoslovakia through
the American zone of Germany, a journey to Marseille at the end of April 1948, and a
day of loading. Larry remembered the Israeli flag, Hatikvah and tears. He described
himself and four other swimmers carrying a pouch ashore and Begin welcoming him.
In this telling he never learned what the pouch contained.

The 2003 version calls the contents documents supplied by a man rendered
“Stovsky” and links his arrival closely to the Latrun fighting. The 2008 version says
that the group was detained at Kfar Vitkin for forty-eight hours before entering the
unified fighting force. The book preserves both versions without choosing a hidden
pouch content or harmonizing the sequence.

The newspaper chronology contains errors. The 2008 article prints “June 1, 1948”
and calls it Friday; 1 June was a Tuesday, while 11 June was a Friday. Palmach
history and the later James T. Williams voyage account give 11 June as the
departure. The newspaper dates the burning to 21 June; the historical accounts give
22 June. A dropped digit may explain one error, but that explanation remains an
inference.

Reported ages also disagree with a 1925 birth: twenty in the 2008 voyage account
and eighty in the 2003 article. Passenger totals vary between the accounts and may
refer to different groups. These figures do not generate new birth dates or a settled
passenger count. The 2003 photograph is captioned Zev Harel and must not be used
as a portrait of Larry.
Sources: Cleveland Jewish News, “Area veterans of 1948 war refute New Historians,” 15 May 2003; “Remembering the
Altalena,” 22 May 2008; USHMM oral history part 3; James T. Williams and Avi Diskin, The Northern Mariner 34(1),

Larry Hersh in the Cleveland press

HERSH AND GARSON



The Garsons of Paris
Victor Garson 1895-1978


Victor Garson was born in Paris on May 30, 1895, the son of Solomon and Rachael
Garson. In 1902 - so his wife would swear decades later - he entered the United
States at New York, a boy of seven; he had sailed from Le Havre, his travelling
companions have not been established by a passenger manifest.

He grew up in Cleveland, became a bookkeeper, and on September 12, 1916 married
Pearl Adelstein in Cuyahoga County. Three years later, on September 18, 1919, he
stood in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio and swore his
Declaration of Intention - the first formal step toward citizenship. The clerk wrote
him down: born Paris, May 30, 1895; occupation bookkeeper; last foreign residence
"Pairs, France," the clerk's own misspelling of Paris; emigrated from Le Havre, the
vessel unnamed. He renounced his allegiance to the French Republic. He was
twenty-four, three years married, a father of four-month-old Rochelle.

The certificate itself took fifteen more years: Victor Garson was naturalized on
November 20, 1934. He lived the rest of his life in Cleveland, and the compiled
family record gives his death at East Cleveland on 13 August 1978, five years after
Pearl. They lie at Zion Memorial Park. His notice, in the Plain Dealer of 14 August
1978, does not print a death day; it styles him simply husband of the late Pearl; the
funeral was held that Monday at three o'clock at the Berkowitz-Kumin Memorial
Chapel, and the family sat shiva again at the Sanford Simons'. The notice's one
misprint is preserved as printed: it calls him dear grandmother of eight and great
grandmother of one.

One page of his story was nearly misread. An early transcription of the declaration
gave his last foreign residence as Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine - and for a while the
search ran to Metz. A magnified reading of the original settled it: the word is "Pairs,"
Paris, where he was born. The correction is printed here as it belongs in a family
record - plainly, and in the same ink as the mistake.

Sources: Family archive, September 2026 edition, original page 23; see the source register.

HERSH AND GARSON



An American twice over
Pearl Adelstein Garson 1897-1973


Pearl Adelstein was born in Cleveland on December 1, 1897 - American from her first
breath, in the city central to her later family record. Family memory had placed her
birth in France, or in Latvia; her own hand settles it. On January 25, 1940, swearing
her Petition for Naturalization, she wrote: "born in Cleveland, Ohio on Dec. 1, 1897.
My race is Hebrew."

That an American-born woman should have to petition for citizenship at all is the
story. When Pearl married Victor Garson on September 12, 1916, he was a French
national - and under the Expatriation Act of 1907, an American woman's citizenship
followed her husband's. By saying yes to Victor, Pearl lost the country of her birth.
The Cable Act of 1922 opened the way back, and in 1940 she took it, swearing "I
have not acquired any other nationality by affirmative act." Her petition was granted;
certificate 73676. Cleveland-born, made foreign by marriage, American again by her
own signature.

The petition is a family document in the fullest sense. It lists her three daughters
with their exact dates, all born in Cleveland: Rochelle, May 9, 1919; Rosamond,
January 6, 1926; Ileen, July 30, 1933. Ileen is Gabby. On one sworn page, Pearl gives
us her birth, her marriage, her husband's crossing and naturalization, and the
birthdays of all three girls.

The compiled record gives Pearl’s death at Cleveland Heights on 3 March 1973; her
newspaper notice itself gives the 4 March service, not a death date. Her obituary
names her daughters - Rosamond Simon, Ileen Hersh and Rochelle Horwich - and
through Ileen the line runs on: to Lori, to Joe's house in Cleveland, and to the nine
grandchildren named in Gabby's own notice thirty-six years later. Her funeral was
held at the Berkowitz-Kumin Memorial Chapel on Sunday, March 4, 1973, at two
o'clock, with interment at Zion Memorial Park, and the notice asked, Please omit
flowers. The family sat shiva at the home of Mr. and Mrs. Sanford Simon - Rosamond
and her husband, identified by the notice itself.

Pearl’s 1973 notice also names siblings: Harry Allen; Sydney Allen of Los Angeles;
Edward, Louis and Howard Allen of Florida; and the late Rose Karr. These named
siblings broaden the Adelstein family beyond the three daughters through whom the
earlier narrative followed her. The notice does not itself explain when or why the
brothers used Allen.
Sources: Family archive, September 2026 edition, original page 26; see the source register.

A postwar childhood
Joe Warszawski born 19 October 1959


Joe Warszawski - Joseph Sam, Yosef Yitzchak ben Shimon - was born on October 19,
1959, in Poland, into the after-silence of the war. The family remembers the country
but not the city; the record has not yet supplied it. His father had been born in

she would only ever describe as the place she fled from. Three years after Joe was

the Heights, stepping up to the Torah the week he turned thirteen. He went on to
Cleveland Heights High School, class of 1978, where the school's memorial page
today keeps his name and his dates.

Of those years the family keeps the texture rather than the chronology: a household
with one child at its center, Szyman at the tailoring shop at Cedar-Center, Berta -
Bella - at the heart of the home.

What a household remembers
Family recollections


A daughter remembers that Joe woke very early to go to the market. It is one line,
and it holds a whole life: the father up before the house, out the door in the dark,
doing the day's work before the day began.

Another memory has music in it. On Purim, people from Telshe yeshiva would come

unmistakable - the students spilling in, the dancing, the house loud with it. It is the
kind of detail a pedigree never holds, and a family never loses.

And one remark keeps its own mystery: he had a thing for skydiving. Whether he
jumped, watched, or only talked about jumping, the line is printed as it was given - a
father's small daring, preserved by the people who loved him.

family drove in to Cleveland and spent the week together in the house.

Where the proven Garson line stops
A family surname and several distinct branches


Victor’s marriage record names his parents as Solomon and Rachael Garson. His
sworn declaration gives Paris as his birthplace. No recovered French birth act or
passenger manifest yet connects those three people to the other Garson families
described in this part. The diagrams and narratives therefore distinguish the direct
family from documentary research households.

An early reading of Victor’s last foreign residence as Metz was corrected to the
clerk’s “Pairs,” meaning Paris. The Metz marriage of Aron Garson and Caroline Levy
in 1865 remains a lead in its own right, not evidence that Victor’s father Solomon
was their son. A shared surname and a plausible chronology are insufficient.

Paris civil acts uncovered Julia’s children, Aline Garson, Louise’s son Gustave and
the household of Charles Alphonse Garson. Tigery and neighboring communes
yielded another extensive Garson or Garcon network. These records have genuine
historical value even while their bridge to Victor remains missing.

This section prints the people and acts as research families, with the missing bridges
stated on each relevant page. Superseded spelling guesses and table errors have
been replaced by the later documented readings. A bounded unsuccessful search is
described as a search result, not proof that an event never happened.

THE FRENCH RESEARCH FAMILIES



The Metz question
Aron Garson and Caroline Levy


Where did the Garsons come from before Paris? The family's oldest documentable
footprint is Victor's - Paris, 1895. Behind that, the trail thins to a surname and a
region.

In Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine, the civil register records a marriage on September 1,
1865: Aron Garson to Caroline Levy. In the Metz-Chambiere cemetery the same
couple lies under a second spelling - "Gerson Aron, 1841-1924" and "Levy Caroline
Gerson, c.1845-1909." A rare surname, one region, two spellings - and in Cleveland,
Victor's marriage record names his father as Solomon.

If a Solomon Garson - or Gerson - was born to Aron and Caroline in Metz in the years
after 1865, the line from Metz to Paris to Cleveland would close into a single thread.
That record has not yet been found. A "Guerchen, Abraham Gershon" who died at
Hellimer in 1855 may belong to an earlier generation still.

A passenger manifest for Victor’s reported 1902 arrival from Le Havre remains an
important missing record. Neither the fact that he was seven nor the later petition
establishes which relatives travelled with him. The search must test the whole
household, including surname variants.

Julia and her children
A Paris household not yet linked to Victor


The registers of Paris hold three more Garsons, and they hold them close. On
November 4, 1895 - five months after Victor's birth across the same city - a young
domestic named Julia Maria Garson, eighteen years old, bore a son, Marcel Eugene
Jules, at a midwife's home in the 18th arrondissement. No father is named on the act;
the boy carried his mother's name. Two weeks later, on November 18, Julia crossed
the city to the 17th arrondissement and stood before a second officer to recognize
the boy as hers - act N° 3451, an acte de reconnaissance, a young mother making
sure the law knew her son was her son. That act gives her name in full: Julia Emelina
Perpetue Garson.

In January 1897 the registers record a second son: Leon Jules, born on the
seventeenth in the 5th arrondissement, to a mother the clerk wrote down as Melina
Perpetue Garson, twenty, a domestic - a closely matching name and address
supporting the identification, though the name forms differ. And in March 1898,
back in the 18th, a daughter: Charlotte, born to Julia Garson, now twenty-two, at
another midwife's rooms. Again, no father is named. Three children in four years -
Marcel, Leon, Charlotte - each carried into the register by their mother alone.

The ages and uncommon name elements support treating the acts as the record of
one woman, Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson, born about 1877. Her relationship, if
any, to Solomon Garson is unproved. Marcel, Leon and Charlotte must not be called
Victor’s cousins unless the missing parentage is established.

Leon's act carries the fullest afterlife. In its margin, two later hands recorded a
whole French lifetime: married to Marie Marthe Therese Rollet at Epineuil, in the
Cher, on August 21, 1920; died at Lagny-sur-Marne on August 31, 1980 -
eighty-three years, from a midwife's rooms in Paris to a marriage in the countryside
and a long life past the wars. The Rollet line may have descendants living today.
Marcel's margin is briefer: a death recorded on February 21, 1963. What became of
Charlotte, the registers have not yet said.

THE FRENCH RESEARCH FAMILIES



The Paris households
Documented separately and not merged


The supplied Paris survey of birth tables for 1893-1902 identified several distinct
Garson households. Its findings are bounded by the tables, dates and spellings
examined; absence from that survey is not proof that another household did not
exist.

Julia's is the first: the unwed domestic whose three children fill the pages before this
one. The second is Louise Garson, a rag-picker three years Julia's senior, also unwed,
who registered her son Gustave in the 5th arrondissement in May 1901 - two women
of the same rare name, alone in the same city, a few years and a few streets apart;
no sister relationship has been established. The third is a wine merchant's family:
Charles Alphonse Garson and Juliette Honorine Boclin, whose son Marcel Alphonse
was born in the 4th in September 1902. And the fourth a shoemaker's: Marius
Gabriel Garson and Marie Guden, whose daughter Aline was born in the 6th in
October 1894 - and whose margin, written twenty-six years later, records her
marriage to Edouard Adolphe Godenne in 1920.

Four doors, one surname. Which of them opens onto Solomon and Rachael - onto
Victor - the acts so far do not say; none of them names him. But the sweep that was
meant to find one boy's birth certificate found a small Paris of Garsons instead, and
every door is now on the map.

A fifth household is documented by Armandine Garson’s 1893 birth act: Auguste
Garson and Francoise Armandine Goujot. The proposed identification of her father
with Auguste, born on 15 August 1864 to Antoine Joseph and Caroline Valentine, is
an age-matched inference. That bridge remains qualified, even though earlier
compiled data placed the acts under one person.

Antoine and Caroline
A marriage cited but not located


Auguste Garson’s 1864 birth act names Antoine Joseph Garson and Caroline

shortened transcription of her surname to Lamperiere. The act states that the

found embedded inside the register. The final untabled acts were examined
separately. The documented negative is limited to the searched register and bands.
It does not justify the stronger claim that the couple never married.

The contradiction remains specific: a contemporary birth act cites a marriage that
the searched marriage series has not yielded. A wrong mairie, a marriage outside
Paris, a spelling or indexing problem, or a mistake in the birth declaration remain
possibilities. None has been established.

A restored pre-1860 card search also failed to identify the Antoine-Caroline pairing
in the inspected surname band. Paris lost many original records, and only a portion
were reconstructed. That negative cannot exclude an earlier marriage. The supplied
Antoine Joseph birth-card and reconstructed-act packet provides a route to further
verification, but does not itself complete the marriage bridge.

proven family. Aline’s father Marius Gabriel and the Auguste born in 1864 are not
merged from an approximate age match. The independent identities and source
records must survive until an act explicitly joins them.

The Kogen question
A Cleveland household without a proven link to Elka


Mordukh Zelkind’s 1996 notice plainly identifies his wife as Elka, nee Kogen. Elka’s
Social Security index names her parents as Isac Kaga and Tsilya Gershon. Rabbi
Meyer Kogen’s Cleveland career made him a plausible research candidate, but the

The original Plain Dealer news obituary of 9 December 1973 describes Meyer as
eighty-one, a former principal of the Columbia Avenue branch of the Cleveland
Hebrew Schools, born in Poland and in Cleveland since 1922. It says that he retired
in 1960, taught at the College of Jewish Studies, led Hebrew teachers’ and
Hebrew-language organizations, and wrote pamphlets on prayer and the Bible.

The article says he died “yesterday,” giving 8 December 1973 by calculation. The
printed age conflicts with other memorial birth-year claims, including 1888 and
1889. His separate family notice calls him the widower of Esther Reiter; father of
Rabbi David C. Kogen, Naomi Spiegelman, wife of Rabbi Marvin Spiegelman, and the
late Leo; grandfather of nine; and brother of Jacob Eshel, Yetta Pietrokowski and
Tzipora Kroitblat. The last surname is a tentative print reading.

corrupted machine transcription. It names Rabbi David C., Naomi I. Spiegelman and
the deceased Arye as children. It also names siblings Jennie Levine, Ruth Sachnoff,
Yehudit Asedman, Shoshana Levovsky, Tzipora Pelech and Yitzchak Reiter. The less
familiar overseas surnames need corroboration before normalization.

“Arye” in 1960 and “Leo” in 1973 may refer to the same deceased child, but the
notices alone do not establish equivalence. Esther’s notice announces services for 4
April 1960 and does not supply a death day or cemetery. None of these newly legible
details proves a relationship to Elka. The Kogen household is therefore documented
separately in the research register.

The uncle in Cleveland
Janowitz and the missing first name


Larry’s 1984 oral history gives one of the most personal bridges between Europe and
Cleveland. After liberation, a Jewish U.S. Army doctor located Larry’s maternal uncle
through a telephone directory. The message identified the survivor as Leibi Hersch,
son of the uncle’s sister Leah. The uncle was already corresponding with Larry’s
surviving sister, later sponsored her immigration and housed Larry for three months
after his 1956 arrival.

The examined passage gives the surname Janowitz but no first name. A 1939 notice
for Louis E. Janowitz names siblings including Sarah Herskovitz and Rose
Herskovitz. That is a useful lead, but Louis died before 1956 and cannot be the uncle
who hosted Larry then. His siblings and their households have not been linked to
Leah.

The 1940 directory supports the household of Louis’s widow Lena and daughter
Sadie; it does not establish the Hersh connection. A Louis Herskovitz married to
Rose, with children Carl, Max, David and Esther Hoffman, is another candidate
household with no demonstrated maiden-name bridge. Morris and Eleanor Janovitz
in the 1955 directory, and the George and Carrie Janowitz family, likewise remain
separate.

Directory coverage limits the negative evidence. After 1951, the main Cleveland
volume did not include all the Heights communities. Separate 1955 and 1956
Heights volumes were not reached in this research. Failure to find the uncle in a
main-volume search is not proof that he was absent from Cleveland.

The decisive next evidence would be a civil or immigration record identifying Leah’s
maiden name and brother, or the original sponsorship and arrival file. Until then, the
uncle remains a real person in Larry’s testimony whose full identity is unknown. The
candidate households are not connected to the core family tree.

Sources: USHMM RG-50.091.0080, part 3; CPL Louis E. Janowitz necrology 163244; Cleveland directories 1940 and

A wartime inquiry from New York
An unlinked Herskovitz household at Huszt in 1917


A three-page U.S. diplomatic packet preserves a First World War welfare inquiry
about a Herskovitz family at Huszt in Maramaros County. On 25 January 1917, the
American Consulate General in Budapest reported to the State Department that Mr.
and Mrs. Hers Herskovitz, their family and the child Emil Wiessel were in good
health.

The report refers back to instruction 631 of 19 December 1916, file 364.64/178. That
earlier instruction has not been located. The State Department’s reply of 14 March
1917 was addressed to Miss Matilda Herskovitz in New York. It told her that her
father and mother remained at the address she had supplied, and that the family and
Emil were well.

The original letters establish a transatlantic inquiry and a named household before
Larry Hersh’s birth. They do not establish that Matilda, Hers or Emil belonged to
Larry’s family. The child’s surname differs slightly between the letters; no
connection to Elie Wiesel is asserted. The care-of initial is read as T., superseding an
earlier R. transcription.

This is exactly the kind of record worth preserving without forcing it into the tree: a
clear family event in the right region, with no demonstrated connecting generation.
Its value will increase if a civil record later identifies Larry’s paternal grandparents
or other relatives.
Sources: U.S. National Archives, RG 59, NAID 286801887, decimal file 364.64/239, images 122-124;
https://catalog.archives.gov/id/286801887?objectPage=122 ; original letters and source note.

SOURCES AND INDEX



Preserving the record
For the next family edition


This book is meant to be read, corrected and kept. A document’s uncertainty can be
part of the family history: a second name, a conflicting date, a missing brother, a
child recognized in a later act. The next discovery should be able to change the story
without destroying the evidence that came before it.

The archive package accompanying this edition includes the editable manuscript, the
complete recovered tree, a separately documented print graph, original downloads,
extracted illustrations, build scripts and a source manifest. Earlier books and
sources are preserved. The PDF is a designed reading edition of that material, not a
claim that every external record has been found.

Please keep this private family edition and its source package within the intended
family audience. Obtain any necessary author, archive, photographer and publisher
permissions before wider distribution. Watermarks remain intact, and source
resolution limits are recorded in the production report.
