Private family edition • 2 October 2026

The Warszawski story joins families from Warsaw, Babruysk and Subcarpathia with a
Cleveland-born Garson daughter and a Paris-born Garson son. The chapters follow the
people who became Joe and Lori Warszawski’s household, then the nine children and their
families. Original records and photographs sit beside the lives they document.

Names move between languages and records. Szyman is Shimon; Berta is Grammy; Ileen is
Gabby; Arie Lawrence Hersh is Zeidy Larry. A familiar name is used in the narrative, while
variants remain in the source register and family atlas. A date in a funeral notice is not
automatically a death date, and a cemetery interment date is identified as such.

The evidence has different strengths. Civil records, court files, contemporary notices and
photographs document what their makers recorded. An oral history records a person’s
recollection. A family memory is valuable testimony, but it does not become a civil record by
repetition. Conflicts remain visible where they affect identity, kinship or chronology.

The family trees follow connected branches across generations, with a named register at the
back. Paris and Tigery families, the Cleveland Kogens and other unresolved leads are kept
distinct from the documented family. A shared surname does not supply a missing
parent-child link.

This dated edition preserves the earlier family narrative, corrects superseded readings and
adds the substantive records gathered through 2 October 2026. Earlier editions and
original sources remain unchanged. It is intended for private family review. Newspaper,
cemetery and publisher photographs retain their source credits; availability in an archive
does not establish permission for wider publication.

The places and the generations
A short route through the book

Warsaw and Babruysk lead to Szyman and Berta Warszawski. Berta’s parents were Mordukh
and Elka Zelkind; the later notices name Berta’s sisters and their children. The war years left
gaps that later family notices cannot fill.

Khust, Nagybanya, Auschwitz, Warsaw and the Dachau camp system appear in Larry
Hersh’s testimony. His postwar account runs through Bricha, Palestine and the Altalena
before Cleveland in 1956. The dates, names and sequences in his later published accounts
do not always agree; they are compared rather than combined.

Paris and Cleveland lead to Victor Garson and Pearl Adelstein. Their 1916 marriage, Victor’s
1919 declaration and Pearl’s 1940 petition give the firm documentary spine of the Garson
story. The French research chapters examine other Garson households without making
them Victor’s ancestors.

Cleveland is where these lines meet. Joe and Lori’s engagement, their children’s notices, the
family’s memories and the graves at Zion Memorial Park record a household that carried the
past into another generation.

WARSAW AND BABRUYSK




The tailor of Cedar Center
Szyman Warszawski 1918–1995

Szyman Warszawski - Shimon to his family - was born in Poland on April 1, 1918; a Social
Security index gives the city as Warsaw. He lived in Russia through the Second World War,
and came home to a Poland that was no longer the world he had left. In 1962 he
immigrated to Cleveland with his wife Berta, and there he built the life his grandchildren
would remember: for twenty-five years he owned the Mayflower Tailoring Company at
Cedar-Center, a member of Kol Israel, a working man of the old school. He retired around
1988, seven years before his death.

His 1995 obituary described a forty-year marriage to Berta, their son Joseph, five
grandchildren then living, and a brother, Benyamin, in Israel. Later generations would
enlarge the family beyond that contemporary count.

The records disagree about one thing, and the disagreement is worth preserving. His
headstone at Zion Memorial Park gives his Hebrew name as Shimon ben Zisl - his father
named Zisl. A Social Security index entry names his parents as Joseph Warszavski and
Rochel Goldberg. Both are printed here, unsettled, exactly as they stand; whether Zisl and
Joseph identify the same man has not been resolved. What is certain is the life: Warsaw, the
war years in Russia, the return, and then thirty-three years in Cleveland.

Szyman died on July 18, 1995, and was buried the next day at Zion Memorial Park, plot
6-6-111 - directly beside Mordukh Zelkind, his father-in-law, at 6-6-110. Their neighboring
graves document the closeness of the two households in Cleveland.

Berta called Grammy
Bila bat Mordechai 1935–2016

Berta Zelkind was born on May 15, 1935, a daughter of Mordechai and Elka Zelkind, and
grew up in a world that was coming apart. She spoke in later years of where she fled from -
not of where she went, how she returned, or how she met Szyman.

What survives is the warmth. To the next generation she was Grammy. Szyman called her
Bella - the name traveling down the family, as names do. A friend named Frieda, meeting
Berta's grandchildren long after, cried when she realized whose family they were. "Bertha
was my friend," she said. It is a small scene, and it says more about her than a date can.

Her headstone reads: Bila bat Mordechai - her father Mordechai's name, carried in stone -
May 15, 1935 to November 28, 2016, niftera 27 Cheshvan 5777, "Beloved Mother,
Grandmother, Great Grandmother, Sister." She was interred the next day, November 29, in
section Z6, row 1, grave 58. The family keeps her yahrtzeit on 26 Cheshvan; the stone's
Hebrew date reads the 27th. Both are kept here, side by side, as the family keeps them.

The Zelkinds of Babruysk
Mordukh and Elka and their four daughters

Mordechai Zelkind - Mordukh - was born in 1912; Elka Kogen, his wife, was born November
12, 1913, in Babruysk, on the Berezina in what is now Belarus. A Social Security index
names her parents as Isac Kaga and Tsilya Gershon. How Mordukh and Elka survived the
war, and when they came to America, the record does not say.

What it tells us in full is the family they raised. Mordukh's death notice in the Plain Dealer
of September 25, 1996 reads: "MORDUKH ZELKIND, beloved husband of Elka (nee Kogen),
devoted father of Bertha Warshawsky, Gail Zelkind, Maria Komisarchik (Isaak) and Celya
Elin (Matvey), loving grandfather of Joseph Warshawsky, Dimitry and Kira Komisarchik,
Inna and Boris Elin, great grandfather of seven... Interment Zion Memorial Park."

Every name in that notice is a thread. Bertha Warshawsky is Berta - Grammy - Joe's mother;
the Warshawsky spelling is how the paper heard the name. Gail, Maria and Celya are Berta's
sisters. And "grandson Joseph Warshawsky" is Joe himself, named in his grandfather's notice
twenty-one years before his own stone would stand in the same cemetery.

Mordukh was interred on September 25, 1996, in section Z6, row 6, grave 110 - beside
Szyman, his son-in-law, at 6-6-111. Elka followed him on March 14, 2005, and was interred
March 16, in Z6, row 4. Her stone reads 1914 - 2005; the Social Security index gives her
birth as November 12, 1913. The year stands as the stone has it, with the index noted
alongside - a single year's disagreement in a life of ninety-one.

Gail Zelkind, Berta's sister, was born July 25, 1942 and died February 28, 2013. Her stone
carries her portrait, a young woman smiling out of black granite - the only face of her the
record holds.

ZEIDY LARRY




Zeidy Larry
Arie Lawrence Hersh 1925–2012

Larry Hersh’s 1984 oral history places his birth in Khust, Czechoslovakia, in June 1925. A
later compiled tribute gives 1 June. In the family he was Aryeh Leib, Zeidy Larry, a survivor
whose childhood world lay in Subcarpathia. His naturalization card preserves the earlier
name as Arie–Larry–Hersh Kovitz and his chosen American signature as Arie Lawrence
Hersh.

He and his sister Judith survived the Holocaust. Family memory speaks of seven children;
Larry’s testimony says that he had four sisters and was the only boy, five children in all.
These are competing accounts of the family’s size. Judith Dratler’s 2025 obituary identifies
her maiden surname as Herskowitz and remembers her parents and siblings who perished.

Judith, called Judy, died on 5 June 2025, aged ninety-six. Her obituary names her late
husband Phillip Dratler, her daughter Leigh Wasserman and son Jay, and subsequent
generations. She was buried at Bet Olam Cemetery in Beachwood after a private funeral.
The obituary links the surviving Cleveland family to the losses Larry described, without
supplying every missing name.

Larry’s own account dates his arrival in Cleveland to 1956, after Israel and the 1956 war. An
earlier index-based estimate of about 1954 is retained as a superseded inference, not the
narrative date. His 1961 naturalization card records him as a subject of Israel; the oral
history supplies the more extensive postwar journey.

In October 1972 the Cleveland Jewish News identified him as a Jewish Survival Legion
leader and national board member and as the former local coordinator of the Jewish
Defense League. The family remembered him at home as Zeidy Larry. His later public
writings show a continued concern with Jewish security and Holocaust remembrance.

The family’s compiled record gives his death as 3 January 2012. Zion Memorial Park records
interment on that date at Z3-1-35, beside Ileen at Z3-1-34. The exact death day is not
independently established by the interment entry alone.

Larry tells his childhood and war
The oral history recorded on 13 August 1984

The following account summarizes Larry’s 1984 oral history, interviewed by Peggy Nathan
for the National Council of Jewish Women, Cleveland Section, and preserved by the
USHMM. The supplied transcript warns of transcription errors. Scenes, numbers and
dialogue below are attributed recollections rather than independent verification of each
event.

He was born in Khust, Czechoslovakia - he spells it C-H-U-S-T - a town of thirty-five or forty
thousand, eight or ten thousand of them Jews, in June 1925. His was, he says, an ideal
childhood: an affluent home - his father dealt in lumber and wine - cheder, then Czech
elementary school, then the Hungarian gymnasium, until the Jewish students were put out;
summers in the Carpathians; Betar, where he rose to lead the region's youth. He read Gone
with the Wind, he liked to say, in four languages. In 1938 he won a fully paid place at a
Prague school for talented youngsters - and his paternal grandmother went to the rabbi:
he'll eat treyf, there'll be no Shabbos, no tefillin. His father canceled the ticket.

In late 1942 the Zionist organization chose him for one of Khust's four Palestine certificates.
His mother objected - it would look like the big macher was taking for himself - and his
younger sister refused it too: she wanted the family together. He gave it away. "The paradox
of it," he says, "is the ones who left, they all survived."

Then the ghetto. He organized the food wagons on the move in; the Judenrat man called
him by his Jewish name, Leibe; the Gestapo chief interrogated him personally. Warned that
his arrest was coming, his father bought him a way out - an army call-up which the
transcript associates with a commander rendered “Kelety”. In the labor service at
Nagybanya he organized a hard core of eight men and smuggled a wagon of bread, hidden
under hay, to the starving ghetto; the general caught him and looked the other way - "next
time don't be a dumb Jew and let yourself be caught" - and later praised the job well done.

His friend Leonard Fox, who lived to see Cleveland, took forty-five lashes on a later run.
Larry escaped over the fields, came back to the ghetto at night, and was deported with its
liquidation in the morning.




Auschwitz and Warsaw
Larry’s testimony continued

Five days in a freight car, eighty to a hundred to a car, water and nothing else, and then
Auschwitz, two days before Shavuot - the testimony’s 1943 references conflict with the
established 1944 ghetto and deportation chronology. Mengele conducted the selection;
Larry said he was seventeen, and was sent right. The transcript renders his prisoner number
as 8485 in one passage and 845 in another. Learning that his father and an uncle had been
sent to Warsaw, he swapped blocks to follow them - to the camp built on the ruins of the
ghetto, where prisoners cleaned bricks from bombed buildings and loaded trains for the
Russian front. He found his father after six weeks. He translated for a guard the words
written in blood on the Anielewicz bunker wall: we were betrayed, remember us. Assigned
to clean the killer-dog cages, he fed his father, his uncle and his friends for weeks on the
kitchen food the work earned. He saw the SS burn bound prisoners stacked, he said, like
wood - "the horror of being stripped of the ability to even scream at one's own pain."

The sequence is preserved as Larry recalled it. The accompanying original interview and its
transcripts allow the family to return to his words. The transcript’s year and
prisoner-number conflicts remain unresolved; those details should not be copied into a civil
chronology without qualification.




From the camps to Cleveland
Larry’s testimony continued

In August 1944 the camp dissolved ahead of the Russian advance. Some ten thousand were
marched out - two days standing without food or water, then the salt-herring ration, and
prisoners who stooped to drink had their necks crushed. Fewer than eight thousand reached



THE WARSZAWSKIS                                                                              18
ZEIDY LARRY




Kutno; about two thousand survived the cattle train to Dachau. His father and uncle made
it. From Dachau to Landsberg, Lager 7 - underground earth huts, typhus quarantine - and
then Kaufering, Lager 4, night construction for the underground airfields. There his father,
forty-eight and broken, was taken away with the older generation, and was never seen
again.

In the early spring of 1945 Larry lay twenty-eight days with typhus, unfed, untreated - an
SS guard held a rifle to his head, and a second guard said leave him, he's finished anyhow;
save the bullet. In May the evacuation train was strafed - the US Air Force disabled the
locomotive - and he and two others, the last three of his eight-man hard core, escaped
through the forest to American-occupied Schwabhausen. He weighed thirty-eight kilos.

The after-war had its own roads. In the hospital, a Jewish US Army doctor traced his family
by telephone from Cleveland - the message ran that Leibi Hersch, son of the sister Leah, was
alive: so his mother's name is known. In Prague he learned that his next-to-youngest sister
had survived, liberated at Bergen-Belsen. In Budapest he was recruited by Joel Brand - Larry
had seen Eichmann there in 1943 without knowing who he was - and he ran the Bricha
borders from 1945 to 1947, sailed for Palestine, was hunted by the British, was smuggled
out to Cyprus hidden among the oranges, and returned to Israel in 1948 on the Altalena.
Eight years in the Israeli army carried him through the 1956 war.

In 1956 he came to Cleveland, to that sister - she had been there since 1947, taken in by
their uncle Janowitz "like one of their own daughters." He met his wife in Cleveland, and
the rest of his story is this book's: Ileen, three daughters, the Marmarosher temple on
Lancashire - one record says B'nai Jeshurun, and both are kept - and the grandchildren who
called him Zeidy Larry.




Bread for the ghetto
Two versions of an act of resistance

A second published account attributes a closely matching story to “Larry Hirsch.” Adam
Reviczky’s book places this speaker in Huszt, born in 1925, speaking several languages, and
reaching Nagybanya with a summons procured by his father. These unusual shared details




THE WARSZAWSKIS                                                                            19
ZEIDY LARRY




make the identity match strong. They do not turn the two tellings into independent
witnesses.

In the 1984 oral account, bread was hidden under hay and the commander gave quiet
approval after the fact. The later book describes an audience first: Larry had collected bread
from labor servicemen’s rations, and Lieutenant Colonel Reviczky instructed the men to take
the bread by horse-drawn transport before it dried out. The book describes an arrest by
gendarmes and difficulty securing release.

The later version names Reviczky explicitly, while the oral transcript renders the
commander’s name as Kelety. The later account puts the summons and flight to Nagybanya
in April 1944. It describes hiding with Christian acquaintances and travel into the Great
Plain before deportation. The oral account instead describes a return to the ghetto after a
failed bread run and deportation the next morning.

These differences matter because an apparently seamless story would erase what the
sources actually say. The two accounts agree on a young multilingual man, a father’s effort
to save him, bread deliveries, danger and subsequent deportation. Their precise sequence
and the commander’s name remain questions for further record and audio work.

The text examined was the publisher’s OCR of the 2008 Múlt és Jövő edition, printed pages
448–450. The original page images were not available for visual verification. The chapter
therefore paraphrases cautiously and supplies no purported exact Hungarian transcription.

The Altalena in Larry’s memory
The 1984 testimony and two newspaper accounts

Larry’s 1984 oral history says that he returned to Israel on the Altalena. Later Cleveland
Jewish News stories add a memorable episode: he swam ashore at Kfar Vitkin with a
waterproof pouch for Menachem Begin. Ellen Harris published a version in 2003; Cynthia
Dettelbach published another in 2008. Both are accounts attributed to Larry, not
independent corroboration by two passengers.



THE WARSZAWSKIS                                                                              20
ZEIDY LARRY




The 2008 account describes recruitment to move arms from Czechoslovakia through the
American zone of Germany, a journey to Marseille at the end of April 1948, and a day of
loading. Larry remembered the Israeli flag, Hatikvah and tears. He described himself and
four other swimmers carrying a pouch ashore and Begin welcoming him. In this telling he
never learned what the pouch contained.

The 2003 version calls the contents documents supplied by a man rendered “Stovsky” and
links his arrival closely to the Latrun fighting. The 2008 version says that the group was
detained at Kfar Vitkin for forty-eight hours before entering the unified fighting force. The
book preserves both versions without choosing a hidden pouch content or harmonizing the
sequence.

The newspaper chronology contains errors. The 2008 article prints “June 1, 1948” and calls
it Friday; 1 June was a Tuesday, while 11 June was a Friday. Palmach history and the later
James T. Williams voyage account give 11 June as the departure. The newspaper dates the
burning to 21 June; the historical accounts give 22 June. A dropped digit may explain one
error, but that explanation remains an inference.

Reported ages also disagree with a 1925 birth: twenty in the 2008 voyage account and
eighty in the 2003 article. Passenger totals vary between the accounts and may refer to
different groups. These figures do not generate new birth dates or a settled passenger count.
The 2003 photograph is captioned Zev Harel and must not be used as a portrait of Larry.




A voice for remembrance
Larry Hersh in the Cleveland press

On 26 May 2000, the Cleveland Jewish News printed a letter signed Larry Hersh of
Cleveland under the heading “Let’s try again for center.” The writer said that he had
proposed a Cleveland Holocaust Center twenty-five years earlier, that the Jewish
Community Federation had supported a committee and allocated funds, and that
disagreements among survivors had brought the effort to a halt. He urged another attempt.

The letter is evidence of an appeal for a permanent place of Holocaust memory. It is not
independent evidence that the writer founded a center, nor verification of his account of
committee disputes. The name, place and concerns fit Larry’s public biography, although the
letter supplies no family identifiers. Its implied beginning around 1975 is arithmetic from
his statement.

A later letter, “Learn from the past,” published on 3 December 2010, was signed Larry Hersh
of Cleveland Heights. It urged American Jews to learn from history and expressed views
about Israel. These writings belong in the family record as a public voice: opinions stated in
print, carefully distinguished from neutral historical findings.

ILEEN AND THE GARSONS




Ileen called Gabby
Shaina Yuda 1933–2009

Ileen Garson was born in Cleveland on July 30, 1933, the youngest of Victor and Pearl
Garson's three daughters, and grew up on the east side in the years when the streetcars still
ran. Her Hebrew name was Shaina Yuda; to her grandchildren she was Gabby. She married
Larry Hersh - Zeidy Larry - and together they raised their family in Cleveland.

Her obituary in the Plain Dealer of May 23, 2009 keeps the whole constellation in one
paragraph: "beloved wife of Lawrence; devoted mother of Suzanne Greenberg, Seth, Laurel
Warszawski, Joseph and Marci Hersh; cherished grandmother of Ilana, Aliya, Shoshana,
Yechiel, Moriah, Shymon, Chaya, Simcha and Hadassah Warszawski, Koby and Dylan
Greenberg; dear sister of the following deceased: Rochelle Horwich and Rosamond Simon."

Laurel Warszawski is Lori - Joe's wife. The nine Warszawski grandchildren named in that
notice are Joe and Lori's children, Gabby's grandchildren, each one counted. Her sisters,
Rochelle and Rosamond, had gone before her.

Gabby died on May 22, 2009 - 28 Iyar 5769, the date the family keeps as her yahrtzeit - and
was interred on May 24 at Zion Memorial Park, plot Z3-1-34. When Larry followed in 2012,
the cemetery's register placed him in the next grave over. The two plots, Z3-1-34 and
Z3-1-35, are the whole story of a marriage, told in the cemetery's own shorthand.




The Garsons of Paris
Victor Garson 1895–1978

Victor reported birth in Paris on May 30, 1895. His 1916 marriage names his parents as
Solomon and Rachael, without establishing either parent’s surname. According to his
naturalization record, he entered the United States at New York in 1902, a boy of seven. His
travelling companions have not been proved by a passenger manifest.

ILEEN AND THE GARSONS




He grew up in Cleveland, became a bookkeeper, and on September 12, 1916 married Pearl
Adelstein in Cuyahoga County. Three years later, on September 18, 1919, he stood in the
U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio and swore his Declaration of Intention -
the first formal step toward citizenship. The clerk recorded Paris, May 30, 1895; occupation
bookkeeper; last foreign residence “Pairs, France,” the clerk’s misspelling; and departure
from Havre. He was twenty-four, three years married, a father of four-month-old Rochelle.

The full 1924 packet now completes that paper trail. Filed as Victor Gershanovitz, petition
19277 records a formal change to Victor Garson when the court admitted him on November
20, 1924. Certificate 2116415 was issued on November 26. Pearl’s later petition gives the
same naturalization date and certificate number. The recovered pages appear in the
new-records section beginning on page 205.

Ohio’s government-derived death index records his death at East Cleveland on August 13,
1978, five years after Pearl. The original death certificate has not been inspected. His
newspaper notice announces the Monday, August 14 funeral at the Berkowitz-Kumin
Memorial Chapel; the cemetery register gives that same date for interment at Zion
Memorial Park. The family sat shiva at the Sanford Simons’. The notice’s feminine forms
“grandmother” and “great grandmother” are preserved as printed.

An early transcription of the declaration gave his last foreign residence as Metz, in
Alsace-Lorraine, and for a while the search ran to Metz. A magnified reading of the original
settled it: the word is “Pairs,” Paris. The new surname evidence opens a better search, while

An American twice over
Pearl Adelstein Garson 1897–1973

Pearl Adelstein was born in Cleveland on December 1, 1897 - American from her first
breath, in the city central to her later family record. Family memory had placed her birth in
France, or in Latvia; her own hand settles it. On January 25, 1940, swearing her Petition for
Naturalization, she wrote: "born in Cleveland, Ohio on Dec. 1, 1897. My race is Hebrew."

That an American-born woman should have to petition for citizenship at all is the story.
When Pearl married Victor Garson on September 12, 1916, he was a French national - and
under the Expatriation Act of 1907, an American woman's citizenship followed her
husband's. By saying yes to Victor, Pearl lost the country of her birth. The Cable Act of 1922
opened the way back, and in 1940 she took it, swearing "I have not acquired any other
nationality by affirmative act." Her petition was granted; certificate 73676. Cleveland-born,
made foreign by marriage, American again by her own signature.

The petition is a family document in the fullest sense. It lists her three daughters with their
exact dates, all born in Cleveland: Rochelle, May 9, 1919; Rosamond, January 6, 1926;
Ileen, July 30, 1933. Ileen is Gabby. On one sworn page, Pearl gives us her birth, her
marriage, her husband's crossing and naturalization, and the birthdays of all three girls.

The compiled record gives Pearl’s death at Cleveland Heights on 3 March 1973; her
newspaper notice itself gives the 4 March service, not a death date. Her obituary names her
daughters - Rosamond Simon, Ileen Hersh and Rochelle Horwich - and through Ileen the
line runs on: to Lori, to Joe's house in Cleveland, and to the nine grandchildren named in
Gabby's own notice thirty-six years later. Her funeral was held at the Berkowitz-Kumin
Memorial Chapel on Sunday, March 4, 1973, at two o'clock, with interment at Zion
Memorial Park, and the notice asked, Please omit flowers. The family sat shiva at the home
of Mr. and Mrs. Sanford Simon - Rosamond and her husband, identified by the notice itself.

Pearl’s 1973 notice also names siblings: Harry Allen; Sydney Allen of Los Angeles; Edward,
Louis and Howard Allen of Florida; and the late Rose Karr. These named siblings broaden
the Adelstein family beyond the three daughters through whom the earlier narrative
followed her. The notice does not itself explain when or why the brothers used Allen.

A postwar childhood
Joe Warszawski born 19 October 1959

Joe Warszawski - Joseph Sam, Yosef Yitzchak ben Shimon - was born on October 19, 1959,
in Poland, into the after-silence of the war. The family remembers the country but not the
city; the record has not yet supplied it. His father had been born in Warsaw and lived
through the war in Russia; his mother had fled from somewhere she would only ever
describe as the place she fled from. Three years after Joe was born, the family carried him to
Cleveland, and Poland became the old country.

He grew up on the east side. A notice in the Cleveland Jewish News of October 13, 1972
announced his bar mitzvah for the next morning at Sinai Synagogue - a boy of the Heights,
stepping up to the Torah the week he turned thirteen. He went on to Cleveland Heights
High School, class of 1978, where the school's memorial page today keeps his name and his
dates.

Of those years the family keeps the texture rather than the chronology: a household with
one child at its center, Szyman at the tailoring shop at Cedar-Center, Berta - Bella - at the
heart of the home.




Joe and Lori
Laurel Ruth Hersh and Joseph Sam Warszawski

The Cleveland Jewish News of 1986 carried the announcement: the engagement of Laurel
Ruth Hersh to Joseph Warszawski.

Laurel Ruth - Lori, Leah Rochel - was Gabby and Larry's daughter, an Ursuline College
graduate working as a registered nurse at Rainbow Babies and Children's Hospital. Joe was
attending Cleveland State University and working as a technical adviser at ProSearch of
Medina. The notice joined two Cleveland survivor families - Hersh of Subcarpathia by way
of Auschwitz and Israel, Warszawski of Warsaw by way of Russia - into one household.

Where the proven Garson line stops
A family surname and several distinct branches

Victor’s marriage names his parents as Solomon and Rachael. Their surnames are not
established; his American name Garson is not projected backward onto them. His own 1924
packet names Gershanovitz but does not name his parents. The French households that
follow remain separate research families, with no proved bridge to Victor.


An early reading of Victor’s last foreign residence as Metz was corrected to the clerk’s
“Pairs,” meaning Paris. The Metz marriage of Aron Garson and Caroline Levy in 1865
remains a lead in its own right, not evidence that Victor’s father Solomon was their son. A
shared surname and a plausible chronology are insufficient.

Paris civil acts uncovered Julia’s children, Aline Garson, Louise’s son Gustave and the
household of Charles Alphonse Garson. Tigery and neighboring communes yielded another
extensive Garson or Garcon network. These records have genuine historical value even
while their bridge to Victor remains missing.

This section prints the people and acts as research families, with the missing bridges stated
on each relevant page. Superseded spelling guesses and table errors have been replaced by
the later documented readings. A bounded unsuccessful search is described as a search
result, not proof that an event never happened.




The Metz question
Aron Garson and Caroline Levy

Where did the Garsons come from before Paris? The family's oldest documentable footprint
is Victor's - Paris, 1895. Behind that, the trail thins to a surname and a region.

In Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine, the civil register records a marriage on September 1, 1865:

Julia and her children
A Paris household not yet linked to Victor

The registers of Paris hold three more Garsons, and they hold them close. On November 4,
1895 - five months after Victor's birth across the same city - a young domestic named Julia
Maria Garson, eighteen years old, bore a son, Marcel Eugene Jules, at a midwife's home in

stood before a second officer to recognize the boy as hers - act N° 3451, an acte de
reconnaissance, a young mother making sure the law knew her son was her son. That act
gives her name in full: Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson.

In January 1897 the registers record a second son: Leon Jules, born on the seventeenth in
the 5th arrondissement, to a mother the clerk wrote down as Melina Perpetue Garson,
twenty, a domestic - a closely matching name and address supporting the identification,
though the name forms differ. And in March 1898, back in the 18th, a daughter: Charlotte,
born to Julia Garson, now twenty-two, at another midwife's rooms. Again, no father is
named. Three children in four years - Marcel, Leon, Charlotte - each carried into the register
by their mother alone.

The ages and uncommon name elements support treating the acts as the record of one
woman, Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson, born about 1877. Her relationship, if any, to
Solomon, Victor’s father, is unproved. The children must not be called Victor’s
cousins unless the missing parentage is established.

Leon's act carries the fullest afterlife. In its margin, two later hands recorded a whole French
lifetime: married to Marie Marthe Therese Rollet at Epineuil, in the Cher, on August 21,
1920; died at Lagny-sur-Marne on August 31, 1980 - eighty-three years, from a midwife's
rooms in Paris to a marriage in the countryside and a long life past the wars. The Rollet line
may have descendants living today. Marcel's margin is briefer: a death recorded on
February 21, 1963. What became of Charlotte, the registers have not yet said.

The Paris households
Documented separately and not merged

The supplied Paris survey of birth tables for 1893–1902 identified several distinct Garson
households. Its findings are bounded by the tables, dates and spellings examined; absence
from that survey is not proof that another household did not exist.

Julia's is the first: the unwed domestic whose three children fill the pages before this one.
The second is Louise Garson, a rag-picker three years Julia's senior, also unwed, who
registered her son Gustave in the 5th arrondissement in May 1901 - two women of the same
rare name, alone in the same city, a few years and a few streets apart; no sister relationship
has been established. The third is a wine merchant's family: Charles Alphonse Garson and
Juliette Honorine Boclin, whose son Marcel Alphonse was born in the 4th in September
1902. And the fourth a shoemaker's: Marius Gabriel Garson and Marie Guden, whose
daughter Aline was born in the 6th in October 1894 - and whose margin, written twenty-six
years later, records her marriage to Edouard Adolphe Godenne in 1920.

Four doors, one surname. Which of them opens onto Solomon and Rachael - onto Victor -
the acts so far do not say; none of them names him. But the sweep that was meant to find
one boy's birth certificate found a small Paris of Garsons instead, and every door is now on
the map.

A fifth household is documented by Armandine Garson’s 1893 birth act: Auguste Garson
and Francoise Armandine Goujot. The proposed identification of her father with Auguste,
born on 15 August 1864 to Antoine Joseph and Caroline Valentine, is an age-matched
inference. That bridge remains qualified, even though earlier compiled data placed the acts
under one person.

The uncle in Cleveland

Larry’s 1984 oral history gives one of the most personal bridges between Europe and
Cleveland. After liberation, a Jewish U.S. Army doctor located Larry’s maternal uncle
through a telephone directory. The message identified the survivor as Leibi Hersch, son of

The examined passage gives the surname Janowitz but no first name. A 1939 notice for

siblings and their households have not been linked to Leah.

not include all the Heights communities. Separate 1955 and 1956 Heights volumes were
not reached in this research. Failure to find the uncle in a main-volume search is not proof
that he was absent from Cleveland.

The decisive next evidence would be a civil or immigration record identifying Leah’s maiden
name and brother, or the original sponsorship and arrival file. Until then, the uncle remains
a real person in Larry’s testimony whose full identity is unknown. The candidate households
are not connected to the core family tree.

A wartime inquiry from New York

A three-page U.S. diplomatic packet preserves a First World War welfare inquiry about a

family event in the right region, with no demonstrated connecting generation. Its value will
increase if a civil record later identifies Larry’s paternal grandparents or other relatives.

What the new pages add
A guide to the discoveries in this edition


The documents gathered here add names and relationships to the Cleveland story. They
also show where an attractive lead still falls short of a family connection. The original pages
are reproduced beside their readings so that the evidence can be revisited.

Victor’s earlier surname
His 1924 petition was filed as Victor Gershanovitz. The court admitted him and formally
changed his name to Victor Garson on 20 November 1924; the certificate was issued six
days later. The earlier 1919 declaration and the final naturalization are now joined by the
complete packet.

Pearl’s parents and sister
The marriage application and delayed birth certificate name Sam and Rebecca Adelstein.
On the supporting affidavit, Rose Alliance identifies herself plainly as Pearl’s sister. Rose’s
1906 marriage names their mother as Rebecca Escowitch. Together, the records provide
strong evidence for that maiden-name reading, while other forms continue to use Adelstein.

The Allen brothers in the house
The original 1920 census places Harry and Louis Allen with Victor, Pearl and baby Rachelle,
each recorded as Victor’s brother-in-law. This is a contemporary household link behind the
Allen names in Pearl’s later notice.

Where the questions remain
The Paris birth act, the Ryndam passengers and a New York census lead remain candidates.
The historical Edelstein records establish a separate research trail, with no shared ancestor
yet proved. None of those proposed connections has been added to the confirmed family
tree.

Victor and Pearl marry
Cuyahoga County, 12 September 1916




Application 111752, the bottom entry on page 188. The license was issued on 11 September; the return
records the marriage on 12 September 1916. The three unrelated applications above it are omitted here.



The original gives Victor’s father as Solomon and his mother as Rachael, followed by “l-n-u,”
apparently last name unknown. It is an explicit limit in the record: her maiden surname has
not been recovered.

Pearl’s father is written as Sam. Her mother’s entry reads Rebecca Adelstein, even though
the printed line asks for the mother’s maiden name. Rose’s 1906 marriage provides the
stronger maiden-name reading Escowitch; the sister affidavit supplies the link to Pearl. The
differing field entries remain visible in this edition.

Pearl is recorded as nineteen. The birth date she later swore to, 1 December 1897, would
make her eighteen at this September 1916 marriage. Both readings are retained rather than
silently adjusted.

Rebecca’s maiden name
Rose’s original marriage supplies the missing field




Rose Adelstein and Sam Alliance, 16 January 1906. Application 45321, page 331. The bride’s father is Sam
Adelstein; the mother’s explicitly designated maiden name reads Rebecca Escowitch. Source W26.


This record directly concerns Rose. The bridge to Pearl is the original 1940 affidavit in
which Mrs Rose Alliance identifies herself as Pearl’s sister. With Sam and Rebecca named
across the records, the combined evidence strongly supports Escowitch as Pearl’s mother’s
maiden name.

Escovitch is an alternate transcription. Pearl’s 1916 marriage and 1940 birth certificate
instead write Rebecca Adelstein; Edward’s 1952 marriage repeats that form. Those entries
have not been erased or silently made to agree. Escowitch is the strongest available reading,
not a settled spelling for every earlier generation.

The original gives Rose as twenty, born in Russia and working as a clerk. The document names her mother; it
does not identify Rebecca’s parents or a precise birth town.

Adelstein and Edelstein
The connection question remains open


The family’s question about Shoshy’s school friend Shifra led to a historical Edelstein
household in Cleveland. Identification of the school friend remains tentative. A public 2012
family announcement is used here only as a dated source for names; it does not establish
anyone’s present relationship status.

The historical household
That announcement names Joseph and Rochelle Edelstein and the late Kayla and Marcus
Edelstein. Marcus’s obituary index names Joseph among his children and Karola as his wife.
The Kayla/Karola identification is strongly supported by the surrounding record trail, but it
should remain qualified until an explicit original naming bridge is obtained.

Karola Faige Edelstein’s Social Security index gives the alias Faigenbaum, birth on 6 April
1921 in Kurow, Poland, and parents Josef Faigenbaum and Shifra Friedman. Ohio’s death
index instead gives 4 June 1921, a month-and-day disagreement retained here. Phillip
Edelstein’s index names Marcus and Karola as parents and a 1947 birth at “Helsenberg,”
Sweden, the index’s own spelling.

What would establish a connection
Pearl’s records place Sam and Rebecca’s daughter in Cleveland by 1897. The Edelstein
sources point toward Poland and postwar Sweden. Neither spelling nor broad geography
supplies a common ancestor. Marcus’s parents and Sam and Rebecca’s precise origins are
the missing connecting evidence.

Sources W11-W16. Survivor-list and published-testimony leads remain separately identified in the source
notes; their full originals have not all been examined.

For the next family edition

This book is meant to be read, corrected and kept. A second name, a conflicting date, a
missing brother, a child recognized in a later act: uncertainty can be part of the family
history. A new discovery should be able to change the story without destroying the evidence
that came before it.

The archive keeps the earlier books, the complete corrected family record, original source
files, the editable text and the reproducible design. The connected trees are a way into that
record. They are not a claim that every question has been settled.

Please keep this edition and its source package within the intended family audience. Obtain any necessary
author, archive, photographer and publisher permissions before wider distribution. In particular, JEM
previews retain their watermarks and have not been cleared for a public print edition.
