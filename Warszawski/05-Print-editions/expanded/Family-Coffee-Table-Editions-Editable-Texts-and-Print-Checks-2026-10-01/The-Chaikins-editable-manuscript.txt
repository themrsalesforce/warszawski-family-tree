The story and the record

This is the story of Shmuel Chaikin’s family: the Chaikins, Kahanovs and Lipskers; the Bachers and Ruchs of Lithuania and South Africa; and the Goldman line. It follows lives through Snovsk, Georgia, postwar Europe, Montreal, New York and Johannesburg, keeping the letters and photographs close to the people they recall.

The earlier family manuscript forms the narrative foundation. Later research has checked dates and names, recovered clearer versions of original pictures and records, and extended the family connections. The companion volume, The Warszawskis, follows Shoshy’s family.

Family memories are kept as memories. Contemporary documents can also disagree. An uncertain link stays uncertain, and an unknown date stays unknown. The wider historical Chaikin branches provide context; the missing generational bridge to the Snovsk household is not treated as proved.

The family connections section offers a way through the generations. The alphabetical register preserves the fuller record, including unresolved identities and historical context people. Detailed references and the questions still open are gathered at the back.

Stories and original records



The rav of Snovsk



Meir Chaim Chaikin lived three lives - the yeshiva bochur of Snovsk and Nevel, the underground rav of Soviet Georgia, and the dean of Montreal - and he lived all three the same way: learning, ruling, and refusing to let go.



Section-opening image caption: Rabbi Meir Chaim Chaikin, 1907–1965. Family portrait.



The star pupil of the Chofetz Chaim

Meir Chaim Chaikin, 1907 - 1930



Meir Chaim Chaikin was born on 13 Shevat 5667 - January 28, 1907 - in Naptonitsh, a hamlet near Snovsk in Chernigov province, the son of Ezriel ben Aryeh Leib Chaikin, a wheat merchant, and Sarah, a daughter of Reb Zeev Wolf Raskin of Homel. A printed directory of 1965 would later give his birthday as October 15; the family's sefer gives 13 Shevat, and the sefer is followed here.



The Chaikins of Snovsk were kin to Reb Yoel Chaikin of Padobranka and to Reb Zalman Chaikin, whose daughter married into the Schneersons - the family of the Rebbe. The Revolution took the wheat business and sent Ezriel to forced labor; he died on 3 Kislev 5684, when Meir Chaim was sixteen.



The boy had already been noticed. He learned in Snovsk, in the yeshiva that the Chofetz Chaim's displaced students carried with them, and the Chofetz Chaim himself tested the boy and blessed him. From there to Tomchei Tmimim in Nevel, and ordination at twenty-one - among his signatories, Reb Refael Cohen of Germanovitch. In 1927 he stood in yechidus with the Rebbe Rayatz in Leningrad. In 1929, still single, he was already the rav of Surazh, in the Vitebsk province.



The Yevsektsia noticed him too. There was a plot, and an escape across a frozen river - the kind of story the family tells in one sentence because one sentence is all that survived. And there was a wedding: on 12 Tammuz 5690 - July 8, 1930 - in Kutaisi, Georgia, he married Sarah Chana Kahanov, whose father had been sent to the Caucasus by the Rashab a generation before.

The name Chaikin

The deep lineage behind the Snovsk stock



The Chaikin name runs deeper and wider than Snovsk. A printed family tree of the name, kept on the movement's encyclopedia - with its own caveat that it sketches a general picture rather than fixing facts - climbs from Reb Chaim Chaikel through Reb Moshe of Kobrin, son-in-law of Chaim Chaikel and brother of Reb Aharon of Karlin, down through Rav Yoel and Rav Tzvi Hirsh to a generation of seven sons, and it ends one step from the Rebbe's own house: Zelda Rachel of this tree was the Rebbe's great-grandmother, and Reb Shneur Zalman Chaikin of Podobranka, of whom the next chapter tells, was his great-grandfather, named for the Alter Rebbe himself.



The movement's memory knew this closeness without the paper: Meir Chaim's family in Snovsk were kin to Reb Yoel Chaikin of Padobranka and to Reb Zalman Chaikin, whose daughter married into the Schneersons. Whether the printed tree's root joins the Snovsk stock is this book's standing question - the encyclopedia's whole category of the name holds no entry for an Ezriel - but the name Ezriel runs on both sides of the page, and the chapters that follow give the elder tree its due.



Shneur Zalman of Podobranka

The Rebbe's great-grandfather - the story of the stove



Reb Shneur Zalman Chaikin of Podobranka was a chassid of the Tzemach Tzedek, the Maharash and the Rashab, and a prize student of Reb Pesach of Mlastovka. The Rashab would ask after him of his son-in-law, Reb Baruch Shneur Zalman - "how is my mechutan, Reb Zalman" - and Baruch Shneur told many stories in his father-in-law's name. His brother, Rav Yoel Chaikin, was the rav of Podobranka and the childhood melamed of Reb Levi Yitzchak, the Rebbe's father; his sister Esther married Reb Shimon Zilber. He died on 29 Teves.



One story carries him whole, preserved in Reshimat Devarim from the telling of Reb Shmuel Gronem Esterman by way of Reb Yehuda Chitrik. A rich man of the town - worth some twenty-five thousand rubles - would come and sit with the poor by the stove. When one of them wondered where a meal might be found, the rich man said, go to Zalman Chaikin's house - his own house - and when they got there, he opened the cupboards himself and fed them. The poor men, not knowing him, scolded the arrogant beggar who gave away another man's bread.



The magnate of Kapitonovka

Rav Tzvi Hirsh Chaikin, c. 1784 - 1884



Rav Tzvi Hirsh Chaikin, son of Rav Yoel, was the rav of Kapitonovka and a chassidic magnate - a man who sent large sums to the Maharash. The Maharash's letters to him open, in the manner of kings writing to friends: To my friend, the wondrous rav. He lived to make aliyah at ninety-seven, in 5641, and died in the Holy Land.



His fame in the movement rests on one piece of devotion: he uncovered the fate of Reb Moshe Schneuri - the Tzemach Tzedek's son, lost to the family in the old country's troubles - through his correspondence with the Maharash and with Reb Levi Yitzchak. Where the record was silent, his letters pressed until it answered. From him descend the tree's three branches: Moshe, of the Rabinowitz line; Shmaryahu, of the Schneerson line; and Yoel Meir, of whom the next chapter tells.



Yoel Meir's road to Jerusalem

The court, the estate, the aliyah - and the son of Romema



Reb Yoel Meir Chaikin, born in Mogilev, was a yoshev - a settled elder - at the court of the Tzemach Tzedek. He married Chaya Esther, a daughter of Reb Tzadok HaLevi; her name also comes down as Halevi Zadikov, and both readings are kept. In 5620 he held an estate between Horodishche and Krementchug, farming and a winery. Around 5646 the landlord went bankrupt and the pogrom whisper reached the estate, and the family made aliyah. He farmed near Jaffa, and at the end he was carried to the Chabad section of the Mount of Olives, dying on 5 Av 5658; Chaya Esther followed on 21 Teves 5678. One printed entry would have him born as early as 1804-05; the family's reckoning has placed him nearer 1840, and both stand here. Nine children survived him, among them a London branch - Nachama, the wife of Moshe Blumenfeld, and a son, Hillel.



His son Reb Menachem Mendel Chaikin - born 1868 - had come up to the Land as a boy of thirteen, in 5641, with his grandfather, the old magnate of Kapitonovka. In Hebron he learned under his kinsman Reb Shimon Menashe Chaikin - born in Shklov in 1798, mechutan of Reb Eliyahu Yosef of Dribin, the man whose kever, the anash.org telling says, would not open until a cavern appeared beside it. Menachem Mendel married Chana Zelda Zalznik in 5646, dealt in wine, spent the years 1893 to 1898 in London, and came home to co-found and serve as rav of Romema. A letter from the Rebbe Rayatz to him survives, warm with honorifics. He died on 21 Teves 5708 - 1948 - and his levaya went up the Mount of Olives under Jordanian sniper fire: the last burial there until 1967. His matzeva was erected twenty-two years later.



The attendant of the Tzemach Tzedek

R' Yisrael Chaikin of Nevel, 5601-5608



A hundred years before Meir Chaim took his semicha in Nevel, a Chaikin was already standing in the movement's innermost room. The kuntres the Previous Rebbe published on the Tzemach Tzedek's battle with the Haskalah tells of the Petersburg committee of 5601, and in its fifth chapter a young man steps out of the shadows: "the young R' Yisrael Chaikin - a native of the city of Nevel - distinguished himself in the most clandestine work, with the deputy minister of the interior, Andratov." The quiet channel to the deputy minister ran through him.



When the Tzemach Tzedek was held in Petersburg in the summer of 1843, the order over his rooms admitted no one - "except his attendant Yisrael Chaikin." He was the sole visitor at the door. The great account of that summer's commission - the twenty-two sittings in which the Rebbe defended the Torah before the ministry's scholars - rests, the kuntres says, on the telling of the chassid R' Yisrael Chaikin, who attended his master through all the days of his stay in Petersburg. When the Interior Minister later received the Rebbe with fitting honor, two intercessors walked in with him: Feitelson and Chaikin. And by the fall of 1847 he sat on the Vaad Askanei HaKlal, the general committee of communal affairs, the first to learn that a second rabbinical conference was being planned.



Whether this Chaikin of Nevel belongs to our own line, the record does not say; Snovsk and Nevel are two hundred kilometers apart, and no document bridges them. But the same town holds the family again a century later - it is where Meir Chaim studied at Tomchei Tmimim and took his semicha - and that makes the attendant's story feel less like a stranger's than the paper can prove. It is printed here for what it is: the earliest documented Chaikin in the Chabad record, standing at the hinge of the movement's hardest century.



The Georgia network

The Rayatz's staffing system, 5688-5690



The letters of the Previous Rebbe preserve, almost as office memoranda, how the Jews of Georgia were staffed between the wars - and at the center of the system stood Rabbi Shmuel Levitin, whom the letters style the chief rabbi of Georgia. In Marcheshvan 5688 the Rebbe sent a young tamim, Siman Yakabshvili, home through Kutaisi as shochet-u-vodek - slaughterer and inspector - to strengthen the chadarim, reporting to the rabbis Slavin and Altshuler. It is the exact template of the shlichus that brought Meir Chaim to Georgia in 1930. A letter of Rosh Chodesh Kislev directs a candidate for the Tiflis rabbinate to "our friend Rabbi R' Shmuel Levitin, the chief rabbi of Georgia" - Levitin was the address through which the network was run. By 5689 the same machinery was placing men in Baku, where Meir Chaim would later be arrested; and a placement letter of 5690 remembers Zuber - the same Yaakov Yisroel Zuber who would one day follow the family to Stockholm - for the years he spent in the cities of Georgia, where he worked and accomplished much.



Read together, the letters describe a second wave of the underground: the tmimim the Rebbe planted across Georgia in the late 5680s, who held the chadarim and the shechita through the terror years. That reading is the research archive's hypothesis, and it is labeled as one - no single document says it in so many words. But the template letters are documents, and Meir Chaim's own appointment to Tiflis came from the same hand and the same desk.



Nine years

Tiflis, arrest, and the camps



From 5697 he served as rav of Tiflis - his son Azriel remembers Sachkhere first, then Tbilisi - leading a community that the NKVD wanted not to exist. The brissos were clandestine. The yeshivos were cellars. The arrests came for everyone eventually, and one day they came for him.



How exactly, the record tells three ways, and all three are printed here as they stand. His own telling, decades later on camera, says 1941, for rabbinic work. A 2019 retelling says he was arrested in Baku on the road toward Samarkand, accused of smuggling Georgian tea. A third account, from 2020, says late 1941 or early 1942, on a charge of speculation. The arrest file itself has not yet been found; when it surfaces, one of these tellings will become the story.



What the tellings agree on is the sentence: nine years. And they agree on what went in with him - tefillin, a shofar, matzos smuggled past the guards - and on the bread, dry, that was all he would eat. They agree on the clemency of Kalinin that cut nine years to three. And they agree on the last morning: his release fell on Shabbos, and he would not sign the papers. He stayed two more days in the camp rather than sign, and walked out on Monday, a free man who had not desecrated the day. After the war's end he served as rav of Gori, and waited for the way out.



The secret portrait

The Soviet years, told whole - from the 2019 interviews



In 2019, Rabbi Azriel Chaikin sat for a long series of interviews with Chabad.org's Dovid Margolin and told the Soviet years whole. What follows is his telling, printed beside the images that survived it.



The Rayatz received the young Meir Chaim in Leningrad - an audience the son kept from his father's retelling all his life. Then Surazh, where Meir Chaim served as rabbi and shochet, until the GPU came for him in the night and he escaped by minutes. Decades later, in Israel, a man recognized the old rav: he had been the landlord's son, and he confirmed that the GPU really had come that night - and found the bed empty.



Georgia, from 1930. In July 1930, in Kutaisi, Meir Chaim married Sarah Chana Kahanov; her father, Reb Eliyahu Shmuel, had been sent to Georgia by the Rebbe Rashab himself, in 1918. Azriel was born the following year in Zestafoni, where his grandfather was the shochet - so the 2019 telling; other tellings give Bandza, and both are kept. In Sachkhere, where Meir Chaim succeeded Rabbi Yaakov Yisroel Zuber - the same Zuber whose post he would later take again in Stockholm, which explains the Stockholm appointment - the mothers came home on 'maternity vacation' to give birth and have their sons' brissim, because the towns they worked in could not be trusted with either. In Tbilisi, around 1937, Azriel was hidden from the Soviet school in his grandfather's attic over the mikva. That was my kingdom, he said.



The NKVD came at midnight, as it did to everyone. Their pitch to him survives in the son's verbatim: People already think you're working for us - why don't you just work for us? His answer survives too: I have done nothing wrong. Either you are bluffing, or it is all false.



They arrested him in Baku - smuggling Georgian tea, the charge read. The sentence was nine years; he served three and was freed in the postwar amnesty - so the 2019 telling, while Tiferes Azriel records the sentence commuted by Kalinin. Both are kept, as the record keeps them. In 1946 the family went out on forged Polish papers, through Lvov.



And the portrait. A rare photograph of the Rebbe Rayatz had crossed the Latvian border into the Soviet Union in the mid-1930s, hidden, and lived in the family album through the searches and the camps. In 1946 it crossed out again with them, to be displayed afterward in Europe and in Montreal - a picture that survived two borders and the whole Soviet night. It is printed in this chapter, and the passport its subject once signed follows it.

The exodus

Pesach 5706 - out of the Soviet Union



It came on Pesach 5706, in the spring of 1946, in the brief window when Poland took back its prewar citizens and the Soviets looked the other way. With repatriation papers - Polish papers, obtained as such papers were obtained - Meir Chaim, Sarah Chana, young Azriel and two daughters moved west through Lvov. His brother-in-law, Reb Chaim Osher Kahanov, ran that route twice, going back in to bring others out.



Prague first, where a beis din of refugees answered the questions of the uprooted. Then Antwerp, and a shteibel on the Oostenstraat. And then, in the summer of 1947, the tragedy this book tells in Sarah Chana's own chapter.



From Antwerp he was sent north: the Rebbe Rayatz instructed him to take the rabbinate of Stockholm. There, from about 1947, he led Adas Yisroel - the congregation's own history lists Rav M. Chaikin among its rabbis of the 1940s - and founded Tiferet Bachurim, whose government recognition stretched refugees' residence permits into something livable. He performed brissos across Scandinavia and sat on the agunah cases of a continent full of missing husbands. When he left for America, the Canadian Jewish Review would look back and call him, simply, Chief Rabbi of Stockholm.



Stockholm, the northern rabbinate

The Great Synagogue of Stockholm, the community he led from 1947



The Great Synagogue of Stockholm. Adas Yisroel's own history lists Rav M. Chaikin among its rabbis of the 1940s. Photograph via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.



Stockholm was the family's first home in the West. Tiferet Bachurim gave the refugee bochurim a roof and a legal foothold; the rav gave them a posek and a mohel and, when the war's terrible arithmetic required it, a dayan for the agunos. The Swedes registered everything - and the Swedish archives have so far registered nothing of him, which the family takes as one more lesson in the difference between a record and a life.

The shechita battle he inherited

Stockholm, 5712 - the letter to Reb Zuber



The battlefield Meir Chaim inherited in Stockholm is on paper. His predecessor there was Reb Yaakov Yisroel Zuber - the same Zuber whose post he had already taken once before, in Sachkhere - and in Adar of 5712, four years before Meir Chaim arrived, the Rebbe wrote Zuber the letter that defined the fight: Igros Kodesh, volume 5, letter 1452.



The Stockholm government had moved to require the electric stunning of cattle before shechita. A leniency circulated, backed by rabbis in the Holy Land and by a veterinary professor: stunning by nerve-shock, they argued, did not kill. The Rebbe took the leniency apart. Death by nerve-shock leaves no post-mortem signs, he wrote - the professor's calm laboratory proof proved nothing, because the death it inflicted was invisible to its own instruments. He doubted the great posek whose endorsement traveled with the hetter had been given the accurate facts. And he closed with the instruction that governed the rabbinate Meir Chaim was about to take up: stand by your issur.



Zuber did not stand alone in it. Beside him in the Swedish shechita struggle stood Reb Yisrael Zecharya Mendel Chasdan - paying the government's daily fine rather than stun - the same Chasdan who had helped spirit the Rebbe Rayatz out to Stockholm and received his thank-you letter, who with Zuber arranged some three thousand visas to Japan, and whose family, a generation later, the Johannesburg record calls relatives of the Bachers. His uncle Reb Zalman Chasdan, who raised him, had been in the Rayatz's orbit since the 1920s - the Rebbe's own Purim memoir of 5686 records a pidyon nefesh for him.



Stockholm had already held the Rayatz himself. On 25 Adar I 5700, pausing in the city on his escape route from Riga to New York, he wrote from Stockholm a letter of welcome to a new student of Tomchei Tmimim - printed in Igros HaRayatz, volume 14, letter 261. Eight years later the same city's rabbinate passed to Meir Chaim. The line from the Rayatz's stopover to the family's Stockholm years is unbroken on the paper.



Fifteen years in Montreal

1951 - 1965



The Rebbe's consent came, and in 5711 the family crossed again. The sources disagree on the exact year of arrival - the obituary says 1950, the Russian Jewish Encyclopedia says 1951, and one reckoning starts his tenure in the summer of 1952; all three are kept. What is not in dispute is the fifteen years that followed.



He became the rav of Beis Yisrael V'Shmuel, a member of the Vaad Ha'ir's beis din, and Rosh Yeshivah of Tomchei Tmimim - dean of the Rabbinical College of Canada for fifteen years, a title the Canadian Jewish Review would use in his obituary without needing to explain it. The 1965 Who's Who in Canadian Jewry gives him a share of its page 120 - the Caucasus pulpits, Stockholm, and Montreal from 1951, with his portrait - and that page is printed at the end of this part. In December 1955 the yeshiva ordained a class at the Young Israel Auditorium, and the Chronicle's page prints him first among the staff: Rabbi M. Chaikin, Rosh Yeshivah. A color film of that evening survives, eleven minutes and change, and in it he is exactly where the page puts him.



Parkinson's disease came in his later years. He gave shiurim until his last day. He died at home in Montreal on Thursday, December 9, 1965 - 15 Kislev 5726 - at fifty-eight; one website says 1966, and the family's documents and the contemporary press say 1965. The funeral left from the Rabbinical College, Rabbi L. Kramer officiating, and went on to 770, where the Rebbe took part. He lies in the Chabad society section of Old Montefiore Cemetery, recorded there under the spelling Mayer Chaikin. His widow was Celia; his sons Ezriel, of Copenhagen, and Berl; his daughters Clara, Mrs. Baruch Brickman of New York, and Hinda, Mrs. Nathan Gourarie of Montreal; nine grandchildren were named in the notice, and two of the granddaughters were named Sarah Chana.

The Rebbe's letters to Meir Chaim

Igros Kodesh, 5708 - 5720



Five letters from the Rebbe to Meir Chaim survive in the printed Igros Kodesh, and together they trace the refugee years.



The first three belong to 5708, the Antwerp year. Letter 310 of volume 2, dated 22 MarCheshvan, concerns the Kehot printing and distribution network - the same work the wedding teshura's facsimile preserves in his own hand. Letter 335 is dated in the printed volume to Purim Katan; it matches another teshura page, though the facsimile's own date line reads 10 Shevat, and both dates are kept until the original settles the difference. Letter 400, of 20 Elul, carries the editor's note that an identical copy went to another address - the network again.



The fourth - volume 3, letter 464, 12 Adar 5709 - is the Sweden letter: the mission to Stockholm stated outright. And it carries one sentence that rewrites a page of this family's tree. The Rebbe writes that the message is also for Meir Chaim's father-in-law, identified by the editor as Reb Eliyahu Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov. So he was alive in early 1949, and with or near his daughter's family - presumably in Stockholm - having come out of Russia with the Chaikin-Kahanov group. Where his story ran afterward, the record has not yet said.



The fifth - volume 19, letter 7271, 25 Nissan 5720 - is a bar mitzvah blessing for Berl. It confirms the year, 5720, matching a birth in 5707 - and it settles the 1965 obituary's loose name: Berl was Meir Chaim's son, the future rav of Cleveland, whose chapter comes later in this book.



The women and the uncles



Behind the rav of Snovsk stand two households - the Kahanovs of Pahar and the Lipskers of the southern colonies - and two women who carried the family through the century: Sarah Chana, who did not survive it, and Cyla, who survived everything.



Section-opening image caption: Homel, 1910. A historical map of the Kahanov family’s region.



Sarah Chana of the Kahanovs

1910 - 1947



Sarah Chana was born on November 10, 1910 - the date comes from the Antwerp burial register, which gives her birthplace as Skolow, a name no one has yet resolved: Shklov, perhaps, or Skole. She was the daughter of Reb Eliyahu Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov of Pahar, a talmid of the original Tomchei Tmimim in Lubavitch, and of Chava Reise, a daughter of Reb Asher Margolis, the rav of Rakshik - and through him, a descendant of the Tosfos Yom Tov.



She was nineteen at the chuppah in Kutaisi on July 8, 1930. She made the underground years her own: Tiflis, the arrest, the camps' long shadow, Gori, and then the exodus - Lvov, Prague, Antwerp, with three children, in the spring of 1946.



In Antwerp, in the summer of 1947, she gave birth to her youngest son, Sholom DovBer - Berl to the family. Fifteen days later, on July 30, 1947 - 13 Av - she died. She was thirty-six. The Antwerp burial register holds her as Sara Chaikin-Kohanoff, born 10.11.1910, and the name, the maiden name, the dates and the window all fit; the register and the burial index differ over the plot letter and a day, in the small way of clerks, and the original certificate is still being sought. The inference about what took her - childbirth, two weeks on - is the family's, and it is labeled as such here.



In the 1965 obituary of her husband, two of the nine grandchildren are named Sarah Chana. She was gone eighteen years, and the name was still being handed forward.



The Tosfos Yom Tov's line

Eliyahu Shmuel and Chava Reise Kahanov



Reb Eliyahu Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov of Pahar - son of Reb Chaim HaKohen Kahanov and Chaya Tzirel - was a talmid of the original Yeshivas Tomchei Tmimim in Lubavitch, and the Rebbe Rashab sent him on shlichus to Georgia for the better part of ten years. That is how a Kahanov chuppah came to be held in Kutaisi in 1930: the Caucasus was already the family's second address.



His wife, Chava Reise, was a daughter of Reb Asher Margolis, the rav of Rakshik in Lithuania, a descendant of the Tosfos Yom Tov - Rabbi Yom Tov Lipmann Heller, the Tosafist whose line runs through half the rabbinic families of Lithuania. Her mother was a daughter of the chossid Reb Eli Abaeler. Reb Asher Margolies was also the father-in-law of Reb Shmuel Levitin, which makes the Levitin family kin. Chava Reise died in 1951, at sixty-three. Her maiden name - the name she was born with - the record has not yet supplied.



Their son was Reb Chaim Osher. Their daughter was Sarah Chana. Between those two, the family crossed the century.



One more page of his story surfaced only this year, in the Rebbe's published correspondence. A letter to Meir Chaim of 12 Adar 5709 - printed in Igros Kodesh, volume 3, letter 464 - says its message is also for Meir Chaim's father-in-law, whom the editor names as Reb Eliyahu Shmuel HaKohen Kahanov. So he was alive in early 1949, with or near his daughter's family - presumably in Stockholm - having come out of Russia with the Chaikin-Kahanov group. Where the road took him afterward, the record has not yet said.

The uncle who went back

Chaim Osher Kahanov, 1923 - 2020



Azriel calls him plainly: my mother's brother, Chaim Osher. He was born on June 27, 1923, and he escaped the Soviet Union twice - left Tbilisi around 1945-46 with the same repatriation tide that carried his sister's family, and then went back, into the country he had just escaped, to help his family and others get out. Cyla

Zobina, Schmukler, Chaikin, Ushpol - 1918 - 2006



Her life would one day be told in a book of its own, and it was. But the bones of it belong here too.



She was born in September 1918 - the 27th by her displaced-persons card, the 20th by her JDC card - in Pinsk by both cards, though the published biography of her life says Koidanov. A textile worker, Yiddish her first language. She married Meir Eliezer Schmukler, and the war made her a widow with two boys, Zalmen and Froim; her sons' accounts of their father's death differ - conscription and a German bullet in one telling, a secret-police arrest at the war's start in another - and no record has yet settled it.



On October 22, 1946, the Allies registered her at Pocking as Zobina Cyla, widowed, born in Pinsk, destination Palestine. The Joint's Munich office opened a file the next summer - August 5, 1947, destination USA, closed that November - and the boys' United Nations identity cards from the Pine City assembly center, signed by their mother, are dated February 16, 1948. Instead there was Paris, and the Joint again: a JDC emigration file, case SWE 66, filed April 29, 1949 from an address on the rue Bichat, and a departure list that records the Zobina trio, Paris to Sweden, in the spring of 1949.



In Sweden, or in Paris, or in a DP camp - the three accounts are all in the record, and all are kept - she became the wife of Rabbi Meir Chaim Chaikin, and the mother of his household. The 1965 directory calls her Tcherna, daughter of David Zobina; the 1965 obituary calls her Mrs. Celia Chaikin. She raised his children, and her own two sons with them, and after his death she married Yitzchok Dovber Ushpal. Her brother Hershel - Grisha - survived Siberia, lost a wife and daughter to the Nazis, and reached Canada after a visa campaign that drew in Prime Minister Trudeau. She died on October 23, 2006, at eighty-eight, and lies beside Meir Chaim in the Chabad society section of Old Montefiore, in the same ground as the Rebbe's Ohel.

Michoel Lipsker

1907 - 1985 - Sarah's father



Michoel Lipsker was born in 1907 in a Jewish agricultural colony near Yekaterinoslav - he writes its name in his own notes, in Yiddish letters no one has yet pinned to a map - and the family later moved to Poltava. His parents were Reb Chaim Tzvi and Batsheva; her maiden name comes down three ways - the chart says Jaffe, the memoir Mamyaffo, and Berman appears besides - and all three are kept. His father's death is dated Adar II 5693 in Michoel's own account; another telling puts it earlier, under interrogation. The two are kept apart, as the record keeps them.



In Leningrad, during the siege, he and his brother-in-law Rabbi Baruch Shifrin did the work no one else could do: they gave thousands of Jews a Jewish burial. After the war he was the mashpia of the Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva in Kutaisi - the same Georgia where the Kahanovs had been sent a generation before. In 1946 he escaped west and settled in Paris, and from Paris the Rebbe made him his first shliach to Morocco; the family joined him there in 1950.



His daughter Sarah married Rabbi Azriel Chaikin. His nephew is Rabbi Mendel Lipskar, who led Chabad of South Africa from 1971. His memoir - youth, siege, Morocco - survives, preserved in a wedding teshura. His stone names him Michoel son of Chaim Tzvi Lipsker, gone 5 Iyar 5745. His wife Teibel - Taiba, daughter of Reb Eliyahu Kalman Par and Henya, sister of Sonia Shifrin - followed him in Iyar 5750; her stone names her father, her husband, and nothing else, which is also a kind of summary.

Solly Bacher

Chaim Shaul Zev HaLevi Bacher



Chaim Shaul Zev Bacher, known in Johannesburg as Solly, was a son of Yitzchak Alter Bacher and Chaya-Sarah-Ita (Sorrel) Ruch. His Ruch connection came through his mother. The wider Ruch family of Rokiskis held a remembered place in Chabad history: the Frierdiker Rebbe stayed in Pesach Ruch's house in Adar 5691, February 1931.



Family recollection preserved the story of a farm that Solly wished to sell and of advice to wait. The surviving correspondence gives the dated sequence more precisely. In October 1948 he referred back to earlier advice against selling; by February 1949 he reported that he had sold the farm, on the Rebbe's advice, for fifty thousand pounds. The scan therefore supports the remembered sale amount. It does not independently date the earlier advice to 1946.



He married Chana, whose yahrtzeit the family long kept as around 19-20 Adar, the year uncertain; the Johannesburg burial register now makes it exact - 20 Adar 5754, March 3, 1994 - and she lies at Westpark, E-1072, the plot her son Koppel and Selma later shared with her. Solly himself, the register records, died on 12 Elul 5725 - September 9, 1965 - and lies at E-1044. After his death, his son Koppel offered the free use of the family home in Observatory to a young institution of higher learning, and in thanks it was renamed Kollel Yad Shaul in Solly's memory - dedicated in September 1969, housed in the family home until the beginning of 1980, and carrying his name still. South Africa already appeared in the Rayatz's relief correspondence before the 1931 visit. A letter of 17 Adar I 5689, February 1929, discusses the matzah-for-Soviet-Jewry campaign and seeks the Chief Rabbi's influence in the colonies, with South Africa specifically named. This is evidence of a wider relief network, not proof of an individual Bacher role in that campaign.



And in the Rebbe's published correspondence there is a letter to him - Igros Kodesh, volume 7, letter 1890, dated 4 Mar-Cheshvan 5713, Brooklyn - encouraging a warm connection with the Previous Rebbe, assuring him the blessings remain in full force, and guiding him in business and in spirit. The printed letter is reproduced with this chapter.



The landsmanschaft's memorial volume gives him a fuller portrait than any American record. Even the scope of his large businesses "did not change a hair of his folksy, Jewish style and manner": the businesses were closed on Shabbos and holidays, and he observed Shabbos and the holidays as in his old home. He was known as a philanthropist, but he distinguished himself with his anonymous help - "there are many people who do not know that he helped them." He gave generously to the yeshivos of Jerusalem, and he was one of the pillars of the Johannesburg Chabad. His farm near Germiston was the local place of hakshara for Habonim - the training farm where the young halutzim learned the work they would carry to the land.



Six letters, preserved on eight scanned pages, now bring Solly's business correspondence into view. They run from 8 Tishrei 5709 to 29 Shevat 5709, 11 October 1948 to 28 February 1949. The first reply, dated 24 Tishrei, discusses the proposed partnership and management of the business. Solly returned to the subject on 16 Tevet, 17 January 1949. The partially overwritten date on his letter is corroborated by the reply of 23 Tevet, which expressly answers his letter of the 16th of that month. He wrote that he had bought the farm in 1942 and had put more than thirty-five thousand pounds into it. That figure describes his investment; it should not be treated as an exact purchase price. On 23 Tevet, 24 January 1949, the office confirmed that advice to sell the farm had been sent by telegram.



On 21 Shevat, 20 February, Solly reported a sale for fifty thousand pounds and forty-seven thousand five hundred remaining after brokerage. He described sending two thousand to the Rebbe for ma'amad, using the word shekalim for that amount; he also set aside five hundred pounds for a free-loan fund and gave five hundred pounds to his sister, whose circumstances were difficult. He reported repaying a bank debt and described the strain of running the store. His next question was whether to sell the store while retaining the building for rental income. The final reply, 29 Shevat, agreed that he should sell the store and free himself of debt. It also asked about his children's education and acknowledged his assistance to Rabbi Weinberg. These were replies from the Rebbe's office, conveying his counsel; the surviving pages do not bear his autograph.



The file was supplied as a scan attributed to the Chabad Library. No formal catalogue numbers or institutional authentication accompanied the copy available for this edition. The dated pages nevertheless correct two earlier readings: the final letter is from February 1949, not January 1950, and the reported farm sale was fifty thousand pounds, not three thousand.

The letter read whole

This is one of several letters now identified as addressed to Chaim Shaul Zev Bacher, and it deserves a slow reading. The new shul, Kislev 5724

The dedication - December 1, 1963



In the last days of 1963, Johannesburg's Chassidisher Shul dedicated its new building, and the Rebbe marked it with a letter - Igros Kodesh, volume 23, letter 8387, written Erev Shabbos Vayeitzei 5724 - to the participants in the foundation-stone laying and dedication. The date falls on Sunday, 15 Kislev 5724: December 1, 1963.



The building was more than real estate. The shul's rabbinic chair had stood between tenures since Alter Hillewitz, and it was on that chair that the Rebbe's condition turned: a shliach for Johannesburg needed a congregation's post, and the post now stood ready in a new building. Within the decade Reb Mendel Lipskar held it, as shliach and rav - the beginning of Chabad of South Africa. And five months after the dedication, in the same congregation's world, Koppel Bacher married Selma Goldman.



Fourteen, at 770

Koppel Bacher's yeshiva years, 1955 - 1961



Avrohom Koppel ben Chaim Shaul Zev HaLevi Bacher was born in 5701 - 1940-41 - in Johannesburg. In 1955, at fourteen, his father put him on a plane to New York, to the yeshiva at 770 Eastern Parkway. After two years he transferred to the Chabad yeshiva in Montreal for five more, where his mentors were Reb Hershel Feigelstock and Reb Menachem Zev Greenglass. His own Sefer HaChassidim card - the registry card the Rebbe's library kept, in his own hand, dated 25 Av 5722 - reads: father Chaim Shaul Ze'ev, mother Chana; born 5701, Johannesburg; yeshiva from 5716 until 5722.



He was not the first South African boy to make that journey. On Simchas Torah eve 5708, seven years before he landed, an unnamed tamim - "recently come from a country overseas, South Africa, on the Rebbe's shlichus" - said l'chaim at the Rebbe Rayatz's hakafos, and the Rayatz answered l'chaim u'lvracha. The record does not give his name. The road from Johannesburg to 770 was already laid when Koppel stepped onto it.



The Rebbe wrote to him in those years, urging him to stay the course. One Erev Yom Kippur, the Rebbe had Rabbi Hodakov tell the bochur from Johannesburg to write to an elderly relative in Cleveland - a sister of Reb Pesach Ruch - a small kindness, assigned like a lesson.



Years later, at Greenglass's ninetieth birthday, the old mentor told Koppel what the Rebbe had once answered when asked about his teaching career: was it worth it? Of course it was worth it, the Rebbe said. For Koppel Bacher alone, it was worth it. He came home to South Africa in 1961. The family has been telling that answer ever since - it opens this book.

The builder

Koppel Bacher, 1941 - 2024



In 1962 the Rebbe pressed him on his shidduch with characteristic directness: So why aren't you doing anything about it? I want you to go right now and arrange a meeting. Koppel went to a phone booth and arranged it. He married Selma Goldman of Brooklyn, and in 1964 the couple returned to South Africa as emissaries - which reconciles the two dates the record gives: 1961 for his own return after yeshiva, 1964 for theirs.



Every Hoshanah Rabbah he had an aliyah in the Rebbe's minyan. One year a delayed plane made him miss it, and the Rebbe said: I missed you this morning - I didn't see you being called to the Torah. The photograph in this chapter catches one such morning in 5752.



South Africa was not gentle. He survived an AK-47 held to his head; his office was attacked five times, and he was tied up and robbed each time. His own account of it is one line: I just closed my eyes and pictured the Rebbe saying m'zol zich nit shreken - one must not be afraid. A Chanukah gelt story survives in two tellings - a silver dollar given to little Shaul at age five, in one, because you won't be here for Chanukah - and both tellings are kept.



He built. He brought and financed the Lipskars' start in South Africa in 1972, helped bring the rabbis who followed - Yossy Goldman among them - and was a founding member of the Hanholo of the Lubavitch Foundation of South Africa. When communal bodies wrote to the Rebbe after the 1979 Torah Academy land dispute asking him to curb his errant emissaries, the Rebbe wrote back telling them to reverse their own decline. And on February 11, 1990 - the day Mandela walked free - Koppel received and conveyed the Rebbe's message for South African Jewry: Tell them they have nothing to fear, and that South Africa will be good for Jews until the coming of moshiach.



He died on 23 Adar II 5784 - April 2, 2024 - at eighty-three, and was laid to rest at Westpark Cemetery, Johannesburg - block E-1072D, sharing the E-1072 plot with Selma, who waited at E-1072E, and with his mother Chana, whose presence in that plot the burial register revealed only after his death. The national Chabad kinus of 2024 was dedicated to his memory, and the rabbis spent it discussing his interaction and communication with the Rebbe. His sisters, Minnie Daniels of Johannesburg and Sorrel Sher of Sydney, survive in the record beside him.



The SA Jewish Report's fiftieth-anniversary profile fills in the determination: "Rabbi Koppel Bacher, a qualified Chabad rabbi living in Johannesburg, opted to work in his family business rather than become a congregational rabbi. But he was determined that an official Chabad office should be opened in this country. He made this known to the rebbe whenever he could." He had gone to the Chabad yeshiva in New York in 1955, at fourteen; nine years later he returned to South Africa intending to build what he called "a Jewish environment" in Johannesburg. When the Rebbe put word out that he wanted someone prepared for the challenge, the Lipskars raised their hands - and Bacher brought them to the country and financed their start-up. "I used to go every year to the rebbe," he says, "and I have since brought lots of rabbis here." Half a century on, the count stood at twenty Chabad centers and more than sixty shluchim in the country.



After his death, the comment thread under the movement's obituary filled in the daily texture. Sunday dollars, 5750

February 11, 1990 - the morning Mandela walked free



On the morning of Sunday, February 11, 1990 - 16 Shevat 5750 - the world watched Nelson Mandela walk out of prison, and the Bachers of Johannesburg stood where they stood on Sundays when they were in New York: in the line for the Rebbe's Sunday dollars. The encounter is on film - a thirty-two-second clip in the JEM archive - Koppel and Selma before the Rebbe on the morning their country changed.



Koppel would tell the SA Jewish Report the date from memory, without notes: the same morning that Nelson Mandela was released. Two liberations kept in one sentence - the nation's, on every screen on earth, and the small, personal kind a family flies eight thousand miles to stand in line for.



The SA Jewish Report kept the message itself whole. "Suddenly, the Rebbe turned to Bacher and said in Hebrew, 'You asked what message you can take there? Tell them not to be afraid. Moshiach is coming soon. It will be good until he comes, and afterwards even better.'" Bacher later wrote to the Rebbe about his feelings about the situation in South Africa - "People were very worried" - and Rabbi Yossy Goldman says of those years: "The Rebbe's repeated reassurances to us that South Africa would be a good place for Jews for all time went a long way to saving our community from complete disintegration."



The film lets the exchange be heard whole. In the archive's subtitles, the Rebbe gives dollars to the Africa-bound travelers in line - to one: Give this to charity in Africa, and may you report good news. To a woman traveling as well - the film shows her with a baby: You're travelling there as well. Give this to charity in Africa. A blessing - Your trip should be with much success - and the line's Amen. Then the message itself: You asked what message you can give over. Tell them not to be afraid, Moshiach is coming soon. It will be good until he comes, and afterwards even better. The preface - You asked what message you can give over - matches, word for word, the wording the SA Jewish Report printed from Koppel's own telling, an independent corroboration thirty-two years on. The bearded man who receives the opening line matches Koppel's tagged photographs of those years but is not labeled in the frame; the archive's own title card names him.



The message has been reprinted at least five times in the record - Rabbi Yossy Goldman's accounts of 2015, 2019 and 2020, the SA Jewish Report's 2022 rendering of Koppel's own words, and Rabbi Yehuda Stern's open letter of August 2025, which frames it as said before returning to South Africa. Goldman's own first-person version runs: Don't worry. It will be good for Jews in South Africa until Moshiach. And after Moshiach, it will be even better.

Selma

Sima Ronya bas Shlomo, 1943 - 2021



Selma Bacher was born Goldman, of Brooklyn - Sima Ronya, daughter of Reb Shlomo and Helen Goldman; one notice writes Sima Rania bas Shlomo, and the family's reading is kept. Her maternal grandmother was active in the Lubavitch Ladies Auxiliary - "in which she assisted in raising funds to participate in rescuing the Frierdiker Rebbe and his family from war-torn Europe," her obituary records - and the family's part in that rescue runs through both her lines. Her brother Dovid Goldman lives in Flatbush; her sister Rochel Weiner in Queens. That grandmother was Ita Yehudis, whose yahrtzeit the family keeps on 1 Tammuz; one family telling places her a generation closer, and both readings are kept.



In Johannesburg she became an educator: founding principal of the Torah Academy Girls High School, and later a Limudei Kodesh teacher in the elementary school - the half of the building that doesn't make the papers, which is to say the half that matters. She died on May 3, 2021 - 21 Iyar 5781 - at seventy-eight. The movement's notices remembered the principal of the Chabad Girls High School; a tribute by a onetime guest, Tzemach Yehudah Richter, published in Your Aliyah Advisor on the day she passed, remembered the house - an open home, its owners cornerstones of Johannesburg's Lubavitch community: a sheva brachas at the Bachers', where, he wrote, they treated us like royalty. Several great-granddaughters now carry her name: Sima Ronya, born again in the next rooms of the family. The comment thread under her obituary reads like a class register. "A very special lady with deep and profound insight, eidel and gentle ways and a twinkle in her eye." "A talmida chachoma who learned each day and taught thousands." "She was both my teacher and principal and she truly cared about each student, explaining with patience and love." COLlive called her one of the founders of the Jewish community in Johannesburg - a city she and Koppel reached, in its phrase, when it was "a barren wasteland of Yiddishkeit" - and recorded that she completed an in-depth study of the entire Tanach twice over. The SA Jewish Report counted thirty-three years in the classroom, and the year after her death the school named its Nach program for her: "a six-year journey through the whole of Tanach."



Rabbi Yossy Goldman also played a major role in South African Jewish education. His father was Reb Shimon Goldman; Selma's father was Reb Shlomo (Solomon) Goldman. The records examined do not establish a family relationship between them. The difference in their fathers' names rules out the proposed sibling identification, but it cannot exclude a more distant connection.



Nearly half a century on, her school is building again. The school's 2023 annual report records construction begun on Campus Leah, a purpose-built new campus for the Girls High School she founded in 1979, and the school's first democratic parent-elected board, seated that October. The half of the building that doesn't make the papers is getting walls of its own.

Zaida Shlomo

Rabbi Solomon Goldman of Brooklyn and Fresh Meadows, 1915 - 2006



Selma's father was born in Brooklyn on July 18, 1915, the son of Louis Goldman - a native of Ratno, in Volhynia - and Rachel Lea Wiener. The 1950 census catches the household whole at 696 Georgia Avenue, East New York: Solomon, recorded as 34, and Helen, also recorded as 34 despite her birth certificate’s 14 February 1909 date, with Selma 7 and David 5, all born in New York, the father's occupation entered as rabbi, his industry a synagogue.



He spent his life in the pulpit. Seventeen years at Tiferet Yisroel of East New York, from 1946 to 1963; then thirty-five years at the Utopia Jewish Center of Fresh Meadows, Queens, from 1964 until his retirement in 1999, and rabbi emeritus after. Alongside the pulpits: twenty-three years as chaplain of the Brookdale hospital, and two as an auxiliary chaplain at the Ossining prison. He died on May 11, 2006, at ninety.



Three years later the city put his name on the street he had served. Local Law 48 of 2008 co-named Utopia Parkway, from the Long Island Expressway to 67th Avenue, Rabbi Solomon Goldman Way; the law's own text remembers "a civic and religious leader in the Fresh Meadows community" who "served 36 years as Rabbi of the Utopia Jewish Center, and as Rabbi Emeritus following his retirement." The sign was unveiled at the 65th Avenue corner, where it stands in the photograph in this chapter. The family called him Zaida Shlomo.



Selma's mother Helen rests beside him in Mount Judah Cemetery, Ridgewood, Queens; her gravestone and her Social Security record give July 4, 2000. The date matters to the record: a 2009 account of the street unveiling describes Rebbetzin Helen Goldman, the rabbi's wife of 59 years, as present at the ceremony - but the stone wins, the reporter's present tense was error, and the circa-1950 marriage date drawn from that phrase is kept with its caveat. His own stone reads Harav Shlomo ben Eliyahu Yaakov, died 13 Iyar 5766.



The congregation outlived him by thirteen years. The Utopia Jewish Center - built in 1954, begun as a Conservative congregation - could no longer maintain a weekday or Shabbos minyan, its last rabbi said, and closed in the spring of 2019; the building now houses a preschool. The arc closes whole: Goldman from 1964 to 1999, and emeritus after; Rabbi Hirtz; closure.

The elder Koppel

Kopl Bacher of Rokiskis and Johannesburg, 1912 - 1974



The landsmanschaft's memorial volume profiles him beside his brother: "Kopl Bacher, the brother of Chaim-Shual (Zev) Bacher, also served communally. He was the co-founder of the Magen David Adom in South Africa and took part with activism and with financial help on behalf of the communal and Zionist funds. The exercises of the South Africa Haganah took place on his farm. The Rakishoker Society and other Jewish organizations would arrange cultural presentations and various undertakings on his farm. The institutions had the servants and goods at hand on the farm at their disposal without cost."



He had gone ahead of the family: when Sorrel immigrated in 1929 with five children, it was "to join her sons, Saul and Koppel." The burial register gives his dates - born 1912, died November 14, 1974, 29 Cheshvan 5735 - and his stone stands at Westpark T-577.



The previous edition of this book called him a cousin, following the family's recollection. The correction is printed here in the open: he was Solly's brother, and Rabbi Koppel's uncle. The memorial book gives Sorrel exactly one son named Koppel; the grave register names his father Yitzchak HaLevi - Solly's father Yitzchak Alter; and both men lie in Johannesburg. The pieces fit only one way.



One question the correction makes sharper, and it is kept visible: Rabbi Koppel was born in 1941, in his uncle's lifetime, and was named for him - or, in the older telling, for the great-grandfather Avraham Koppel Ruch, who died in 1904. Ashkenazi custom does not usually name for the living. Both namesakes stand in the tree; the family's naming is what it is, and the flag stays up.



Sorrel of Rokiskis

Chaya-Sarah-Ita Bacher, 1878 - 1943



The memorial book keeps a chapter for her, written by S. Klass, who knew her. "The wife of Yitzchak (Alter) Bacher was an unusually righteous woman." She was born "in an area of farms and forests on the outskirts of Rokiskis"; in the poor early years of her marriage she went from house to house collecting for poor families, and when she was widowed in 1923 she gave her days to providing poor brides with the necessities for their weddings. In 1929 she immigrated to South Africa with her five children, to join her sons Saul and Koppel, who had gone ahead. From Johannesburg she sent parcels back to the old home, and she raised the money that brought young women out - "all her work alone, without any help from committees or societies."



Klass kept one morning in full. A knock at the door in Johannesburg; two letters from Rokiskis - a young wife wanting to join her husband, a bride wanting to come to her future husband, and neither had the fare. "We took a tramcar, which took us to the end stop. There we went by another tramcar to Kensington... Suddenly a heavy rain came down. We then ran to a tree for shelter. The uphill climb left us breathless. When we reached the house of the Rokisker family, the lady of the house gave us a handsome donation... From there we went to other homes, and none refused. On that day alone we collected 50 pounds. Within several days Chaya-Sarah-Ita Bacher collected 175 pounds, which she sent to Rokiskis to the interested persons, who quickly came to South Africa." Many women, the chapter ends, are thankful for her efforts, which enabled them to escape the Nazi persecution and to come to South Africa.



The record gives her five names, and they are all one woman: Chaye-Sore-Ite and Chaya-Sarah-Ita in the memorial book, Sora Eta Chai in the burial registers, Sorrel Ruch in the family, and once, inside Klass's own story, plain Sarah Bacher. Her grave stands at Westpark E-283.



The memorial essay dates her passing to 28 September 1943. The burial registers instead record 28 August 1943, 27 Av 5703; a grave photograph is now available and is reproduced with the cemetery records. The September date remains a conflicting statement in the memorial essay, rather than an equally preferred date.



Yitzchak Alter

The first Bacher of the record, d. 1923



The memorial book gives the Bacher line its first fixed dates and its first fixed place. "Yitzchak (Alter) Bacher was born on a farm near Salok. In his youth he learned tailoring. After he married he immigrated to America. He was not happy with the American way of life and longed for the religious way in his hometown. When he saved up some dollars he returned home and opened a little shop in Radute on the outskirts of Rokiskis. At the time of the First World War Yitzchak settled in Rokiskis."



America did not keep him - he is one of the few who came, saw, and went home for the sake of the old way of life. The tailor of Radute is the ancestor the Johannesburg Bachers knew: Solly's father, the grandfather whose name the elder Koppel's stone carries - Koppel ben Yitzchak HaLevi. The last rabbis of Rokiskis

Levitin, Betzalel, Orelovitch



For a generation before the destruction, the rabbinate of Rokiskis ran in the family's own circle. From 1906 to 1915 the town's rav was Rabbi Shmuel Levitin - the same chief rabbi of Georgia of the earlier chapter - and the printed apparatus of the Rebbe's letters records that he sat "on the seat of his father-in-law, R' Asher Margolies." The memorial book's portrait of that father-in-law is kept whole: "The Rabbi, Reb Asher was an old man and weak, with a grey beard... At the end of his life, he dedicated his entire time to study and to writing a sefer about religious questions and answers. His house became full of his religious books and published works that his daughter sent away to cities and shtetlekh... After he died, his rabbinical seat was taken by his son-in-law, Reb Shmuel, who is now in New York and occupies an eminent place in the Lubavitch yeshiva there." And the same R' Asher Margolies is named in the family record as the great-grandfather of Berl Chaikin of Cleveland - which would make the last rabbis of Rokiskis kin to Sarah Chana's house. The side of that descent is not stated in the source, and it is left open here as it is in the tree. The Zelig memoir adds a glimpse of the seat a generation earlier: in about 1893, the bar-mitzva boy from Rokiskis told the Alter of Slabodka that "Btzalel der rav" held the town - a single memoir's datum, recorded as such.



In the winter of 5689 Levitin was arrested in Nevel, where the Rebbe had set him over the Beis Midrash l'Rabbanim, and the Rokiskis chassidim wrote to the Rebbe Rayatz in Riga. The answer, dated 24 Shevat 5689, is the town's Chabad soul in one paragraph: "his situation is not yet known... It is right and necessary that they gather a proper sum, to be held until it becomes possible, with G-d's help, to bring him to you - for the sum they wrote of is very small, and they must endeavor to collect properly." The town was raising ransom money for its former rav, ten years gone. He survived; by February 1940 he was in Brooklyn - a telegram of 26 Shevat 5700 reaches "Rabbis Levitin and Jacobson" at their Brooklyn address - and he ended his days the senior mashpia of 770.



After Levitin came Rabbi Betzalel - the landsleit's relief parcel of March 14, 1920, a hundred pounds, was sent "to the address of the Rakishok Rabbi, Reb Betzalel, of blessed memory" - and after Betzalel, the last: Dr. Zelig Orelovitch, "Shlomoh Skopishker's son," a PhD of the Berlin Beis Midrash Elyon, married to Roza Snieg. He was invited to a rabbinate in Sweden and offered one in Johannesburg - the very city the Bachers had made their own - and he stayed. When the end came at the open pits, he spoke to the condemned for two hours, and perished with Roza and their son Moshele. The Johannesburg pulpit that might have been his is one of the book's quiet what-ifs.

Reb Zelig Rakishker

The Lomza rosh yeshiva, 1879 - 1941 - a Ruch of the old home



The son's memoir names Reb Zelig's parents as Michel and Pere, and the KehilaLinks Ruch family account gives the same parents and identifies Pesach among his siblings. The family tree now places Zelig under Michel Ruch, whose branch connects to Sorrel's family. That placement rests on a reasoned identification of the Michel in these accounts with the Michel in the family tree; it is marked as inferred, not as a fully documented genealogical link. Zelig's life belongs here with that qualification visible. The memoir runs in the Rokiskis book under the title My Father, Of Blessed Memory, signed Meir Dovid of Johannesburg; read at full resolution, its byline names the subject - the Lomzha rosh yeshiva Rabbi Yehoshua Zelig Ruch - and its author is his son, Meir Dovid Ruch.



Rokiskis, the son writes, was famous in the rabbinic-yeshiva world as the birth-town of R' Zelig Rakishker - and famous in another way at home: the town was, in his phrase, the fortress of Lubavitch chassidus in Lite, and it shrugged at sitting-and-learning. The boy was born in 1880 by his son's reckoning - the necrology gives 1879, and both are kept - "the son of the simple and decent Jew R' Michel and Pere, plain, ordinary but honest folks." At bar-mitzva age he went up to Slabodka, and the Alter asked where he came from and who was rav there. Btzalel is the rav, the boy answered. The Alter marveled: Btzalel is the rav THERE, and yet such a child comes from there. He became the Alter's emissary when the musar dispute erupted at Telz and Mir, spending months in each yeshiva until the quarrel settled - a successful synthesis of lomdus, Chabad and musar, his son writes, formed the complete yeshiva-man. Reb Lazer Lomzer chose him as son-in-law, and he was appointed rosh yeshiva of Lomza. In the First World War he evacuated with his family and his students to Priluki, near Poltava, and students from Minsk, Kiev and Kharkov followed him back to Lomza when he rebuilt. On the road home, his son records, "a warm home we found at his brother R' Pesach Ruch's, and family." Family tradition holds that this Reb Pesach of Rokiskis was the same Pesach Ruch who would host the Frierdiker Rebbe in the town in 1931; the name, the place and the era all agree, and the identification is offered as such.



When his father-in-law founded the yeshiva's branch in the Land, Zelig traveled with the first fifty bochurim - his best students, his son writes - "to be there the first rosh yeshiva." He loved the land, and he returned to his Lomzha students all the same. The standard histories record the founding group led by Rabbi Eliyahu Dushnitzer, with Rabbi Moshe Aryeh Ozer and later Rabbi Reuven Katz at the yeshiva's head; the memoir's account is the family's, and both stand.



In the 1930s the Depression crushed the yeshiva's finances, and he had to leave it for a short time and come to South Africa to raise funds. The Ponevezher Rav told his son: "The Lomzha Rosh Hayeshiva lacks the ability to make money. His virtues stand in his way." Yet it worked - "only with his sincerity and decency," the son writes - and decades later a Jew in Johannesburg was still telling him: your father is remembered here. "Thank God my trip was successful," Reb Zelig told it. "I influenced one Jewish man to put on teffilin and another to keep his store closed on the Sabbath." A man in Johannesburg told the son: "To this day I keep my store closed on Shabbos Shuva... in memory of your father."



In September 1939, in Lomza, German soldiers cut his beard. He answered them "danke sehr" - thank you very much - the only ironic thank-you, his son writes, that he ever uttered. He refused escape while any of his students could not come - "go, children, I will yet come to you" - and by the time he could leave, the roads were sealed. Days before the destruction, students hiding in bunkers smuggled him and his family to Vilna; tired and broken, he paid a last visit to Rakishok, which welcomed him warmly. On his death the two memorial accounts part ways, and both are printed: one line in the book says he perished at the hands of the Nazi hangmen in Lomza; his son's account and the necrology memorial together say he died a martyr in Vilna, with his students - "the beloved and dear in their lives were even in their death not parted."



The landsmanschaft

The Rakishker circle of Johannesburg



The Rakishoker landsleit of Johannesburg kept the old home alive in the new one, and a Ruch sat at the center of it. Berl Ruch was born in Kurkletz near Rakishok. The Yiddish original gives 1913, whereas the English translation gives 1919; the original is preferred here, with the difference recorded. His father Pesakh, described as an ardent Hasid and deeply religious man, moved the family to town during the First World War. Berl went from kheder and Talmud Torah to yeshiva before leaving for South Africa around the turn of 1928-1929. There he helped found the Rakishok Aid Society and served as its treasurer; when the Aid Society and the Landsmanschaft merged he was chosen vice-chairman, under chairman Yerakhmial Arons-Arsh - "and is to this day," the memorial book says. In the 1930s, at a committee table in Johannesburg, it was B. Ruch who rose to speak about not buying German goods. In 1937 he sat on the committee that assisted the visiting fundraiser from home. "He helped the Chabad movement financially," the profile adds, "and he helped the Joniskelis Rabbi, Reb Yosef Sidrin, when he came to collect money for the Rakishok Yeshiva. At the same time, he was a Zionist and he was devoted to the Zionist movement heart and soul." On June 16, 1949, Berl and Mrs. Ruch were in the room when the landsleit resolved to publish the memorial book itself, and B. Ruch was chosen for the five-man commission that made it - the book these chapters have been quoting.



His wife Liba was born in 1921 in Panevezys and came as a girl to Muizenberg; English-schooled and Yiddish-speaking, she became, the book says, "a devoted member of the Rakishok Landsmanschaft: helps to organize events, to send packages for landsleit and to gather funds," and was chosen to its committee. "Her love of Yiddish theater is great. And to this day she takes part in producing and appears with recitations at various small art evenings."



The memorial book’s postwar survivor list names Pesach, Leibe and Basye Ruch in Siberia. A separately captioned family photograph identifies Pesach alive there in 1952. Together these sources contradict the older family-tree estimate of a death around 1941. The photograph’s caption says that he died in Siberia, but gives no date; his death must therefore remain undated, after the photograph, rather than being printed as about 1941.



The book preserves the society's inner life with the same care. Its correspondence committee - Shuel Bacher among the names, with Zerach Kosef and Berl Stein, the second name read uncertainly - kept up an intensive letter exchange with the old home, and the Aid Society, the book says, never refused anyone help. Its women are named beside its men: Toybe Orlin, Etl Arans-Arsh, Libe Ruch, Alte Muskat, and the vice-chairwoman Sarah Klam. The merger of the Aid Society and the Landsmanschaft passed on July 7, 1948, after Yerachmiel Arans-Arsh rose to defend it. And the portrait pages give the circle faces: Toybe and Moshe Orlin, who entered the landsmanschaft together; Yitzchak-Zerach Beinart, among the society's first twelve founders in 1912; Nachum Blacher; Yerachmiel and Etl Arans-Arsh. A generation earlier, in Rokiskis itself, a photograph taken on July 2, 1925 shows the town's Keren Kayemet committee of thirteen beneath a hand-painted banner and a Star of David - Yerachmiel Ruch named first among the standing, and Chana Arsh, Pesach's partner in the iron store, among the seated.



The house on the market square

Pesach Ruch of Rokiskis - merchant, host, exile



In the center of Rokiskis, on the market square, stood a wooden house the Ruch family built in 1921 - built jointly, the family's record says, by Pesach Ruch and Rachmiel Ruch. The town's 1921 owners' map marks the lot; the kehilalinks record makes Rachmiel Pesach's brother, while the family's written tree calls him a cousin, and both readings are kept. The house still stands, a shop at its street level.



Pesach was a metal-products merchant. His store - the ANGRO-IRON store on Independence Square, run with his partner Chana Arsh, as the memorial book's own caption names her; an earlier reading heard the partner as Khone Arsh, and the variant is kept - stood at number 26 on the square until the partners split it into separate iron stores in the late 1930s. He held four properties in the town. A guided tour of the old town, recorded by a visitor, remembers him elected to the city council - the council lists have not been pulled - and tells a First World War story about him: that he hid a wealthy persecuted landowner during the 1914 expulsions, and that the landowner's postwar gratitude made him one of the town's wealthiest men. The book prints it as the tour tells it. The memorial book's own pages are firmer: Pesach Ruch co-financed the building of the town's Talmud Torah, and when the writer L. Jaffe visited, it was Pesach Ruch who drove out to meet him with a carriage and a horse - one horse, the page says, where an earlier telling had two, and both are kept - while Maccabi and Hashomer Hatzair marched, and the Gentiles of the town called him der yidisher kayzer, the Jewish emperor.



The 1952 family photograph names Leibe, his wife Gita, and Batya, also called Baske, later Zborovsky. It also names the young Rachmiel and Pinchas, but does not establish their parents; they should not be listed as Pesach’s sons solely on that evidence. The memorial on printed page 585 names Pere-Eyde as wife and mother and records her death in Siberia on 16 Sivan, 1952. The Basheve or Basye in the survivor list is not securely identified as Pesach’s wife, so that name should not be treated as an equally established spouse.



The family’s deportation account places Pesach, his wife, Leibe and Batya in the Pavlovsky district of Altai Krai. The published deportation list dates the removal to 14 June 1941; the guided tour’s July date is a later recollection and is not preferred. The photograph caption places Pesach in Siberia in 1952 and states that he died there. It does not supply the date of his death or prove from the image alone that he never travelled elsewhere afterward.

The week the Rebbe stayed

Rokiskis, 1 - 9 Adar 5691 - February 18 - 26, 1931



In the first week of Adar 5691 - February 18 to 26, 1931, the dates pinned against the rabbinic calendar - the Frierdiker Rebbe, Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, came to Rokiskis and stayed the week in Pesach Ruch's house on the market square. The visit had been postponed from the winter on account of illness. Earlier printings and the family's own notices long gave 1930, and a summer visit; the Rebbe's published record fixes Adar 5691, and the variants are kept.



The town competed to host him. By the tour's telling, Pesach won the honor by undertaking all the expenses and preparing the second floor and special rooms, against a rival who proposed sharing the costs. The documentary record adds the detail: Pesach built a special room for the guest and connected the house to the electric grid - a rarity in those days. A family legend, printed as a legend, says the dancing after the Shabbos meal shook the snow clean off the roof - the only clear roof in Rokiskis that mid-winter.



The week survives in the Rebbe's own published record. The hall was packed day and night, a witness wrote - like the Days of Awe. The maamar Vehabriech Hatichon was said before kabbalas Shabbos in the lodging itself; Ata Echad after Mincha in the great beis midrash. On the Monday several hundred women assembled and asked to see him; he came out and blessed them, and spoke to the women in Yiddish. On the Tuesday evening, at a gathering on taharas hamishpacha, the Agudas Nashim women's union was founded, its pinkas opened with the founding inscription he wrote and signed in the house hall. To the teachers of the Yavneh schools he said, on the Thursday: the fate of hundreds of Jewish children - meaning thousands - is placed on your conscience. That Thursday, 9 Adar, before his departure, he said the farewell maamar in the house - Na'aseh na aliyas kir ketanah, let us make a small upper room, the verse of a room prepared for a holy guest - then met the yeshiva students and the Yavneh children, and at half past two the town saw him off, hundreds escorting him with song, to cries of tzeischem l'shalom.



The photograph of the visit was taken inside the Ruch house. A Ruch descendant, publishing it, wrote that his father took it during the visit, in the family home; the print in the memorial book carries a local studio's signature - J. Klopsky, the reading is uncertain - and a second, Hebrew hand, and the two attributions stand side by side. Rachmiel Ruch, the family photographer, is a candidate for the cameraman; the record does not say.



And the family's own account, from Reuben Ruch, Rachmiel's son, who grew up in that house: the Rebbe stayed a week; minyanim were held in the house; visitors came and went; and on the day of his departure he blessed the gathered Ruch relatives. Reuben said that everyone who received the Rebbe's blessing survived the Holocaust. It is printed as the family tells it - an eyewitness tradition, and the measure of what the week meant.



Eitan Shelach remembers

Ahuva’s letters, Hanan’s photographs and a remembered visit



Dr. Eitan Shelach’s memoir, I Remember Now, names his parents as Ahuva of the Ruch family and Hanan Shneiderman, both born in Rokiskis and emigrating to the Land of Israel in 1935. In his childhood recollections he calls his mother Ahuva (Libke) and his father Hanan (Elchanan-Tzvi). Hanan worked as a photographer in Rehovot and Hadera; while establishing the second shop, the family sometimes slept on its floor. These are explicit family identifications in Shelach’s own account.



Ahuva kept the scattered Ruch family close through letters in Yiddish, written almost every evening. Their return envelopes supplied stamps for her son’s collection. During a 1972 visit to Canada, Eitan met relatives in Moncton and Montreal. He identifies Yerachmiel, who had remained in Lithuania, as his mother’s brother, and Sasha as Yerachmiel’s son. At Ahuva’s request, he collected $1,000 from relatives to buy a dental chair for Sasha’s wife Masha after their arrival in Israel from the Soviet Union. His account makes family solidarity tangible across the distance.



The memoir also recalls a later visit to the Lubavitcher community in Brooklyn. Eitan introduced himself as a member of the Ruch family and showed a photograph that he attributed to his father, depicting the previous Lubavitcher Rebbe, whom he remembered through family accounts as a guest in their Rokiskis home. The memoir’s attribution should be retained as Eitan’s testimony: his later suggestion that this was the same photograph published by JEM was tentative, and neither the identity of the particular image nor its photographer is independently settled here.



A separate recollection preserved from Shelach’s subsequent correspondence describes his mother and other girls finding the visitors’ written requests, or kvitlach, after the Rebbe’s visit and playing at being the Rebbe and his audience. Torah Academy

The school at the center of the story



The school that Selma and Koppel built their working lives around deserves its own chapter. Rabbi Mendel Lipskar's biography on the school's own site gives the founding: "Rabbi Mendel Lipskar, Chairman of Lubavitch Foundation of Southern Africa, established Torah Academy in 1979. He has served as the Chairman of the Hanhala from inception until today." The school's other histories date the founding to 1983, when Rabbi Dovid Hazdan arrived as principal of the Boys High School - then about sixteen boys; both dates stand in the record, and the 1980 purchase of the eleven-acre Lubavitch House campus sits between them. Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Goldman was a member of the founding committee and the school's first rav.



Hazdan came on the Rebbe's own instruction: "When they wrote to the Rebbe with various options, including the Chabad in North Hollywood which Rabbi Hazdan was running remotely, the Rebbe indicated that he should give preference to Torah Academy." He has been dean since 2000. In 1989 he also took the pulpit of the Great Synagogue on Wolmarans Street, walking downtown for five years to keep it afloat, and when that building closed he moved the shul to his backyard in Oaklands - the Great Park Shul. In 1998 the school launched Cycalive, the bridge-building cycle ride with Soweto students that is now an official Mandela Day event.



Selma was the founding principal of the Girls High School - thirty-three years in the building - and after her death the school named its Nach program for her, "a six-year journey through the whole of Tanach." The family keeps teaching in the school: Chanie Bacher's first-graders sang their siddur concerts in the SA Jewish Report's pages. The school today counts some sixteen hundred students across its campuses.

A childhood in Tbilisi

Azriel Chaikin, born 1931



Azriel Chaikin was born in 1931 in Soviet Georgia - Bandza, in one telling of his own, Zestafoni in the 2019 interviews; both are kept - the year after the Kutaisi chuppah. The sources quarrel over his exact birthday and even his birth year, with one encyclopedia offering 1938; the family biography's 1931 is preferred here.



He was a child of the underground: he learned in the Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva in Kutaisi while his father sat in the camps, and his bar mitzvah was in Kutaisi too. Around that bar mitzvah there is the memory the family tells quietly: a visit to his father, in prison. In 1946 he made the exodus with his parents and sisters, passing through Lvov, Prague and Antwerp. A published account calls him fourteen when he concealed his father's papers in his coat; the exact age depends on the unresolved birthday chronology. In 1947 he crossed to New York and entered the central Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva at 770.



In 1950, about nineteen, he wrote to the Rebbe about a visa to Canada, where his father was heading. His own rabbinic career began early: rabbi of a synagogue in Pittsburgh in 1954, dayan in Montreal in 1955 - twenty-four years old and already sitting on a beis din.

There is no city where a mikva can't be built

Morocco, 5716 - 5718



In the winter of 5716 he married Sarah, the daughter of Rabbi Michoel Lipsker - the shadchan was Rabbi Binyomin Gorodetsky, and a blessing letter from Rebbetzin Chana is preserved. The records call her both Chaya and Sarah, perhaps Sarah Chaya; the family knows her as Sarah, and so does this book. They settled in Agadir, Morocco, where her father had been the Rebbe's first shliach to the country, and they built: a yeshiva in Agadir, a Beis Rivka school for the girls.



And a mikva. Agadir had none, and the materials were what they were, so the mikva of Agadir was built with blocks of ice factory concrete - the story survives with the Rebbe's answer attached to it, when the impossibility was raised: there is no city where a mikva can't be built.



On 15 Shevat 5718 - 1958 - Morocco expelled him, on a false suspicion over aliyah. A governor shouted he's a spy; he was tailed to the plane. The Rebbe personally telegraphed the United States ambassador and the consul in Casablanca: I have known Rabbi Chaikin many years; his work is only religious education. The expulsion stood - Moroccan independence had its own logic - and the couple turned north, to Denmark.



Chief Rabbi

Copenhagen, Brussels, Kyiv - 1958 onward



In Copenhagen he founded Yeshivas Oholei Yosef Yitzchak and served as chief rabbi for the better part of a decade, coordinating Merkos shlichus for Scandinavia by 5724 and relaying instructions from Rabbi Hodakov; in 1963 the press catches him assisting an Israeli colonel, Peron, passing through. A 1962 letter on the letterhead of the city's Scandinavian Jewish High School asks the Conference on Jewish Material Claims for twelve thousand dollars toward a camp house of the school's own, signed Rabbi I. Chaikin - the initial of his Russian name; it is printed a few pages on. In 5728 he became Ravad of Brussels. In 5750, King Baudouin made him a knight of the Order of the Crown. In 5752 he became Chief Rabbi of Belgium. From 5763 to 5768 he served as Chief Rabbi of Ukraine, presented at Kyiv's President Hotel in September 2003, and he has long stood in the Conference of European Rabbis. The synagogue of Copenhagen, as it looked in his century, is printed in this chapter.



Through all of it he carried one private commission. In 5729 his brother Sholom DovBer had written to the Rebbe about publishing their father's shiurim, and the Rebbe answered in his own hand: haya la'asos - it should be done. Forty years later, through Merkaz Rabanei Europa, Rabbi Azriel Chaikin published his father's sefer: Tiferes Azriel, 5771. The son who had visited his father in prison closed the account with a book. The Chaikins live now in Crown Heights.

The Rebbe's letters to Azriel

Igros Kodesh, 5716 - 5721



Nine letters from the Rebbe to Azriel Chaikin survive in the printed Igros Kodesh, from the Agadir years to the Copenhagen rabbinate, and they read like a master class in what the Rebbe asked of his young shluchim.



The first is dated 4 Sivan 5716, weeks after the wedding - volume 13, letter 4413. The Rebbe presses the expansion of the yeshiva and the camp, remarks that Azriel's new wife should put her talents to work in the camp, and adds a postscript that belongs to the century: check - unofficially - whether the changed situation for Jews in the Arab lands would block a return permit if he visited New York.



Letter 4639 of the same volume, 21 Av 5716, chides him over cuts at Beis Rivka - expansion, not standing still - names Rabbi Binyomin Gorodetzky as his shadchan, and permits him to write that he acts on the Rebbe's suggestion in buying a building. Volume 14, letter 4874, 26 Cheshvan 5717, founds the all-day Beis Rivka school, and quotes the Rayatz's street-lamp sicha - the small light that shames the darkness. An open letter of the same volume, 5075, names Azriel the living spirit of the Agadir mikva. And in volume 15, letter 5328, the Rebbe names him outright his point man in Morocco. Volume 13, letter 4273, preserves even the silence between letters: the Rebbe tracking that no letter had arrived from Azriel.



Then the Copenhagen era. Volume 17, letter 6460, 25 Av 5718, is the first letter addressed to Copenhagen - the timeline pin of the move north, six months after the Morocco expulsion - an avoda letter: when you toil, you find. Volume 20, letter 7607, 25 Adar 5721, handles the sale of chometz with a communal power-of-attorney form - the paperwork of a chief rabbi. And letter 7750 of the same volume, 13 Elul 5721, answers a tefillin question and then settles a career: when Azriel talked of leaving Copenhagen, the Rebbe told him a new rabbinate needs time to bed down - stay a few more months. He stayed ten years.



The Cleveland rav and the Crown Heights household

Sholom DovBer, Chaya Tzirl, Hinda - the Berl question, answered



Sholom DovBer Chaikin - Berl - was born in 1947 in Antwerp, fifteen days before his mother's death; one encyclopedia moves his birth to Montreal and dates it 27 Tammuz, but the family was in Antwerp, where his mother died on July 30, and the Antwerp reading stands. The inference that childbirth took her is the family's, and it is labeled as such. He grew up, in the family's phrase, never leaving his father's hand until his last day - and he collected the shiurim that became Tiferes Azriel.



He learned at 770 from 5725 to 5730 and was baal koreh in the Rebbe's mourning-year minyanim. He married Sheindel-Sheina, daughter of Rabbi Chaim Shneur Zalman Schorr, spent about ten years on shlichus in London, and became rav of the Chabad community in Cleveland and a member of Vaad Rabonei Lubavitch. His children are shluchim across the map - Dovid Leib and Menachem Mendel in Toronto, Meir in Montreal, Eliyahu Shmuel in Barbados, Elchanan in Baltimore, with daughters' households in Moscow, Cleveland, Staten Island, Oak Park, Philadelphia and Bala - first cousins of Yossi, and a whole cousin-line of their own.



His sister Chaya Tzirl - the Clara, Mrs. Baruch Brickman, of the 1965 obituary - married Baruch ben Tzion Brickman of Klimovitch on 19 Adar 5718, with the Rebbe as mesader kiddushin. Baruch, who died 29 Iyar 5786, was a co-founder of the Empire Shtiebel; the 19 Kislev campaign kept his line verbatim - der choson Brickman, drei mol azoy fil: the bridegroom Brickman gave three times as much. Their son is Rabbi Meir Chaim Brickman of Sea Gate, named for his grandfather; their daughter Sarah Chana Posner is named for her grandmother. Their sister Hinda is Mrs. Nathan Gourarie of Montreal, mother of Joseph Yitzchak, Sholem Ber, Ovadya and Sarah Chana.



Earlier editions of this book paused on one more name in the 1965 obituary: a son, Berl Chaikin, no title, no city, never seen in the record before or since - and asked whose son he was. The question is answered now, by the Rebbe's own pen. A letter of 25 Nissan 5720, printed in Igros Kodesh, volume 19, letter 7271, is the Rebbe's bar mitzvah blessing to Berl, addressed through Meir Chaim - confirming the bar mitzvah year, 5720, against a birth in 5707, and confirming what the family always knew: Berl was Meir Chaim's son, Sholom DovBer, the rav of Cleveland. The obituary's loose name is tied off. His own encyclopedia entry adds one more thread of the mother's side: Berl's great-grandfather was Reb Asher Margolis, the rav of Rakshik and mechutan of Reb Shmuel Levitin - the same Asher Margolis this book met in Sarah Chana's chapter, where he belongs.

Every photograph in this part comes from the JEM Living Archive, Jewish Educational Media's photo library, which indexes the family under "Bacher": Koppel in fifty-six frames across sixteen years of Tishrei visits, Selma in three, the children in more, and nine photographs of Rebbetzin Chana's funeral on President Street in 1964 that reach the archive in a collection labeled "Bacher Prints" - apparently the family's own prints.



The archive's identifications stand in the captions as tagged. One the family will notice: the tradition that Solly attended the 1964 funeral is not confirmed by the archive, which names only Chaim Yehuda Krinsky, Yisroel Jacobson and Shaya Treitel in those frames. Solly was living that Tishrei - the last of his life - and family tradition places him at the levaya. The archive's tags do not say. The record leaves it there.



The photographs are retained in their original watermarked preview form for this private family review. A printable licensed master and permission for wider distribution have not been established. The watermark is part of the reproduced image and has not been removed.

The Snovsk bridge

The precise connection between Ezriel and Meir Chaim Chaikin of Snovsk and the older Podobranka, Shklov, Kiev and Hebron branches remains unproved. Their wider Chabad history is retained without turning the missing connection into a completed descent claim.

Dates and identities

Meir Chaim’s arrest date, charge and sentence vary between later accounts. The original arrest file has not been recovered. Azriel’s exact birthday is disputed. Sarah Chana’s matching Antwerp burial entry is strong evidence, while a civil death certificate could resolve the spelling, birthplace and date differences.

Cyla and the boys

The Arolsen and JDC documents disagree on some birth dates and places. Cyla’s Pinsk and Koidanov accounts, September 20 and 27 birth dates, the boys’ recorded birthplaces and the circumstances of her marriage to Meir Chaim should continue to be checked against civil records. A matching name in a memorial database is not sufficient to identify her father.

The Ruch households

The narrative and family register distinguish family tradition, memoir, cemetery evidence and civil-record inference. Rachmiel’s parentage, the competing placements of Dovid and Yissachar, and names attached to Pesach’s household remain qualified. The 1952 photograph establishes Pesach’s survival to that time, without settling his later death date.

Names in the Bacher family

Frieda and Freide, Debra and Devorah, and Scop or Kaplin require comparison of original family records. They have not been silently merged or multiplied. The order of children in a family list is not assumed to be birth order; an unnamed birth notice remains unnamed.
