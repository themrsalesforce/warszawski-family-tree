# The Warszawskis

Coffee-table edition · 1 October 2026


## Reading this family history

Private family edition  •  1 October 2026

The Warszawski story joins families from Warsaw, Babruysk and Subcarpathia with a Cleveland-born Garson daughter and a Paris-born Garson son. The chapters follow the people who became Joe and Lori Warszawski’s household, then the nine children and their families. Original records and photographs sit beside the lives they document.

Names move between languages and records. Szyman is Shimon; Berta is Grammy; Ileen is Gabby; Arie Lawrence Hersh is Zeidy Larry. A familiar name is used in the narrative, while variants remain in the source register and family atlas. A date in a funeral notice is not automatically a death date, and a cemetery interment date is identified as such.

The evidence has different strengths. Civil records, court files, contemporary notices and photographs document what their makers recorded. An oral history records a person’s recollection. A family memory is valuable testimony, but it does not become a civil record by repetition. Conflicts remain visible where they affect identity, kinship or chronology.

The family trees follow connected branches across generations, with a named register at the back. Paris and Tigery families, the Cleveland Kogens and other unresolved leads are kept distinct from the documented family. A shared surname does not supply a missing parent-child link.

This dated edition preserves the earlier family narrative, corrects superseded readings and adds the substantive records gathered through 30 September 2026. Earlier editions and original sources remain unchanged. It is intended for private family review. Newspaper, cemetery and publisher photographs retain their source credits; availability in an archive does not establish permission for wider publication.

## The places and the generations

A short route through the book

Warsaw and Babruysk lead to Szyman and Berta Warszawski. Berta’s parents were Mordukh and Elka Zelkind; the later notices name Berta’s sisters and their children. The war years left gaps that later family notices cannot fill.

Khust, Nagybanya, Auschwitz, Warsaw and the Dachau camp system appear in Larry Hersh’s testimony. His postwar account runs through Bricha, Palestine and the Altalena before Cleveland in 1956. The dates, names and sequences in his later published accounts do not always agree; they are compared rather than combined.

Paris and Cleveland lead to Victor Garson and Pearl Adelstein. Their 1916 marriage, Victor’s 1919 declaration and Pearl’s 1940 petition give the firm documentary spine of the Garson story. The French research chapters examine other Garson households without making them Victor’s ancestors.

Cleveland is where these lines meet. Joe and Lori’s engagement, their children’s notices, the family’s memories and the graves at Zion Memorial Park record a household that carried the past into another generation.

## The tailor of Cedar Center

Szyman Warszawski 1918–1995

Szyman Warszawski - Shimon to his family - was born in Poland on April 1, 1918; a Social Security index gives the city as Warsaw. He lived in Russia through the Second World War, and came home to a Poland that was no longer the world he had left. In 1962 he immigrated to Cleveland with his wife Berta, and there he built the life his grandchildren would remember: for twenty-five years he owned the Mayflower Tailoring Company at Cedar-Center, a member of Kol Israel, a working man of the old school. He retired around 1988, seven years before his death.

His 1995 obituary described a forty-year marriage to Berta, their son Joseph, five grandchildren then living, and a brother, Benyamin, in Israel. Later generations would enlarge the family beyond that contemporary count.

The records disagree about one thing, and the disagreement is worth preserving. His headstone at Zion Memorial Park gives his Hebrew name as Shimon ben Zisl - his father named Zisl. A Social Security index entry names his parents as Joseph Warszavski and Rochel Goldberg. Both are printed here, unsettled, exactly as they stand; whether Zisl and Joseph identify the same man has not been resolved. What is certain is the life: Warsaw, the war years in Russia, the return, and then thirty-three years in Cleveland.

Szyman died on July 18, 1995, and was buried the next day at Zion Memorial Park, plot 6-6-111 - directly beside Mordukh Zelkind, his father-in-law, at 6-6-110. Their neighboring graves document the closeness of the two households in Cleveland.

## Berta called Grammy

Bila bat Mordechai 1935–2016

Berta Zelkind was born on May 15, 1935, a daughter of Mordechai and Elka Zelkind, and grew up in a world that was coming apart. She spoke in later years of where she fled from - not of where she went, how she returned, or how she met Szyman.

What survives is the warmth. To the next generation she was Grammy. Szyman called her Bella - the name traveling down the family, as names do. A friend named Frieda, meeting Berta's grandchildren long after, cried when she realized whose family they were. "Bertha was my friend," she said. It is a small scene, and it says more about her than a date can.

Her headstone reads: Bila bat Mordechai - her father Mordechai's name, carried in stone - May 15, 1935 to November 28, 2016, niftera 27 Cheshvan 5777, "Beloved Mother, Grandmother, Great Grandmother, Sister." She was interred the next day, November 29, in section Z6, row 1, grave 58. The family keeps her yahrtzeit on 26 Cheshvan; the stone's Hebrew date reads the 27th. Both are kept here, side by side, as the family keeps them.

## The Zelkinds of Babruysk

Mordukh and Elka and their four daughters

Mordechai Zelkind - Mordukh - was born in 1912; Elka Kogen, his wife, was born November 12, 1913, in Babruysk, on the Berezina in what is now Belarus. A Social Security index names her parents as Isac Kaga and Tsilya Gershon. How Mordukh and Elka survived the war, and when they came to America, the record does not say.

What it tells us in full is the family they raised. Mordukh's death notice in the Plain Dealer of September 25, 1996 reads: "MORDUKH ZELKIND, beloved husband of Elka (nee Kogen), devoted father of Bertha Warshawsky, Gail Zelkind, Maria Komisarchik (Isaak) and Celya Elin (Matvey), loving grandfather of Joseph Warshawsky, Dimitry and Kira Komisarchik, Inna and Boris Elin, great grandfather of seven... Interment Zion Memorial Park."

Every name in that notice is a thread. Bertha Warshawsky is Berta - Grammy - Joe's mother; the Warshawsky spelling is how the paper heard the name. Gail, Maria and Celya are Berta's sisters. And "grandson Joseph Warshawsky" is Joe himself, named in his grandfather's notice twenty-one years before his own stone would stand in the same cemetery.

Mordukh was interred on September 25, 1996, in section Z6, row 6, grave 110 - beside Szyman, his son-in-law, at 6-6-111. Elka followed him on March 14, 2005, and was interred March 16, in Z6, row 4. Her stone reads 1914 - 2005; the Social Security index gives her birth as November 12, 1913. The year stands as the stone has it, with the index noted alongside - a single year's disagreement in a life of ninety-one.

Gail Zelkind, Berta's sister, was born July 25, 1942 and died February 28, 2013. Her stone carries her portrait, a young woman smiling out of black granite - the only face of her the record holds.

## Zeidy Larry

Arie Lawrence Hersh 1925–2012

Larry Hersh’s 1984 oral history places his birth in Khust, Czechoslovakia, in June 1925. A later compiled tribute gives 1 June. In the family he was Aryeh Leib, Zeidy Larry, a survivor whose childhood world lay in Subcarpathia. His naturalization card preserves the earlier name as Arie–Larry–Hersh Kovitz and his chosen American signature as Arie Lawrence Hersh.

He and his sister Judith survived the Holocaust. Family memory speaks of seven children; Larry’s testimony says that he had four sisters and was the only boy, five children in all. These are competing accounts of the family’s size. Judith Dratler’s 2025 obituary identifies her maiden surname as Herskowitz and remembers her parents and siblings who perished.

Judith, called Judy, died on 5 June 2025, aged ninety-six. Her obituary names her late husband Phillip Dratler, her daughter Leigh Wasserman and son Jay, and subsequent generations. She was buried at Bet Olam Cemetery in Beachwood after a private funeral. The obituary links the surviving Cleveland family to the losses Larry described, without supplying every missing name.

Larry’s own account dates his arrival in Cleveland to 1956, after Israel and the 1956 war. An earlier index-based estimate of about 1954 is retained as a superseded inference, not the narrative date. His 1961 naturalization card records him as a subject of Israel; the oral history supplies the more extensive postwar journey.

In October 1972 the Cleveland Jewish News identified him as a Jewish Survival Legion leader and national board member and as the former local coordinator of the Jewish Defense League. The family remembered him at home as Zeidy Larry. His later public writings show a continued concern with Jewish security and Holocaust remembrance.

The family’s compiled record gives his death as 3 January 2012. Zion Memorial Park records interment on that date at Z3-1-35, beside Ileen at Z3-1-34. The exact death day is not independently established by the interment entry alone.

Source note: Larry Hersh oral history, 13 August 1984, USHMM RG-50.091.0080; 1961 naturalization card; CJN, October 1972; Judith Dratler obituary, 9 June 2025; Zion Memorial Park register.

## Larry tells his childhood and war

The oral history recorded on 13 August 1984

The following account summarizes Larry’s 1984 oral history, interviewed by Peggy Nathan for the National Council of Jewish Women, Cleveland Section, and preserved by the USHMM. The supplied transcript warns of transcription errors. Scenes, numbers and dialogue below are attributed recollections rather than independent verification of each event.

He was born in Khust, Czechoslovakia - he spells it C-H-U-S-T - a town of thirty-five or forty thousand, eight or ten thousand of them Jews, in June 1925. His was, he says, an ideal childhood: an affluent home - his father dealt in lumber and wine - cheder, then Czech elementary school, then the Hungarian gymnasium, until the Jewish students were put out; summers in the Carpathians; Betar, where he rose to lead the region's youth. He read Gone with the Wind, he liked to say, in four languages. In 1938 he won a fully paid place at a Prague school for talented youngsters - and his paternal grandmother went to the rabbi: he'll eat treyf, there'll be no Shabbos, no tefillin. His father canceled the ticket.

In late 1942 the Zionist organization chose him for one of Khust's four Palestine certificates. His mother objected - it would look like the big macher was taking for himself - and his younger sister refused it too: she wanted the family together. He gave it away. "The paradox of it," he says, "is the ones who left, they all survived."

Then the ghetto. He organized the food wagons on the move in; the Judenrat man called him by his Jewish name, Leibe; the Gestapo chief interrogated him personally. Warned that his arrest was coming, his father bought him a way out - an army call-up which the transcript associates with a commander rendered “Kelety”. In the labor service at Nagybanya he organized a hard core of eight men and smuggled a wagon of bread, hidden under hay, to the starving ghetto; the general caught him and looked the other way - "next time don't be a dumb Jew and let yourself be caught" - and later praised the job well done. His friend Leonard Fox, who lived to see Cleveland, took forty-five lashes on a later run. Larry escaped over the fields, came back to the ghetto at night, and was deported with its liquidation in the morning.

Source note: USHMM, Larry Hersh oral history, RG-50.091.0080, parts 1–2; interview 13 August 1984. See the comparison chapter for later versions.

## Auschwitz and Warsaw

Larry’s testimony continued

Five days in a freight car, eighty to a hundred to a car, water and nothing else, and then Auschwitz, two days before Shavuot - the testimony’s 1943 references conflict with the established 1944 ghetto and deportation chronology. Mengele conducted the selection; Larry said he was seventeen, and was sent right. The transcript renders his prisoner number as 8485 in one passage and 845 in another. Learning that his father and an uncle had been sent to Warsaw, he swapped blocks to follow them - to the camp built on the ruins of the ghetto, where prisoners cleaned bricks from bombed buildings and loaded trains for the Russian front. He found his father after six weeks. He translated for a guard the words written in blood on the Anielewicz bunker wall: we were betrayed, remember us. Assigned to clean the killer-dog cages, he fed his father, his uncle and his friends for weeks on the kitchen food the work earned. He saw the SS burn bound prisoners stacked, he said, like wood - "the horror of being stripped of the ability to even scream at one's own pain."

The sequence is preserved as Larry recalled it. The accompanying original interview and its transcripts allow the family to return to his words. The transcript’s year and prisoner-number conflicts remain unresolved; those details should not be copied into a civil chronology without qualification.

Source note: USHMM, Larry Hersh oral history, RG-50.091.0080, parts 1–2, interview 13 August 1984.

## From the camps to Cleveland

Larry’s testimony continued

In August 1944 the camp dissolved ahead of the Russian advance. Some ten thousand were marched out - two days standing without food or water, then the salt-herring ration, and prisoners who stooped to drink had their necks crushed. Fewer than eight thousand reached Kutno; about two thousand survived the cattle train to Dachau. His father and uncle made it. From Dachau to Landsberg, Lager 7 - underground earth huts, typhus quarantine - and then Kaufering, Lager 4, night construction for the underground airfields. There his father, forty-eight and broken, was taken away with the older generation, and was never seen again.

In the early spring of 1945 Larry lay twenty-eight days with typhus, unfed, untreated - an SS guard held a rifle to his head, and a second guard said leave him, he's finished anyhow; save the bullet. In May the evacuation train was strafed - the US Air Force disabled the locomotive - and he and two others, the last three of his eight-man hard core, escaped through the forest to American-occupied Schwabhausen. He weighed thirty-eight kilos.

The after-war had its own roads. In the hospital, a Jewish US Army doctor traced his family by telephone from Cleveland - the message ran that Leibi Hersch, son of the sister Leah, was alive: so his mother's name is known. In Prague he learned that his next-to-youngest sister had survived, liberated at Bergen-Belsen. In Budapest he was recruited by Joel Brand - Larry had seen Eichmann there in 1943 without knowing who he was - and he ran the Bricha borders from 1945 to 1947, sailed for Palestine, was hunted by the British, was smuggled out to Cyprus hidden among the oranges, and returned to Israel in 1948 on the Altalena. Eight years in the Israeli army carried him through the 1956 war.

In 1956 he came to Cleveland, to that sister - she had been there since 1947, taken in by their uncle Janowitz "like one of their own daughters." He met his wife in Cleveland, and the rest of his story is this book's: Ileen, three daughters, the Marmarosher temple on Lancashire - one record says B'nai Jeshurun, and both are kept - and the grandchildren who called him Zeidy Larry.

Source note: USHMM, Larry Hersh oral history, RG-50.091.0080, parts 2–3. Numerical estimates and sequences are Larry’s recollections.

## Bread for the ghetto

Two versions of an act of resistance

A second published account attributes a closely matching story to “Larry Hirsch.” Adam Reviczky’s book places this speaker in Huszt, born in 1925, speaking several languages, and reaching Nagybanya with a summons procured by his father. These unusual shared details make the identity match strong. They do not turn the two tellings into independent witnesses.

In the 1984 oral account, bread was hidden under hay and the commander gave quiet approval after the fact. The later book describes an audience first: Larry had collected bread from labor servicemen’s rations, and Lieutenant Colonel Reviczky instructed the men to take the bread by horse-drawn transport before it dried out. The book describes an arrest by gendarmes and difficulty securing release.

The later version names Reviczky explicitly, while the oral transcript renders the commander’s name as Kelety. The later account puts the summons and flight to Nagybanya in April 1944. It describes hiding with Christian acquaintances and travel into the Great Plain before deportation. The oral account instead describes a return to the ghetto after a failed bread run and deportation the next morning.

These differences matter because an apparently seamless story would erase what the sources actually say. The two accounts agree on a young multilingual man, a father’s effort to save him, bread deliveries, danger and subsequent deportation. Their precise sequence and the commander’s name remain questions for further record and audio work.

The text examined was the publisher’s OCR of the 2008 Múlt és Jövő edition, printed pages 448–450. The original page images were not available for visual verification. The chapter therefore paraphrases cautiously and supplies no purported exact Hungarian transcription. A bibliographic record for a 1985 edition does not establish that the passage was identical in that edition.

Source note: Adam Reviczky, Vesztes háborúk – megnyert csaták (2008), pp. 448–450, publisher OCR at szaktars.hu; USHMM RG-50.091.0080, parts 1–2; research comparison, 30 September 2026.

## The Altalena in Larry’s memory

The 1984 testimony and two newspaper accounts

Larry’s 1984 oral history says that he returned to Israel on the Altalena. Later Cleveland Jewish News stories add a memorable episode: he swam ashore at Kfar Vitkin with a waterproof pouch for Menachem Begin. Ellen Harris published a version in 2003; Cynthia Dettelbach published another in 2008. Both are accounts attributed to Larry, not independent corroboration by two passengers.

The 2008 account describes recruitment to move arms from Czechoslovakia through the American zone of Germany, a journey to Marseille at the end of April 1948, and a day of loading. Larry remembered the Israeli flag, Hatikvah and tears. He described himself and four other swimmers carrying a pouch ashore and Begin welcoming him. In this telling he never learned what the pouch contained.

The 2003 version calls the contents documents supplied by a man rendered “Stovsky” and links his arrival closely to the Latrun fighting. The 2008 version says that the group was detained at Kfar Vitkin for forty-eight hours before entering the unified fighting force. The book preserves both versions without choosing a hidden pouch content or harmonizing the sequence.

The newspaper chronology contains errors. The 2008 article prints “June 1, 1948” and calls it Friday; 1 June was a Tuesday, while 11 June was a Friday. Palmach history and the later James T. Williams voyage account give 11 June as the departure. The newspaper dates the burning to 21 June; the historical accounts give 22 June. A dropped digit may explain one error, but that explanation remains an inference.

Reported ages also disagree with a 1925 birth: twenty in the 2008 voyage account and eighty in the 2003 article. Passenger totals vary between the accounts and may refer to different groups. These figures do not generate new birth dates or a settled passenger count. The 2003 photograph is captioned Zev Harel and must not be used as a portrait of Larry.

Source note: Cleveland Jewish News, “Area veterans of 1948 war refute New Historians,” 15 May 2003; “Remembering the Altalena,” 22 May 2008; USHMM oral history part 3; James T. Williams and Avi Diskin, The Northern Mariner 34(1), 2024, pp. 129–138.

## A voice for remembrance

Larry Hersh in the Cleveland press

On 26 May 2000, the Cleveland Jewish News printed a letter signed Larry Hersh of Cleveland under the heading “Let’s try again for center.” The writer said that he had proposed a Cleveland Holocaust Center twenty-five years earlier, that the Jewish Community Federation had supported a committee and allocated funds, and that disagreements among survivors had brought the effort to a halt. He urged another attempt.

The letter is evidence of an appeal for a permanent place of Holocaust memory. It is not independent evidence that the writer founded a center, nor verification of his account of committee disputes. The name, place and concerns fit Larry’s public biography, although the letter supplies no family identifiers. Its implied beginning around 1975 is arithmetic from his statement.

A later letter, “Learn from the past,” published on 3 December 2010, was signed Larry Hersh of Cleveland Heights. It urged American Jews to learn from history and expressed views about Israel. These writings belong in the family record as a public voice: opinions stated in print, carefully distinguished from neutral historical findings.

Source note: Cleveland Jewish News, 26 May 2000, p. 8; “Learn from the past,” 3 December 2010; source comparisons, 30 September 2026.

## Ileen called Gabby

Shaina Yuda 1933–2009

Ileen Garson was born in Cleveland on July 30, 1933, the youngest of Victor and Pearl Garson's three daughters, and grew up on the east side in the years when the streetcars still ran. Her Hebrew name was Shaina Yuda; to her grandchildren she was Gabby. She married Larry Hersh - Zeidy Larry - and together they raised their family in Cleveland.

Her obituary in the Plain Dealer of May 23, 2009 keeps the whole constellation in one paragraph: "beloved wife of Lawrence; devoted mother of Suzanne Greenberg, Seth, Laurel Warszawski, Joseph and Marci Hersh; cherished grandmother of Ilana, Aliya, Shoshana, Yechiel, Moriah, Shymon, Chaya, Simcha and Hadassah Warszawski, Koby and Dylan Greenberg; dear sister of the following deceased: Rochelle Horwich and Rosamond Simon."

Laurel Warszawski is Lori - Joe's wife. The nine Warszawski grandchildren named in that notice are Joe and Lori's children, Gabby's grandchildren, each one counted. Her sisters, Rochelle and Rosamond, had gone before her.

Gabby died on May 22, 2009 - 28 Iyar 5769, the date the family keeps as her yahrtzeit - and was interred on May 24 at Zion Memorial Park, plot Z3-1-34. When Larry followed in 2012, the cemetery's register placed him in the next grave over. The two plots, Z3-1-34 and Z3-1-35, are the whole story of a marriage, told in the cemetery's own shorthand.

## The Garsons of Paris

Victor Garson 1895–1978

Victor Garson was born in Paris on May 30, 1895, the son of Solomon and Rachael Garson. In 1902 - so his wife would swear decades later - he entered the United States at New York, a boy of seven; he had sailed from Le Havre, his travelling companions have not been established by a passenger manifest.

He grew up in Cleveland, became a bookkeeper, and on September 12, 1916 married Pearl Adelstein in Cuyahoga County. Three years later, on September 18, 1919, he stood in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio and swore his Declaration of Intention - the first formal step toward citizenship. The clerk wrote him down: born Paris, May 30, 1895; occupation bookkeeper; last foreign residence "Pairs, France," the clerk's own misspelling of Paris; emigrated from Le Havre, the vessel unnamed. He renounced his allegiance to the French Republic. He was twenty-four, three years married, a father of four-month-old Rochelle.

Pearl’s 1940 petition records Victor’s naturalization on November 20, 1924, certificate 2116415. He lived the rest of his life in Cleveland, and the compiled family record gives his death at East Cleveland on 13 August 1978, five years after Pearl. They lie at Zion Memorial Park. His notice, in the Plain Dealer of 14 August 1978, does not print a death day; it styles him simply husband of the late Pearl; the funeral was held that Monday at three o'clock at the Berkowitz-Kumin Memorial Chapel, and the family sat shiva again at the Sanford Simons'. The notice's one misprint is preserved as printed: it calls him dear grandmother of eight and great grandmother of one.

One page of his story was nearly misread. An early transcription of the declaration gave his last foreign residence as Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine - and for a while the search ran to Metz. A magnified reading of the original settled it: the word is "Pairs," Paris, where he was born. The correction is printed here as it belongs in a family record - plainly, and in the same ink as the mistake.

Source note: Family archive, September 2026 edition, original page 23; see the source register. Naturalization: Pearl’s petition (S070), 20 November 1924, certificate 2116415.

## An American twice over

Pearl Adelstein Garson 1897–1973

Pearl Adelstein was born in Cleveland on December 1, 1897 - American from her first breath, in the city central to her later family record. Family memory had placed her birth in France, or in Latvia; her own hand settles it. On January 25, 1940, swearing her Petition for Naturalization, she wrote: "born in Cleveland, Ohio on Dec. 1, 1897. My race is Hebrew."

That an American-born woman should have to petition for citizenship at all is the story. When Pearl married Victor Garson on September 12, 1916, he was a French national - and under the Expatriation Act of 1907, an American woman's citizenship followed her husband's. By saying yes to Victor, Pearl lost the country of her birth. The Cable Act of 1922 opened the way back, and in 1940 she took it, swearing "I have not acquired any other nationality by affirmative act." Her petition was granted; certificate 73676. Cleveland-born, made foreign by marriage, American again by her own signature.

The petition is a family document in the fullest sense. ## Where the proven Garson line stops

A family surname and several distinct branches

Victor’s marriage record names his parents as Solomon and Rachael Garson. His sworn declaration gives Paris as his birthplace. No recovered French birth act or passenger manifest yet connects those three people to the other Garson families described in this part. The diagrams and narratives therefore distinguish the direct family from documentary research households.

An early reading of Victor’s last foreign residence as Metz was corrected to the clerk’s “Pairs,” meaning Paris. The Metz marriage of Aron Garson and Caroline Levy in 1865 remains a lead in its own right, not evidence that Victor’s father Solomon was their son. A shared surname and a plausible chronology are insufficient.

Paris civil acts uncovered Julia’s children, Aline Garson, Louise’s son Gustave and the household of Charles Alphonse Garson. Tigery and neighboring communes yielded another extensive Garson or Garcon network. These records have genuine historical value even while their bridge to Victor remains missing.

This section prints the people and acts as research families, with the missing bridges stated on each relevant page. Superseded spelling guesses and table errors have been replaced by the later documented readings. A bounded unsuccessful search is described as a search result, not proof that an event never happened.

## The Metz question

Aron Garson and Caroline Levy

Where did the Garsons come from before Paris? The family's oldest documentable footprint is Victor's - Paris, 1895. Behind that, the trail thins to a surname and a region.

In Metz, in Alsace-Lorraine, the civil register records a marriage on September 1, 1865: Aron Garson to Caroline Levy. In the Metz-Chambiere cemetery the same couple lies under a second spelling - "Gerson Aron, 1841-1924" and "Levy Caroline Gerson, c.1845-1909." A rare surname, one region, two spellings - and in Cleveland, Victor's marriage record names his father as Solomon.

If a Solomon Garson - or Gerson - was born to Aron and Caroline in Metz in the years after 1865, the line from Metz to Paris to Cleveland would close into a single thread. That record has not yet been found. A "Guerchen, Abraham Gershon" who died at Hellimer in 1855 may belong to an earlier generation still.

A passenger manifest for Victor’s reported 1902 arrival from Le Havre remains an important missing record. Neither the fact that he was seven nor the later petition establishes which relatives travelled with him. The search must test the whole household, including surname variants.

## Julia and her children

A Paris household not yet linked to Victor

The registers of Paris hold three more Garsons, and they hold them close. On November 4, 1895 - five months after Victor's birth across the same city - a young domestic named Julia Maria Garson, eighteen years old, bore a son, Marcel Eugene Jules, at a midwife's home in the 18th arrondissement. No father is named on the act; the boy carried his mother's name. Two weeks later, on November 18, Julia crossed the city to the 17th arrondissement and stood before a second officer to recognize the boy as hers - act N° 3451, an acte de reconnaissance, a young mother making sure the law knew her son was her son. That act gives her name in full: Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson.

In January 1897 the registers record a second son: Leon Jules, born on the seventeenth in the 5th arrondissement, to a mother the clerk wrote down as Melina Perpetue Garson, twenty, a domestic - a closely matching name and address supporting the identification, though the name forms differ. And in March 1898, back in the 18th, a daughter: Charlotte, born to Julia Garson, now twenty-two, at another midwife's rooms. Again, no father is named. Three children in four years - Marcel, Leon, Charlotte - each carried into the register by their mother alone.

The ages and uncommon name elements support treating the acts as the record of one woman, Julia Emelina Perpetue Garson, born about 1877. Her relationship, if any, to Solomon Garson is unproved. Marcel, Leon and Charlotte must not be called Victor’s cousins unless the missing parentage is established.

Leon's act carries the fullest afterlife. In its margin, two later hands recorded a whole French lifetime: married to Marie Marthe Therese Rollet at Epineuil, in the Cher, on August 21, 1920; died at Lagny-sur-Marne on August 31, 1980 - eighty-three years, from a midwife's rooms in Paris to a marriage in the countryside and a long life past the wars. The Rollet line may have descendants living today. Marcel's margin is briefer: a death recorded on February 21, 1963. What became of Charlotte, the registers have not yet said.

## The Paris households

Documented separately and not merged

The supplied Paris survey of birth tables for 1893–1902 identified several distinct Garson households. Its findings are bounded by the tables, dates and spellings examined; absence from that survey is not proof that another household did not exist.

Julia's is the first: the unwed domestic whose three children fill the pages before this one. The second is Louise Garson, a rag-picker three years Julia's senior, also unwed, who registered her son Gustave in the 5th arrondissement in May 1901 - two women of the same rare name, alone in the same city, a few years and a few streets apart; no sister relationship has been established. The third is a wine merchant's family: Charles Alphonse Garson and Juliette Honorine Boclin, whose son Marcel Alphonse was born in the 4th in September 1902. And the fourth a shoemaker's: Marius Gabriel Garson and Marie Guden, whose daughter Aline was born in the 6th in October 1894 - and whose margin, written twenty-six years later, records her marriage to Edouard Adolphe Godenne in 1920.

Four doors, one surname. Which of them opens onto Solomon and Rachael - onto Victor - the acts so far do not say; none of them names him. But the sweep that was meant to find one boy's birth certificate found a small Paris of Garsons instead, and every door is now on the map.

A fifth household is documented by Armandine Garson’s 1893 birth act: Auguste Garson and Francoise Armandine Goujot. The proposed identification of her father with Auguste, born on 15 August 1864 to Antoine Joseph and Caroline Valentine, is an age-matched inference. That bridge remains qualified, even though earlier compiled data placed the acts under one person.

## Aline’s marriage in 1920

The act that names two generations

On 16 October 1920 at 11:27 a.m., Aline Garson and Edouard Adolphe Godenne appeared at the town hall of the seventeenth arrondissement of Paris. She was a commerce employee, twenty-five, born in the sixth arrondissement on 19 October 1894. He was a railway employee, thirty-eight, born in Saint-Ghislain, Belgium, on 7 August 1882.

The act identifies Aline as the adult daughter of the late Marius Gabriel Garson and his widow Marie Guden, a cook, who was present and consenting. “Guden” is the sharper reading of the surname; an earlier “Guder” transcription is superseded but the spelling still deserves corroboration from another act.

Edouard’s parents are Jean Baptiste Godenne and Emma Marie Vandenbossche, also read Van den driessche, merchants in Saint-Ghislain. The mother’s surname remains a difficult reading. These are the groom’s parents, not Aline’s. A later summary that called them Aline’s parents confused the sides of the marriage.

The parties declared that they wished to marry; the act says that no marriage contract had been made. Pierre Guden, a butcher, and Jules Godenne, a turner, witnessed the ceremony. Although the surnames invite hypotheses, the act does not state a kinship relationship for either witness.

The correct marriage is act 3605 in register 17M399, viewer position 21. The former 5559 reference from the birth margin was a mistaken reading. ## The Garsons of Tigery

A documented village network with an unproved bridge

The Tigery acts trace several generations whose surname alternates between Garson and Garcon. Jacques Sebastien Garson, a winegrower, died on 7 November 1820, about seventy-one. His death act names Jacques Desire, forty-two, as his son, and Louis Rognon as his son-in-law. The day in the original act corrects an index date one day earlier. Another act names an older Jacques Desire with Marie Elisabeth Gauthier. The Jacques Sebastien and Jacques Desire name forms are not assumed to identify one man; the older-generation identity remains a question.

Jacques Desire, often distinguished in the research as Jacques Desire II, appears as a day laborer, manual worker, winegrower and fisherman. His first wife is most often Marie Ursule Breton; earlier birth acts render her as Marie Desire or Marie Desiree Breton. The repeated household context is important, but the differing given names remain a source conflict.

The later original-act reading places Jacques Desire’s death on 20 July 1838. Ferdinand and Theodore are named as his sons. The act calls Catherine Fournier his wife in a second marriage; her own 1842 remarriage to Gabriel Marquignon further documents her widowhood. The marriage that joined Jacques Desire and Catherine has not been located at Tigery.

Jacques, called Theodore, was born on 28 March 1809; Eugene on 10 June 1813; and Louis Jacques on 20 August 1816. Josephine’s 1820 birth act identifies the same parental household. Ferdinand and Desire Lamoureux appear as brothers of Theodore in the 1834 marriage evidence. These relationships are documented within the Tigery network. ## The uncle in Cleveland

Janowitz and the missing first name

Larry’s 1984 oral history gives one of the most personal bridges between Europe and Cleveland. After liberation, a Jewish U.S. Army doctor located Larry’s maternal uncle through a telephone directory. The message identified the survivor as Leibi Hersch, son of the uncle’s sister Leah. The uncle was already corresponding with Larry’s surviving sister, later sponsored her immigration and housed Larry for three months after his 1956 arrival.

The examined passage gives the surname Janowitz but no first name. A 1939 notice for Louis E. Janowitz names siblings including Sarah Herskovitz and Rose Herskovitz. That is a useful lead, but Louis died before 1956 and cannot be the uncle who hosted Larry then. His siblings and their households have not been linked to Leah.

The 1940 directory supports the household of Louis’s widow Lena and daughter Sadie; it does not establish the Hersh connection. A Louis Herskovitz married to Rose, with children Carl, Max, David and Esther Hoffman, is another candidate household with no demonstrated maiden-name bridge. Morris and Eleanor Janovitz in the 1955 directory, and the George and Carrie Janowitz family, likewise remain separate.

Directory coverage limits the negative evidence. After 1951, the main Cleveland volume did not include all the Heights communities. Separate 1955 and 1956 Heights volumes were not reached in this research. Failure to find the uncle in a main-volume search is not proof that he was absent from Cleveland.

The decisive next evidence would be a civil or immigration record identifying Leah’s maiden name and brother, or the original sponsorship and arrival file. Until then, the uncle remains a real person in Larry’s testimony whose full identity is unknown. The candidate households are not connected to the core family tree.

Source note: USHMM RG-50.091.0080, part 3; CPL Louis E. Janowitz necrology 163244; Cleveland directories 1940 and 1955; September 2026 candidate audit.
