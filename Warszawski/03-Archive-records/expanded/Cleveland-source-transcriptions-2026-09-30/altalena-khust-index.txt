# Remembering the Altalena: a shipmate’s tale

- BY: CYNTHIA DETTELBACH (cdettelbach@cjn.org) Editor
- May 22, 2008
- Posted May 22, 2008 at 12: 00 AM

![Remembering the Altalena: a shipmate’s tale ](https://bloximages.chicago2.vip.townnews.com/clevelandjewishnews.com/content/tncms/assets/v3/editorial/6/ab/6ab9e6e0-422e-51b7-907e-dcee663bf60d/4e8ba56d78625.image.jpg)

Larry Hersh, inset, today and on the Altalena, top right, June 1948.

Next month marks the 60th anniversary of the so-called Altalena affair.

The low point of that little-known event took place June 21, 1948:

The Altalena, a ship carrying almost 1,000 young Holocaust survivors, all members of the Jewish underground Irgun, plus arms and ammunition for the fledging Jewish state, is fired upon and sunk in Tel Aviv harbor.

The attackers are neither the British nor the Arabs, but the newly constituted Israel Defense Forces (IDF). It shoots on orders of Israeli Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion. When the smoke clears, 16 Irgun fighters and three IDF soldiers lie dead.

To this day, the Altalena attack remains a black mark against the blue and white flag of Israel.

But for many of the men and women who sailed on that ship, the real story resides not in its terrible demise but in the tale of how it acquired its cargo, how it got loaded aboard, and the journey made from wartorn Europe to Palestine.

One former Altalena shipmate is South Euclid resident Larry Hersh. A survivor of Auschwitz, Dachau and Kaufering (in Germany), Hersh had been a member of the Zionist group Betar in prewar Hungary. After the war, an Israeli emissary, who had come to Europe to help survivors and to prepare for a hoped-for Jewish state, learned of Hersh’s Betar affiliation and put him in touch with a man named Kidon.

Kidon recruited young Betarniks like Hersh for an Irgun operation that involved smuggling arms from Czechoslovakia to the American Zone in Germany for the purpose of getting them to Palestine. With the Czechs and Americans “looking the other way,” explains Hersh, these undercover operations lasted until 1948.

Using money acquired from the sale of American cigarettes and coffee on the black market, plus the generous contributions of American Jews, the Irgun also struck a deal with the French to buy guns, half-tracks, artillery shells, and rounds of ammunition.

In spring 1948, when rumors were rife that a Jewish state would soon be declared, the Irgun purchased an old Navy landing vessel. The goal: to ferry needed arms to the new Jewish state already under threat of Arab attack. The boat was named the Altalena, pen name of Betar’s founder, Ze’ev Jabotinsky.

“Our (Irgun) group was closest to France geographically, so the burden of getting the arms to Marseille and loading them on the ship was ours,” reports Hersh. He and his group arrived in Marseille at the end of April, but it was not until June that all the transports with war materiel arrived from Czechoslovakia, Italy, Austria and Germany as well as from the French.

When everything was finally in place, Hersh’s group worked “for 24 hours straight” to load the ship. (They didn’t want Arab dockworkers to learn about or to engage in this stealth enterprise.) Also streaming onboard were almost 1,000 Irgun members, all between the ages of 18 and 25.

For Hersh, memory of the day the Altalena raised anchor (June 1, 1948) is as vivid as if it happened yesterday: “You should have seen the faces that Friday at noon, with tears running down our cheeks as we raised the (Israeli) flag and sang ‘Hatikvah,’” he reports. “I was 20 years old and realized the privilege, after 2,000 years,” of going to the Jewish homeland.

But the Altalena was not destined for easy sailing. Ten days earlier, notes Yehuda Lapidot, a former Irgun member, IDF officer, and professor at Hebrew University, an agreement had been signed for absorption of the Irgun and Haganah into a single military force, the IDF. One of the clauses stated that the Irgun had to “cease all independent arms acquisition activities.” Irgun leaders in Israel feared that openly bringing in military equipment and Irgun fighters would breach the agreement.

Irgun head Menachem Begin wired the Altalena to stay in Marseille, but the ship’s captain apparently didn’t receive the message in time. “We learned of this information at sea,” Hersh reports.

So instead of landing, as planned, in Tel Aviv, word came for them to disembark at the more secluded Kfar Vitkin beach.

Hersh and four other good swimmers were entrusted with swimming ashore with a pouch and personally handing it to Begin.

“Begin embraced me and then said, ‘Welcome home,’” Hersh recalls, misty-eyed. “I took off the pouch and gave it to him, and to this day I have no idea what was in it.”

According to Lapidot, Ben-Gurion demanded that Begin hand over all the weapons on the Altalena, which Begin was loath to do. He wanted to keep 80% to equip the new Irgun battalions. The debate ended in a resolution: “Use force if necessary to confiscate the ship and its cargo.” When Begin refused to respond to the Ben-Gurion ultimatum, “in particular the demand for an answer within ten minutes,” fighting broke out, and there were several casualties.

The Altalena, with Begin now aboard, then steamed toward Tel Aviv where, “at 4 p.m. Ben-Gurion ordered the shelling” of the ill-fated vessel.

Hersh and the other swimmers, who had been detained at Kfar Vitkin for 48 hours, were reunited with their shipmates, and all were resettled in a military camp. There, Hersh recalls, “Begin banged his fist on the table saying, ‘There’s not going to be a brotherly war’ (meaning the Irgun would not remain a separate fighting force). Two days later, we all registered to be part of the Haganah.”

For years afterward, the Altalena stood in Tel Aviv harbor as a painful reminder of the day Jews fired on other Jews. But for Hersh and others who sailed on the Altalena that long-ago June, the ship and its mission also conjures vivid memories of being young, free and fearless, and determined to do their part in making the new Jewish homeland secure.

Note:

1. In the section entitled “And again: Duchnovich Street” Mr. Berner the tailor, on page 160, is Jacob Herman Jenő Berner. On page 172 Zvi Menshel writes about Avraham Jenő Berner the baker. However, the World War I military record he attributes to Avraham Jenő Berner in fact is that of Jacob Jenő Berner. The listing in Hegedűs Márton szerk., A magyar hadviselt zsidók aranyalbuma az 1914-1918 - as világháború emlékére (Márton Hegedűs ed., The golden album of the Hungarian Jews who fought in the war - in memory of the World War 1914-1918), (Budapest: Hungária Ny, 1940), 14, listing number 106, is Jacob Jenő Berner the tailor, not Avraham Jenő Berner the baker. Jacob Jenő Berner enlisted in the 9th Honvéd (Royal Hungarian) Infantry Regiment in 1915. Later in the war he served in the 12th HIR, and the 16th HIR. He was awarded the Bronze Medal for Valour twice, the Karoly (Karl) Troop Cross, and was wounded in battle. This revision is based on documents in the Military Archives in Vienna, Yad Vashem, Arolsen Archives and the government archives of Zakarpattia oblast, Ukraine researched by Jack S. Eisner, Jacob Jenő Berner’s grandson. Return
