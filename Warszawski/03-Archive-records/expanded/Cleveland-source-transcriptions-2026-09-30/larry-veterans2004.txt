# Area veterans of 1948 war refute 'New Historians'

- By ELLEN HARRIS (eharris@cjn.org) City editor
- May 15, 2003
- Posted May 15, 2003 at 12: 00 AM Updated Oct 4, 2011 at 6: 34 PM

![Area veterans of 1948 war refute 'New Historians' ](https://bloximages.chicago2.vip.townnews.com/clevelandjewishnews.com/content/tncms/assets/v3/editorial/4/ad/4ad51fff-15a2-5cd7-b33f-823a765324e6/4e8b89e991861.image.jpg)

Zev Harel, above left, commanding machine gun unit in 1948, and, at right, today.

Clevelanders who fought in the 1948 War of Independence are outraged by some contemporary historians who claim that contrary to popular belief, Israeli soldiers were well armed and relatively large in numbers, compared to the enemy.

Cleveland Heights residents Eli and Yaffa Levy say that they and their colleagues had to resort to deception in order to convince the Arabs they had weapons. When Eli served in the Israeli artillery, his unit of 34 fighting men had only one machine gun that worked. "We ran from one trench to another, firing from a different spot each time," so the enemy would believe they had an arsenal of weapons, he says.

Yaffa worked as a nurse during the War of Independence. In the months leading up to the battle, the Arabs began shelling Jerusalem, forcing Levy and many of her neighbors into a nearby shelter. "We didn't have any ammunition, very little food, no extra clothes. We beat tin cans with sticks to fool the enemy into thinking we had guns. It was a very, very rough time," says Yaffa, who met her husband while serving in the army.

Yaffa, 73, will never forget tending to a patient who became critically ill with tetanus and had to be transferred to Hadassah Hospital in Jerusalem. Yaffa went with him, pitching in to help the frantically busy medical staff.

"Sometimes we stayed all day and all night," says Yaffa. "Everyone was busy helping everyone else. There was a lot of hand-to-hand fighting in the army and horrible injuries-open stomach wounds, legs cut off. It was terrible."

Her husband will always remember the terror he felt while fighting the Arabs in a trench battle near what is now Yad Vashem.

"We could hear British officers giving orders in English to the Jordanians," says Eli. "That's when we realized we weren't just fighting the Arabs, we were fighting the British forces as well."

He knew enough English to understand the commands. "We realized what they were going to do next, but we couldn't do much to prevent it," says Eli, 73, a retired, longtime b'nai mitzvah tutor at Park Synagogue.

He takes exception to contemporary historians who suggest that captured Arab soldiers were mistreated by the Israelis.When Levy's unit took one as prisoner, they treated him humanely, even sharing the little food the Israeli troops had. "Believe me, that's not what they did when they captured us," he adds.

---

At age 17 and a newcomer to Israel, Zev Harel found himself in the thick of the battle in 1948, three years after being liberated from Auschwitz. A member of the Palmach, the elite fighting arm of the Haganah, his unit suffered heavy losses in the battle to defend Jerusalem.

Harel insists it is "nonsense" to believe the Israeli army could come anywhere near close to matching Arab troops in strength or numbers.

While his unit consisted of between 20 and 30 soldiers traveling in convoys to bring food to Jerusalem, there were Arab units on both sides of the road "numbering in the hundreds," says Harel, a professor of social work at Cleveland State University.

"We had very simple rifles, machine guns and local submachine guns against tanks and other formal military might," says Harel, 73. By contrast, the Jordanians were equipped with armored tanks and heavy machinery.

"The greatest strength we had in those days was the same determination and spirit that inspired the Warsaw ghetto uprising," says the University Heights resident. "It gave backbone to the Palestinian brigade and the leaders of the early Jewish state."

Looking back on the battle 55 years later, Harel says what he remembers most were the mutual respect and informality that distinguished the early Israeli army. Harel's brigadier commander was Yitzhak Rabin, later to become prime minister of Israel. Rabin's staff and Harel's machine gun unit were stationed in the same building.

"Evenings, we would sit around and just talk about the war discussing general concerns and military operations," says Harel. Rabin and his officers didn't wear insignia or other indications of their high rank, he points out. "Everyone had the right to express their opinions. The feeling was, everyone is in this together. You were part of the overall effort."

One night, when Harel was wounded in action, Rabin picked up Harel in his army vehicle and took him to a first-aid station. "For a commanding officer to do this, it's an indication of respect for his soldiers."

The Israeli soldiers were equally committed to their cause, says Harel. After he was injured in his leg and head, he was sent to rehabilitation center. "When word got around that our units were moving to take back Latrun, the hospital emptied out," he says. "We all left to return to our units. You just volunteered."

---

"There is no question we were outnumbered by the Arabs. To suggest anything else is baloney," declares Larry Hersh, another Holocaust survivor who fought in the War of Independence.

In 1948, the entire population of Israel numbered 600,000, he points out. "There were millions of Arabs from Syria, Egypt, Jordan, Iraq, Lebanon. They all came."

To build up its fighting force, Israel turned to newcomers, Hersh says. He was one of 550 survivors who came over on the Altalena (ship) and almost immediately joined the battle of Latrun. Hundreds of (us) died to stop the Jordanians," says Hersh. "You have no idea of the pain, the blood, the suffering, that the entire generation went through to put Israel on the map."

The Cleveland Heights resident still remembers swimming ashore just before the war and being greeted by Menachem Begin, who years later would be elected prime minister. After they embraced, Hersh handed Begin a waterproof pouch he had worn around his neck throughout the journey. They contained documents for the fledgling Jewish state that had been given to him by Stovsky, a leader of an Israeli underground organization.

Hersh, 80, dismisses the work of historians such as Mordecai Bar-On, who are re-examining moral and military issues surrounding the War of Independence. "It's easy for them to sit in judgmen, but they weren't there. We paid the price in blood."

(With reports from Jennifer Slate)

Ingenuity was key to survival

It was engineering know and creativity, not stockpiles of acquired weapons, that helped Israel win the War of Independence, says Harold Isaacs.

The University Height resident is in a position to know. A long-time Zionist, he joined the Haganah several years before the war broke out. Drawing on his toolmaking background, Isaacs opened a machine company in New York. It became "the Haganah center" for adapting general-purpose machinery to the specific task of manufacturing arms.

Because shipping weapons to Palestine was forbidden by the British, Isaac's team got permission to ostensibly set up a plant there to manufacture small printing presses. In fact, the facility was used to make parts for guns. Similarly, his group was given the green light to establish a (fictitious) canning factory in Holon, whose real purpose was manufacturing gun components.

While he was in Israel, Isaacs worked with kibbutzim in the Galilee to establish shops to make arms and ammunition. Evenually, half of all weapons the Israelis used in the War of Independence were made in Palestine, he says.
